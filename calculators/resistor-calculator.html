Quick answer. Brown, black, red, gold reads 10 × 100 = 1 kΩ with ±5%, so 950 to 1,050 Ω. Resistors of 100, 220 and 330 Ω give 56.897 Ω in parallel and 650 Ω in series.

What the calculator does

The page has three tools. The colour code tool turns a resistor’s coloured bands into its resistance, tolerance and allowed range, for 4-band and 5-band resistors. The parallel tool and the series tool take a comma-separated list of resistances in ohms and give the total. All three apply standard formulas, so the answers are exact for the values you type.

Reading the colour bands

Hold the resistor with the tolerance band, usually gold or silver, on the right and read from the left. On a 4-band resistor the first two bands are digits, the third is a multiplier and the fourth is the tolerance. A 5-band resistor adds a third digit before the multiplier, which gives more precision. The value is the digits read as a number, times the multiplier.

Gold and silver are used as multipliers only for fractions of an ohm (×0.1 and ×0.01) and as tolerance bands (±5% and ±10%). Digit bands never use gold or silver.

Worked example with the default bands

The colour tool opens on brown, black, red and gold. Brown is 1 and black is 0, so the digits make 10. Red is the multiplier ×100, so the value is 10 × 100 = 1 kΩ. Gold means ±5%, so the real resistance can be anywhere from 950 Ω to 1.05 kΩ: 5% of 1,000 is 50 ohms either side. Switching to 5 bands with brown, black, black, red and brown reads 100 × 100 = 10 kΩ at ±1%.

For the calculator’s 5-band mode the third band is read as a digit, so brown, black, black is 100, not 10. Make sure the band count matches the resistor before reading the result.

Resistors in series

In series the same current flows through each resistor, and the resistances simply add: R total = R1 + R2 + …. The series tool’s default values of 100, 220 and 330 Ω give 650 Ω. The total is always larger than the largest single resistor.

Resistors in parallel

In parallel the current splits between paths, so the reciprocals add: 1 / R total = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + …. For 100, 220 and 330 Ω that is 0.01 + 0.004545 + 0.003030 = 0.017576, and the total is 1 ÷ 0.017576 = 56.897 Ω. The total is always less than the smallest resistor, here 100 Ω.

For just two resistors there is a shortcut: R1 × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2). For 100 and 220 Ω that is 22,000 ÷ 320 = 68.75 Ω. When all the resistors are equal, divide one value by the count.

Standard values

Resistors are made in preferred values rather than every number. The common E12 series has twelve steps per decade (10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82), and the E24 and E96 series add more steps for tighter tolerances. That is why you rarely find a 100 Ω and 220 Ω pair that combine to exactly the value you want. Combining two or three resistors in series or parallel is the usual way to get close to a needed value.

What the colours do not show

  • Power rating. The size of the resistor sets how much heat it can handle, commonly ¼ W for small ones. A 1 kΩ resistor across 5 V dissipates 5² ÷ 1,000 = 25 mW, comfortably within ¼ W (250 mW).
  • Temperature coefficient. Some 6-band resistors add a band for it. This calculator covers 4 and 5 bands only.
  • Current condition. Bands fade and resistors drift with heat, so confirm with a multimeter when in doubt.

Common mistakes

  • Reading the bands from the wrong end. The tolerance band is separated from the others and is read last.
  • Choosing 4 bands for a 5-band resistor, which shifts every digit.
  • Adding resistors in parallel instead of using reciprocals.
  • Entering kilo-ohms and ohms in the same list. The tools expect ohms only, so 4.7 kΩ is 4700.
  • Assuming the printed value is exact. Within the tolerance band, any value is possible.

How to read your result

The colour tool shows the nominal resistance with its tolerance, then the allowed range underneath. The parallel and series tools show only the total, formatted in Ω, kΩ or MΩ. Values of 1,000 or more appear in kilo-ohms and values of a million or more in mega-ohms. Both tools need at least two positive values.

Check a resistor with a multimeter before using it in a circuit that matters. Faded bands can be misread, and the colours say nothing about power rating.

Common questions

How do I read resistor colour bands?

Hold the resistor with the gold or silver tolerance band on the right and read from the left. The first digits give a number, the next band is the multiplier and the last is the tolerance. Brown, black, red, gold is 10 times 100, or 1,000 ohms at plus or minus 5 percent.

Which end do I read the bands from?

Start from the side where the bands are closer together. The tolerance band is usually separated from the rest and sits at the far end. It is always read last.

What is the difference between 4-band and 5-band resistors?

A 5-band resistor has a third significant digit before the multiplier, so it can show values with more precision. A 4-band resistor has two digits.

How do I find the total resistance in parallel?

Add the reciprocals of each resistance, then take the reciprocal of the sum. For 100, 220 and 330 ohms, that gives 56.897 ohms. The result is always lower than the smallest resistor.

How do I find the total resistance in series?

Add the resistances together. For 100, 220 and 330 ohms the total is 650 ohms.

What if a resistor has no tolerance band?

A resistor with no separate tolerance band is usually taken as plus or minus 20 percent. The calculator's band options do not cover that case.
