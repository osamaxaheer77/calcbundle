What body surface area is

Body surface area, or BSA, is an estimate of the total skin area of the body, in square metres. It is worked out from height and weight, because measuring skin area directly is not practical. Because it takes both height and weight into account, it tracks metabolic mass more closely than weight alone, and doctors use it for some drug doses, for the cardiac index and for burns.

What the calculator does

Enter your height in feet and inches or in centimetres, and your weight in pounds, kilograms or grams. The calculator gives the BSA from five formulas, each in square metres and square feet, the average of the five, and the BMI for reference. The gram option is there for infants.

Researchers published several equations over the decades, each fitted to a different group of people. Mosteller is the simplest to calculate by hand, Du Bois is the oldest and most cited, and Haycock and Gehan and George were fitted with children in mind.

Why surface area grows more slowly than weight

Skin area rises with the square root of weight, roughly, because a larger body has more volume for its surface. The figure shows Mosteller for a 175 cm person: going from 60 kg to 120 kg doubles the weight but raises the area by only about 41%.

Where BSA is used

  • Drug dosing. Some medicines, especially chemotherapy, are dosed in milligrams per square metre. As arithmetic only, a drug dosed at 75 mg/m² for a BSA of 1.85 m² comes to 138.8 mg. Real doses are set by the prescriber using the local protocol, which names the formula and any cap to use.
  • Cardiac index. Cardiac output divided by BSA. A cardiac output of 5.5 litres a minute with a BSA of 1.85 m² gives a cardiac index of 2.97 L/min/m², in the range of about 2.5 to 4.0 that is usually described as normal.
  • Burns. The extent of a burn is given as a percentage of body surface area.
  • Kidney function. Glomerular filtration rate is often reported per 1.73 m² of BSA, so results are comparable between people of different sizes.

Average or one formula?

The tool shows the average of the five formulas as the headline figure, which is a sensible general estimate. For clinical use, follow the formula named in the local protocol and use that line, not the average. Mosteller is the most common in modern practice because it is simple, and Du Bois is the most cited historically.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up pounds and kilograms. Check the weight unit.
  • Entering height in centimetres while the ft/in option is selected.
  • Using the average where a protocol calls for a specific formula.
  • Using an adult formula result for an infant dose. Use the gram option and the formula your clinic uses.
  • Treating the estimate as a measurement of skin area.

Common questions

Which BSA formula should I use?

Mosteller is the most commonly cited in modern clinical practice for its simplicity, though Du Bois remains widely referenced historically. For any medical purpose, use the formula your provider or protocol names.

Why does the calculator show five formulas?

Researchers have published several equations fitted to different study populations. They agree closely for average adults but differ for children and for very large or small bodies.

What is the Mosteller formula?

BSA in square metres is the square root of height in centimetres times weight in kilograms divided by 3600. For 170 cm and 70 kg, that is 1.82 square metres.

What is body surface area used for?

It is used to dose some drugs, especially chemotherapy, to work out the cardiac index, to describe the extent of burns and to express kidney function.

Can I use this for a baby?

The calculator accepts weight in grams for infants. The formulas are still estimates, and medical dosing must follow the protocol used by the clinic.

Is the average the right number to use?

It is a reasonable general estimate. Clinical use follows the single formula named in the local protocol, not the average.
