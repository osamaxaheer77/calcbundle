Engine Horsepower Calculator

Engine power varies across the rpm range; peak torque and peak power often occur at different engine speeds. Use torque measured at the same rpm.

Elapsed Time (ET) Method

Horsepower = weight × (5.825 ÷ ET)³, with the weight in pounds and the time in seconds.

Trap-Speed Method

Horsepower = weight × (speed ÷ 234)³, with the weight in pounds and the speed in miles per hour.

Quick answer. You can estimate engine horsepower from a quarter-mile run: weight × (5.825 ÷ ET)3, or weight × (trap speed ÷ 234)3. A 3,200 lb car running 12.5 s makes about 324 hp, and one trapping 110 mph about 332 hp.

Two ways to estimate power from a drag-strip run

A car that is more powerful for its weight finishes a quarter mile (402.3 metres) in less time and at a higher speed. The calculator has two tools:

  • Elapsed time (ET) method: horsepower = weight × (5.825 ÷ ET)3.
  • Trap-speed method: horsepower = weight × (speed ÷ 234)3.

In both, weight is in pounds, time in seconds and speed in miles per hour; the calculator converts other units for you.

Worked example with the default numbers

A 5,000 lb vehicle with a 20 second ET: 5.825 ÷ 20 = 0.29125, cubed is 0.02471, and times 5,000 gives 123.5 horsepower (92.1 kW). The same vehicle with a trap speed of 70 mph: 70 ÷ 234 = 0.2991, cubed is 0.02677, and times 5,000 gives 133.8 horsepower (99.8 kW). The two methods disagree by about 8%, which is normal.

A more typical car, 3,200 lb, running 12.5 seconds, makes about 324 hp by the ET method. At a trap speed of 110 mph the same car comes out at about 332 hp.

What weight to use

Use the weight of the vehicle as it ran, which includes the driver, passengers, fuel and anything else of significant weight. The trap speed is the speed at the end of the quarter mile, not the average speed over the run.

Horsepower from torque and rpm

If you know the engine’s torque, horsepower = torque (lb-ft) × rpm ÷ 5,252. An engine making 200 lb-ft at 3,000 rpm produces 114.2 hp. Torque and power peak at different engine speeds, so use the torque measured at the same rpm.

Power-to-weight ratio

Power alone does not decide speed; what matters is power for each pound the engine must move. The 3,200 lb car above at 324 hp has 101.2 hp per 1,000 lb, which is about 9.9 lb per hp. A heavier vehicle needs proportionally more power to match it, which is why the weight is part of both formulas.

Why the formulas use a cube

The force needed to push a car through the air and accelerate it grows quickly with speed, and the power needed grows with the cube of the speed. That is why a small gain in trap speed implies a large gain in power: going from 100 mph to 110 mph is a 10% rise in speed but a 33% rise in the power the formula estimates.

Units

James Watt defined horsepower in the 18th century to compare steam engines with the horses they replaced. One mechanical horsepower is 550 foot-pounds of work a second, 33,000 a minute, or about 745.7 watts. The calculator shows kilowatts as well: 100 hp is 74.6 kW.

How accurate is it?

These are estimates. They assume a good launch and a vehicle in good condition, and the result depends on gearing, traction, aerodynamics, altitude and weather. Power measured at the wheels is lower than at the engine because of drivetrain losses, and these formulas aim at engine power. Compare the two methods; the trap speed is usually steadier because it is less affected by the launch.

Safety

Only measure quarter-mile times at a drag strip or another legal venue. Never do it on public roads.

Common mistakes

  • Using the empty weight of the car and forgetting the driver and fuel.
  • Entering the average speed instead of the trap speed.
  • Mixing units, such as kilograms with a pounds formula. Set the unit selectors.
  • Treating the result as a dyno measurement.
  • Ignoring conditions: high altitude, hot air, a poor launch or a headwind all slow the run and lower the estimate.
  • Comparing numbers from different tracks without allowing for the surface and the weather.

How to read your result

The large number is the estimated engine power in horsepower, with watts below it, then kilowatts, the weight used and the elapsed time or speed after unit conversion. For a conversion between power units or a general horsepower calculation, see the Horsepower Calculator.

This is an estimate of peak engine power from a vehicle's performance, and is only as good as the numbers you put in.

Common questions

How do I estimate horsepower from quarter mile time?

Use horsepower = weight in pounds x (5.825 / ET in seconds)^3. A 3,200 lb car with a 12.5 second ET makes about 324 horsepower.

How do I estimate horsepower from trap speed?

Use horsepower = weight in pounds x (speed in mph / 234)^3. A 3,200 lb car with a trap speed of 110 mph makes about 332 horsepower.

How many watts is a horsepower?

One mechanical horsepower is about 745.7 watts.

Is this wheel horsepower or engine horsepower?

The formulas estimate engine horsepower. Wheel horsepower is lower because power is lost in the transmission and drivetrain.

Why do the two methods give different answers?

They use different parts of the run, and each rests on averages from many cars. The trap speed is usually the better indicator, since it is less affected by the launch.

How do I calculate horsepower from torque and rpm?

Horsepower equals torque in lb-ft times rpm divided by 5,252. For 200 lb-ft at 3,000 rpm that is about 114 horsepower.
