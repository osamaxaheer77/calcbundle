IRA Calculator

Compare retirement account choices using the same saving horizon but different contribution and taxation assumptions. Focus on after-tax value, rather than comparing only the displayed account balance.

Balance Accumulation

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Traditional wins if your retirement tax rate is lower than today's, and Roth wins if it is higher. On $30,000 plus $7,500 a year for 35 years at 6%, with 25% tax now and 15% later, the Traditional IRA ends at $906,392 after tax and the Roth at $799,758.

Traditional or Roth: the tax question

A Traditional IRA (and its employer cousins, the SEP and SIMPLE IRA) takes before-tax money. You save tax when you contribute, the money grows untaxed, and you pay ordinary income tax on what you withdraw. A Roth IRA is the mirror image: you pay tax first, then the money grows and comes out tax-free. Both let the money grow untaxed, so the real contest is between the tax rate you pay now and the one you expect to pay in retirement.

How the comparison is set up

The calculator imagines you start from the same before-tax amount each year. Put it in a Traditional IRA and all of it goes to work. Put it in a Roth IRA or a taxable account and your current tax comes off first, so a smaller amount is invested. At retirement the Traditional balance is reduced by your expected retirement tax rate, so all three accounts are compared on an after-tax basis. Taxable savings are also taxed on their growth every year at your current rate, which slows their compounding. Each year’s contribution is added at the end of the year.

Worked example with the default numbers

The page opens with a balance of $30,000, $7,500 a year of before-tax contributions, a 6% return, age 30 to 65, a current tax rate of 25% and a retirement tax rate of 15%. The Traditional IRA grows to $1,066,343 before tax, which is $906,392 after 15% tax. The Roth IRA starts with $30,000 × 0.75 = $22,500 and adds $5,625 a year, reaching $799,758. Taxable savings grow only at 6% × 0.75 = 4.5% a year and reach $563,434. The winner is the Traditional IRA, ahead of the Roth by $106,634. You contributed $292,500 before tax in total.

The deciding rule: current rate against retirement rate

If the tax rate you expect in retirement is lower than your current rate, the Traditional IRA usually comes out ahead. If it is higher, the Roth wins. If the two rates are the same, they finish equal. With 25% now and 25% later the Traditional IRA is worth $799,758 after tax and the Roth is worth $799,758, a tie. Raise the retirement rate to 35% and the Traditional falls to $693,123, below the Roth’s $799,758.

Why taxable savings fall behind

A regular account is funded with after-tax money, and the growth is taxed every year. At a 25% tax rate the 6% return becomes 4.5% a year. Over 35 years that is the difference between $799,758 and $563,434. Retirement accounts avoid that annual tax drag, which is why both IRAs beat taxable savings even before counting any deduction.

What the calculator does not include

  • Contribution limits. The IRS sets a yearly limit on IRA contributions. For 2026 the IRS announced $7,500, with a higher catch-up limit for people 50 and over. The default of $7,500 matches it, and the tool does not stop you entering more.
  • Income limits and deductions. Whether a Traditional contribution is deductible, and whether you can contribute to a Roth directly, depends on your income and on workplace plan coverage.
  • Early withdrawals. Taking money out before age 59½ can bring a penalty and tax. The tool assumes none.
  • Required minimum distributions. Traditional IRAs must pay out minimum amounts once you reach a certain age, while the original owner of a Roth IRA has none. The RMD Calculator covers that.
  • Changing rates. The calculator uses one steady return and fixed tax rates. Real markets and tax rules change.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the same tax rate for now and retirement and expecting a winner.
  • Using your average tax rate. Use your marginal rate, the rate on the next dollar.
  • Putting the take-home amount in the contribution box. The box is for the before-tax amount.
  • Forgetting that this tool ignores contribution limits and income limits.
  • Treating the result as a prediction. It is a comparison under steady assumptions.

How to read your result

The large line names the account with the best after-tax result. Below it are the Traditional IRA after tax, the Roth IRA and taxable savings, then a short table with the balance at retirement before and after tax. The chart and the age-by-age table show how each account grows.

This is an estimate for planning, not tax or investment advice. Rules for IRAs change, so check the current IRS rules or ask a tax professional before you decide.

Common questions

Should I choose a Traditional or a Roth IRA?

It depends mainly on tax rates. If you expect a lower tax rate in retirement than you pay now, the Traditional IRA usually comes out ahead. If you expect a higher rate, the Roth does. If they are equal, the two finish the same.

How does the calculator compare the accounts?

It assumes you start from the same before-tax amount each year. The Traditional IRA invests all of it, the Roth and taxable account invest what is left after your current tax, and the Traditional balance is reduced by your retirement tax rate at the end so the three are compared after tax.

Why do taxable savings do worse?

The growth in a taxable account is taxed every year, which slows compounding. In an IRA the money grows without a yearly tax.

Does the calculator apply contribution limits?

No. It does not apply contribution limits, income limits or deduction rules, so check the current IRS rules for your own situation.

What tax rate should I enter?

Use your marginal rate, the rate on your next dollar of income, for the current rate, and your best estimate of your marginal rate in retirement for the later rate.

Does it include early withdrawals or required distributions?

No. It assumes you leave the money alone until retirement and ignores penalties and required minimum distributions.
