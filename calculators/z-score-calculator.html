What a z-score actually tells you

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value sits above or below the average. A z-score of 0 is exactly average, +1 is one standard deviation above, and −2 is two below. That makes very different things comparable. A 78 on an easy exam and a 64 on a hard one can both be turned into z-scores, and the higher z-score is the better result relative to the group. The calculation is simply (x − μ) ÷ σ.

A worked example with an exam result

Say a test has a mean of 70 and a standard deviation of 10, and you scored 85. Your z-score is (85 − 70) ÷ 10 = 1.5. Enter those three numbers above and the probability of scoring below 85 comes out as 0.93319, so you did better than about 93% of the group, and about 6.7% scored higher. Because the figure assumes a roughly normal bell-shaped spread of scores, it is a good guide for exams, heights and measurement errors, and a poor one for heavily skewed data like incomes.

Using it as a z-table

Before calculators, people looked these probabilities up in a printed z-table. The converter does the same job in both directions. Enter a z-score to see the area to its left, to its right and in between, or enter a probability to get the z-score that goes with it. A few values are worth memorising: the 90th percentile is at z = 1.282, the 95th at 1.645, the 97.5th at 1.96 and the 99th at 2.326. They show up again and again in testing and in building ranges of likely values.

The 68-95-99.7 rule, checked

For a normal distribution, about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three. You can check that with the last box: between z = −1 and 1 the area is 0.68269, between −2 and 2 it is 0.9545, and between −3 and 3 it is 0.9973. A value more than three standard deviations out is rare, about 3 in 1,000, which is why it often gets flagged as an outlier.

Common mistakes to avoid

Using the sample standard deviation when the population value is known, or the other way round, shifts the z-score slightly, so be clear which one you have. Another mistake is applying a z-score to a sample average without dividing the standard deviation by the square root of the sample size. Finally, remember that the probabilities assume a normal distribution, so check with a quick histogram or common sense before trusting them for strongly lopsided data.

Rule of thumb: a z-score beyond about ±2 is unusual, and beyond ±3 is rare. If you see z-scores like 8 or −10 in everyday data, the data is probably not normal or there is an entry error.

Common questions

How do you calculate a z-score?

Subtract the mean from your value and divide by the standard deviation: z = (x − μ) ÷ σ.

What is a good z-score?

It depends on what you are measuring. A positive z-score means above average and a negative one below. For exam results, a z-score above 1 puts you roughly in the top 16%.

How do I find the percentile from a z-score?

Use the converter and read P(x < Z). That probability, written as a percentage, is the percentile. A z-score of 1.5 gives 0.93319, which is about the 93rd percentile.

Can a z-score be negative?

Yes. A negative z-score means the value is below the mean. The probability to its left is then less than 0.5.

What is the difference between a z-score and a t-score?

A z-score uses the known population standard deviation. A t-score is used when you only have a small sample and must estimate the standard deviation from it, which gives slightly wider tails.
