Roth IRA Calculator

Estimate how annual contributions and assumed growth could change a Roth IRA balance by retirement. Compare the projection with taxable savings, while remembering that contribution limits and qualified-distribution rules can change.

Quick answer. A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax money and grows tax-free. Starting with $30,000 at 30, adding $7,500 a year to 65 at 6% and a 25% tax rate, a Roth IRA reaches $1,066,343, which is $315,098 more than a taxable account.

How a Roth IRA works

A Roth IRA is a retirement account funded with money that has already been taxed. You get no tax deduction when you contribute, but the money grows without tax, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. It tends to be most attractive when you expect to pay the same or a higher tax rate later, or when you want tax-free income alongside other savings.

Contribution limits

For 2026 the calculator uses a limit of $7,500 a year, or $8,600 if you are 50 or older, as long as you have at least that much earned income. Roth contributions phase out and then stop for higher earners (above roughly $168,000 of income for single filers and $252,000 for married couples filing jointly in the calculator’s assumptions). The same combined limit applies to all of your Roth and traditional IRAs. If you choose “Maximize contributions”, the calculator uses the limit for your age each year, rising at 50. If you enter more than the limit yourself, it is cut to the limit.

What the calculator compares

It runs the same savings through two accounts. In the Roth IRA the balance simply grows at your expected return. In a taxable account, each year’s growth is taxed at your marginal rate, so only the after-tax return compounds. The gap between the two at retirement is the value of tax-free growth. Each year’s contribution is added at the end of the year.

Worked example with the default numbers

Start with $30,000 at age 30, contribute $7,500 a year until 65, expect 6% a year and a 25% marginal tax rate. Over 35 years you put in $292,500 in total. The Roth IRA grows to $1,066,343, of which $773,843 is growth. The taxable account ends at $751,245, because $152,915 of tax on growth was paid along the way. The Roth IRA is ahead by $315,098.

Why starting early matters

Roth vs traditional IRA in one idea

A traditional IRA gives a tax deduction now and taxes withdrawals later. A Roth taxes you now and not later. If your tax rate is the same at both times, the two end up equal: for example, $10,000 before tax in a traditional account grows by the same factor as $7,500 of after-tax money in a Roth at a 25% tax rate (about 5.7 times over 30 years at 6%), and the traditional account is then taxed at 25% on withdrawal. The Roth wins if your rate is higher in retirement and loses if it is lower. The IRA Calculator compares them directly.

Withdrawal rules to know

  • Contributions can generally be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, because they were already taxed.
  • Earnings are tax-free only for qualified withdrawals, which generally require that the account has been open for five years and that you are 59½ or older (or meet another qualifying reason).
  • Roth IRAs have no required minimum distributions for the original owner.
  • Rules change, so check the IRS page for current limits and requirements.

What the calculator does not include

  • Inflation, so the balance is in future dollars.
  • Changing returns. It uses a steady return every year.
  • Changes to contribution limits after 2026 and income eligibility details.
  • State taxes and the tax treatment of capital gains or dividends in the taxable account.

Common mistakes

  • Contributing above the limit, which can bring penalties.
  • Forgetting that eligibility phases out at higher incomes.
  • Treating the projection as guaranteed. Actual returns vary.
  • Not comparing with a traditional IRA or employer plan match first.

How to read your result

The headline difference shows what tax-free growth could be worth under your assumptions. Test a lower return and a different tax rate to see how it changes. If you have an employer match in a 401(k), usually capture that first, then consider a Roth IRA for tax-free growth.

Common questions

How much can I contribute to a Roth IRA?

The calculator uses 2026 limits of $7,500 a year, or $8,600 if you are 50 or older, subject to earned income. The same combined limit applies to all your IRAs, and contributions phase out at higher incomes.

Is a Roth IRA better than a taxable account?

For retirement saving, usually yes if you are eligible, because growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free while a taxable account is taxed on growth along the way. The calculator shows the size of the gap for your numbers.

Roth or traditional IRA?

A Roth is better if you expect a higher tax rate in retirement, a traditional IRA if you expect a lower one. If rates are equal, the outcomes are about the same. The IRA Calculator compares them.

Can I withdraw contributions early?

Contributions can generally be taken out at any time without tax or penalty because they were already taxed. Earnings are tax-free only for qualified withdrawals.

What return should I assume?

Use a cautious long-term figure and test a lower one. Returns vary from year to year, and the calculator uses the same return every year.

Does the calculator include inflation?

No. Balances are in future dollars. Use the Inflation Calculator to see what the result could buy in today's terms.
