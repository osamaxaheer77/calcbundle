Depreciation Calculator

Build a depreciation schedule for an asset using the straight line, declining balance or sum of the year’s digits method, with partial-year options.

Depreciation Schedule

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. An $11,000 asset with $1,000 salvage over 5 years loses $2,000 a year on the straight line method. Double declining balance takes $4,400 in year 1 and the sum of the year's digits takes $3,333.

What depreciation does

Depreciation spreads the cost of an asset, such as a vehicle, a machine or a computer, over the years it is expected to be useful. Each year a part of the cost is recorded as an expense and the book value falls. The depreciable amount is the cost minus the salvage value, what the asset is expected to be worth at the end of its life. The book value never goes below the salvage value.

Worked example with the default numbers

The calculator opens on an asset costing $11,000 with a salvage value of $1,000 over 5 years, using the straight line method with rounding to whole dollars. The depreciable amount is $10,000, so each year takes $10,000 ÷ 5 = $2,000, and the book value falls from $11,000 to $1,000.

The three methods

  • Straight line: the same amount every year, (cost − salvage) ÷ years.
  • Declining balance: a fixed percentage of the remaining book value each year. The percentage is the factor divided by the years, so a factor of 2 over 5 years is 40%, the double declining balance method.
  • Sum of the year’s digits: a falling fraction of the depreciable amount. For 5 years the parts are 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 out of 15.

Declining balance with a factor of 2

Switch the method to declining balance and keep the factor of 2. The rate is 2 ÷ 5 = 40%, taken from the book value at the start of each year: 40% of $11,000 is $4,400, then 40% of $6,600 is $2,640, then $1,584 and $950. The last year takes whatever brings the book value down to the salvage value, here $426. The rate applies to the whole book value, salvage included, so the last year is capped to stop exactly at the $1,000 salvage value.

Sum of the year’s digits

For a 5-year asset the digits add up to 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15. Year 1 takes 5/15 of the $10,000 depreciable amount, which is $3,333, year 2 takes 4/15 ($2,667), year 3 takes 3/15 ($2,000), year 4 takes 2/15 ($1,333) and year 5 takes the rest, $667. The last figure is 1/15 of $10,000, or $666.67, rounded up to $667 so that the total is exactly $10,000.

Partial years

Assets are rarely put into service on the first day of the accounting year. Turn on partial-year depreciation, enter the service start date and the date your accounting year begins, and the first period is shortened. As an example, suppose the asset starts on March 17, 2026 and the accounting year starts on January 1. There are 9 months and 15 days (290 days) until January 1, 2027. Rounding

The default setting rounds each year to whole dollars. The final year is then set to bring the book value exactly to the salvage value, which can make it slightly different from the pattern, like $667 in the sum of the digits example. Choose No to see cents.

Book depreciation versus tax depreciation

This tool shows the common accounting methods for planning and for management reports. Tax depreciation follows its own rules. In the United States, for example, business property is generally depreciated under MACRS, with set recovery periods and conventions that are laid out in IRS Publication 946. For a tax return, check the current rules or ask an accountant.

Common mistakes

  • Applying the declining balance rate to the depreciable amount. The tool applies it to the remaining book value, salvage included, and caps the last year at the salvage value.
  • Entering a salvage value equal to or above the cost. The tool needs salvage to be lower than cost.
  • Using a factor above the number of years, which the tool does not accept.
  • Expecting book depreciation to match the figure on a tax return.
  • Forgetting that a partial first year adds an extra period at the end.

How to read your result

The large number is the total depreciation over the schedule, which equals the cost minus the salvage value. Under it are the cost, the salvage value and a sentence about the method. The table gives, for each year or accounting period, the beginning book value, the percentage, the depreciation, the accumulated total and the ending book value.

This calculator is for planning and illustration. Tax depreciation and accounting policy have their own rules, so check them with an accountant before you file.

Common questions

What is salvage value?

Salvage value is the amount you expect the asset to be worth when you stop using it. Depreciation only spreads the cost minus the salvage value, so the book value ends at the salvage value.

What is the double declining balance method?

It is declining balance with a factor of 2. The yearly rate is 2 divided by the years, applied to the book value at the start of each year. For 5 years the rate is 40%.

How is straight line depreciation calculated?

Subtract the salvage value from the cost and divide by the number of years. For $11,000, $1,000 salvage and 5 years, the depreciation is $2,000 a year.

How does the sum of the year's digits method work?

Add the digits 1 to n for an n-year asset. Year 1 takes n of that total, year 2 takes n minus 1, and so on. For 5 years the total is 15, so year 1 takes 5/15 of the depreciable amount.

Why does a partial year add an extra period?

If the asset starts partway through the accounting year, the first period is shortened and the remaining depreciation moves into one more period at the end.

Is this the same as tax depreciation?

Not necessarily. Tax rules, such as MACRS in the United States, use their own tables and conventions. Check with an accountant for tax filings.
