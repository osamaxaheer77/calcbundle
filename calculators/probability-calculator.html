What probability measures

Probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) that says how likely an event is. You can write it as a decimal (0.25), a fraction (1/4) or a percentage (25%). The calculator has two tools: one combines the probabilities of two independent events, and the other finds the probability that a value from a normal distribution falls between two bounds.

Two independent events

Two events are independent when one happening does not change the chance of the other, like two separate coin flips. Given P(A) and P(B), the calculator finds:

  • Both happen (intersection): P(A) × P(B)
  • A or B or both (union): P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The overlap is subtracted so it is not counted twice.
  • Exactly one happens (A or B but not both): the union minus the intersection.
  • Neither happens: 1 − the union. Also the complements P(A′) = 1 − P(A) and P(B′) = 1 − P(B).

Everyday probability examples

  • A fair die: the chance of a six is 1/6 ≈ 16.67%. Two sixes in a row (independent) is 1/36 ≈ 2.78%.
  • At least one head in 3 coin flips: it is easier to find the opposite. No heads is 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125, so at least one head is 1 − 0.125 = 0.875.
  • Dependent events: drawing two aces from a deck without replacement is not 4/52 × 4/52. The second draw depends on the first: 4/52 × 3/51 ≈ 0.0045. The independent-event formula does not apply here.

The normal distribution

Many measurements, such as heights, test scores and measurement errors, cluster around an average in a bell curve. The calculator turns your bounds into z-scores (how many standard deviations a value is from the mean): z = (x − mean) ÷ standard deviation, then finds the area under the curve between them. You can enter a bound such as -inf or inf for a one-sided probability.

Worked example: normal distribution

Heights with a mean of 68 inches and a standard deviation of 4. What is the chance of a height between 60 and 72? The z-scores are (60 − 68) ÷ 4 = -2 and (72 − 68) ÷ 4 = 1. The area between them is 0.81859, or 81.9%.

One-sided versions of the same question: the chance of a height above 76 is 2.3% (z = 2), and the chance of one below 60 is 2.3% (z = −2).

The 68-95-99.7 rule

Choosing the right model

  • Equally likely outcomes (dice, cards, coins): probability = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. Count carefully, and use the Permutation and Combination Calculator when the counting is hard.
  • Independent events: multiply for “and”, and use the union formula for “or”. Check that one event really does not change the other.
  • Measurements with a bell curve (heights, errors, scores): use the normal distribution with the right mean and standard deviation.
  • Repeated trials: for “at least once” questions, work out the chance of it never happening and subtract from 1.

If you are not sure which applies, write the question as a sentence and ask: are the outcomes counted, combined, or measured on a scale?

Common mistakes

  • Using the independent-event formulas when events affect each other (drawing without replacement, for example).
  • Adding probabilities for “A or B” without subtracting the overlap.
  • Entering percentages (65) where decimals (0.65) are required.
  • Using a normal distribution for data that is clearly skewed or bounded.
  • Confusing the standard deviation with the variance.

How to read your result

A probability of 0.77 means that in the long run about 77 out of 100 repeats would have the outcome. It does not guarantee any single result. For a normal distribution, check that the mean and standard deviation really describe your data, then use the z-scores to judge how unusual a value is: beyond 2 in either direction is rare, beyond 3 is very rare.

This calculator gives mathematical results from the numbers you enter. Real-world data may not follow the assumptions of independence or a normal distribution.

Common questions

What if my events are not independent?

Then you need conditional probability: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B given A). This calculator assumes independence, so it will not give the right answer when one event changes the chance of the other.

How is the normal distribution probability worked out?

The calculator converts each bound to a z-score and finds the area under the standard normal curve between them. That area equals the probability of a value falling in that range.

What does P(A Δ B) mean?

It is the probability that A or B happens but not both, which is the union minus the intersection.

Can I enter infinity as a bound?

Yes. Use -inf or inf for a one-sided probability, for example the chance of a value above a certain number.

Do probabilities have to add to 1?

All possible outcomes together add to 1. Two events A and B do not need to add to 1 unless they cover every outcome and cannot overlap.

Why is the answer different from my intuition?

Chance is often counter-intuitive, for example with small overlaps or repeated trials. Writing down the formula and checking whether events are really independent usually finds the reason.
