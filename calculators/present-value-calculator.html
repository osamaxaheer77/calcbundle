Quick answer. Present value is what a future amount is worth today. $1,000 received in 10 years at a 6% discount rate is worth $558.39 now, and $100 a year for 10 years is worth $736.01. The higher the rate, the lower the value.

Why a dollar today is worth more than a dollar later

Present value is the flip side of compound interest. Instead of asking what money grows into, it asks what a future amount is worth right now, given that money in hand could be earning interest. The higher the interest rate, or the further away the payment, the less it is worth today. The rate you use is called the discount rate.

Tool 1: a single future amount

The formula is PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n. With the defaults, $1,000 received in 10 years at 6% has a present value of $558.39, so the interest is $441.61. A simpler check: $1,100 expected in one year at a 10% discount rate is worth $1,100 ÷ 1.10 = $1,000.00 today.

Tool 2: a series of equal payments

The second tool values a stream of equal payments, such as an annuity, a pension or a savings habit. For $100 a year for 10 years at 6% with payments at the end of each year, the present value is $736.01. The same stream would grow to $1,318.08 (the future value), and you would pay in $1,000, so interest is $318.08. If payments come at the beginning of each period, the present value is higher: $780.17, because each payment is one period closer.

Using present value to decide

An offer now or later. Suppose someone offers $40,000 today or $50,000 in 5 years, and you could earn 5% elsewhere. The $50,000 is worth $39,176 today, so taking $40,000 now is the better deal in money terms. At a 0% rate the later offer would win.

Lump sum or payments. Compare a $120,000 lump sum with $10,000 a year for 20 years. At a 5% discount rate the payments are worth $124,622 today, so they are worth slightly more than the lump sum; at 7% they are worth $105,940, so the lump sum is better. The right rate depends on what you could actually earn, so test more than one.

Choosing a discount rate

Use the return you could reasonably earn elsewhere on the money, with similar risk. A safe rate, such as a savings rate or a government bond yield, suits safe payments; a higher rate suits riskier ones. Because the answer depends heavily on this rate, show it alongside the result and try a range.

Present value and loans

The same idea values a loan. The present value of all your future loan payments, discounted at the loan’s own interest rate, equals the amount you borrowed. That is how the payment on a loan is worked out, and why a loan at a lower rate has a lower payment for the same amount. Use the Loan Calculator to see it in action.

What it leaves out

  • Inflation, unless your discount rate already includes it.
  • Taxes and fees on the money you receive.
  • The risk that a future payment is not made.
  • Changes in the discount rate over time.

Common mistakes

  • Using the same rate for risky and safe payments.
  • Mixing periods: an annual rate with monthly payments gives a wrong answer.
  • Forgetting whether payments come at the beginning or the end of each period.
  • Treating present value as a prediction. It is a comparison tool.

How to read your result

The large number is the present value, the amount that would grow to your future amount if invested at the discount rate. Below it are the future value, the total of your payments (for the series tool) and the interest. To project what a balance grows to, use the Future Value Calculator; for saving regularly toward a goal, use the Savings Calculator.

Results depend on the discount rate you choose and are not a forecast. They are not financial advice.

Common questions

What interest rate should I use?

Use the rate that reflects the return you could reasonably earn elsewhere on your money, called the discount rate. A higher assumed rate always produces a lower present value.

Does timing (beginning vs. end of period) really matter?

Yes. Payments made at the beginning of each period are one period closer to today, so their present value is slightly higher than the same payments at the end of each period.

What is the difference between present value and future value?

They are two views of the same relationship. Present value discounts a future amount back to today; future value grows today's amount forward.

Why is the present value lower than the future amount?

Because money today could be earning interest. To reach a given amount later, you need less now.

Can I use it to compare a lump sum with payments?

Yes. Find the present value of the payments with the second tool and compare it with the lump sum, trying more than one discount rate.

Does it account for inflation?

Only if your discount rate includes it. Use the Inflation Calculator to see the effect on purchasing power separately.
