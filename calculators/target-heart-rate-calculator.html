Target Heart Rate Calculator

Work out the heart rate zones to aim for when you exercise, from your age or tested maximum and your resting heart rate.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Your maximum heart rate is often estimated as 220 minus your age. A 30-year-old with a resting rate of 70 has a maximum of 190 and a heart rate reserve of 120, so the Karvonen target for 70% intensity is 154 bpm, and general aerobic exercise falls between 130 and 172 bpm.

Heart rate terms

  • Maximum heart rate (MHR): the highest rate your heart reaches during all-out exercise.
  • Resting heart rate (RHR): your pulse when calm, best measured on waking.
  • Heart rate reserve (HRR): maximum minus resting. It is the “range” you can use for exercise.

The best way to find your true maximum is a supervised stress test. Most people estimate it from age instead.

Three formulas for maximum heart rate

  • Haskell and Fox: 220 − age. Simple and widely used, but it was never meant to be exact.
  • Tanaka: 208 − 0.7 × age. Fitted to a larger group of adults, and often better at older ages.
  • Nes: 211 − 0.64 × age. Another large-sample formula that gives results close to Tanaka.

Two ways to set zones

Percent of maximum multiplies the maximum by a percentage. The Karvonen method is more personal because it uses your resting rate: target = (maximum − resting) × intensity + resting. Two people with the same age but different fitness get different targets.

Worked example with the default numbers

A 30-year-old using Haskell and Fox has a maximum of 220 − 30 = 190 bpm. With a resting rate of 70, the reserve is 190 − 70 = 120 bpm. For 70% intensity, the Karvonen target is 120 × 0.70 + 70 = 154 bpm. The calculator’s zones are:

Example: age 50 with a lower resting heart rate

At 50, Haskell and Fox gives 170 bpm. A resting rate of 60 gives a reserve of 110, so the Karvonen target for 60% to 70% is 126 to 137 bpm. A fitter person with a lower resting rate gets a wider reserve and slightly higher targets than someone with a resting rate of 80, who would see 134 to 143 bpm for the same zone.

Perceived exertion scales

You can also judge intensity by feel. The Borg scale runs from 6 (no effort) to 20 (maximal) and the CR10 scale from 0 to 10. When you enter a resting heart rate, the calculator matches each rating to a heart rate between your resting and maximum. Simple checks that work without a monitor:

  • Talk test: at moderate intensity you can talk but not sing. At vigorous intensity you can only say a few words.
  • On a 0 to 10 effort scale, moderate activity is about a 5 or 6 and vigorous activity a 7 or 8.

How much exercise to aim for

Adult guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix. Use the zones to tell whether a workout is moderate or vigorous, and mix easy and hard sessions rather than training at the top of your range every day.

Safety

  • Heart rate formulas give averages. Some medicines, including beta blockers, change heart rate, and so do heat, caffeine, stress and illness.
  • If you have a heart condition, symptoms such as chest pain or dizziness, or you are new to exercise, talk to a doctor before training at high intensity.
  • Stop and get help if you feel faint, have chest pain or severe breathlessness.

Common mistakes

  • Treating 220 − age as exact. It can be off by 10 to 20 beats.
  • Measuring the resting rate after coffee, a walk or a stressful morning. Measure it lying down on waking.
  • Using zones from a wrist device without checking that it tracks your pulse accurately.
  • Training in the hardest zone every day.

How to read your result

Pick the zone that matches your goal and the effort you can sustain, and check it against how the exercise feels. If the numbers and your effort disagree, trust a tested maximum or the talk test over a formula.

This calculator gives estimates and is not medical advice. Ask a doctor before starting or changing an exercise programme, especially with a heart condition or medication.

Common questions

How do I calculate my target heart rate?

Estimate your maximum heart rate, such as 220 minus your age. Then take percentages of the maximum, or use the Karvonen method, which uses your heart rate reserve (maximum minus resting) and adds your resting rate back.

Which maximum heart rate formula is the best?

None is exact. Haskell and Fox is the simplest. Tanaka and Nes were fitted to larger groups and can suit some ages better, especially older adults. A supervised test is the only way to know your true maximum.

Why does the Karvonen method give different numbers?

It includes your resting heart rate, so fitter people with a lower resting rate get a different zone from people with a higher one, even at the same age and maximum.

How do I measure my resting heart rate?

Count your pulse for a full minute lying down shortly after waking, before getting up or having caffeine. Take it on a few days and use the average.

What intensity is best for fat loss?

Light to moderate zones are often used for steady cardio, but total energy burned and consistency matter more than the exact zone. Harder sessions burn more per minute.

Do medicines change my target?

Some do. Beta blockers and certain other drugs lower heart rate, so zones from a formula may not apply. Ask a doctor for advice.
