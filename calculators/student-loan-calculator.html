Quick answer. A $30,000 student loan at 6.8% repaid over 10 years costs $345.24 a month and $11,429 in interest. Adding $150 a month to a $350 payment pays the loan off in 6 years and 2 months instead of 9 years and 10 months and saves $4,421.

How student loan repayment works

A student loan is repaid with fixed monthly payments. Interest builds on the balance each month, and each payment covers that month’s interest first and the rest reduces the principal. Early in the loan most of each payment is interest, and the principal share grows over time. This page has three tools: a simple payment solver, a repayment tool for extra payments, and a projection tool for what you will owe after you leave school.

Tool 1: solve for payment, balance, rate or term

Fill in any three of the four boxes (balance, term, rate and payment) and leave the one you want to find empty. With the default $30,000, 10 years and 6.8% the payment is $345.24 a month. You pay $41,428.92 in total, of which $11,428.92 is interest.

Tool 2: paying the loan off faster

Enter your balance, payment and rate, then add extra money: a regular amount each month, a lump sum each year, or one payment now. With a $30,000 balance at 6.8% and a $350 payment, normal repayment takes 9 years and 10 months and costs $11,189 in interest. Adding $150 a month pays it off in 6 years and 2 months and saves $4,421 in interest.

Tool 3: what you will owe after graduation

With an unsubsidized or private loan, interest builds up while you study and during a grace period after you leave. If you do not pay it, it is added to the balance (capitalized), so you start repayment owing more than you borrowed. The default inputs are $20,000 already owed, $10,000 borrowed each year for 2 years, a 6-month grace period, 6.8% interest and a 10-year repayment term. You borrow $40,000 in total. By graduation the balance is $44,264 and after the grace period it is $45,790. The repayment is $526.96 a month for 120 months, and the total interest is $23,235.

Should you pay the interest while in school?

If you pay the interest as it builds, the balance stays at what you borrowed. In the same example, paying interest during school means repayment starts at $40,000 and the monthly payment is $460.32 instead of $526.96. You spend more during school, but you owe less afterwards.

Federal and private loans

  • Federal Direct Subsidized Loans do not build interest while you are enrolled at least half time. Unsubsidized loans do.
  • Federal loans usually have a six-month grace period after you leave school. Many private loans have different terms.
  • Federal loans offer repayment plans, deferment and other options that this calculator does not model.
  • The calculator assumes a fixed rate and equal monthly payments.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that interest builds during school and the grace period.
  • Entering a payment that is less than the monthly interest, which never pays the loan off.
  • Choosing the longest term only because the payment is lowest.
  • Sending extra money without telling the servicer to apply it to principal.

How to read your result

Compare total interest, not just the monthly payment. Try a shorter term, a small monthly extra and paying interest in school, and see which combination you can afford. Then check the details of your own loans, such as their rates and any benefits, with your servicer.

This calculator gives estimates for planning. It is not financial advice and does not include income-driven plans, deferment, forgiveness or fees.

Common questions

What is a grace period?

A grace period is a set time after you leave school before repayment begins, often six months on federal loans. Interest may still build up during that time, which raises your balance, so the calculator adds it before working out the payment.

Should I pay the interest while I am in school?

Paying the interest while you study stops it being added to the balance, so you start repayment owing only what you borrowed and pay less in total. If you cannot afford it, the interest is added to the loan instead.

How do extra payments save interest?

Interest is charged on the balance still owed. An extra payment lowers the balance right away, so less interest is charged every month after that. The calculator shows the interest saved against the normal schedule.

What if my payment is too low?

If your payment is no larger than the interest that builds up each month, the balance never falls and the loan cannot be paid off. Raise the payment above the monthly interest to get a payoff time.

Does this work for federal and private loans?

It uses a fixed interest rate and equal payments, so it fits most federal and private student loans on standard repayment. It does not model income-driven plans, deferment or forgiveness.

Why is the balance after school more than I borrowed?

Unpaid interest on unsubsidized and most private loans is added to the balance. The longer you study, and the higher the rate, the more is added.
