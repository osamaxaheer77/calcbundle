Full Pregnancy Weight Gain Schedule

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Recommended weight gain in pregnancy depends on your pre-pregnancy BMI: 25 to 35 lb for a normal weight and one baby. For a woman of 5 ft 6 in who weighed 120 lb, the range at week 20 is 127.2 to 132.0 lb, and her 130 lb is within it.

How the recommended range is set

The Institute of Medicine’s 2009 guidelines set a total recommended weight gain based on your pre-pregnancy BMI (your weight before pregnancy divided by your height squared). For one baby the ranges are roughly 28 to 40 lb if underweight, 25 to 35 lb at a normal weight, 15 to 25 lb if overweight and 11 to 20 lb if obese. Within that total, standard guidance calls for a small gain in the first trimester, then a fairly steady weekly rate. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists follows the same ranges.

Worked example with the default numbers

A woman who is 5 ft 6 in (66 inches) and weighed 120 lb before pregnancy has a BMI of 703 × 120 ÷ 66² = 19.4, which is normal weight, so the total recommended gain is 25 to 35 lb. At week 20 the calculator’s range is a gain of 7.2 to 12.0 lb, which means a weight of 127.2 to 132.0 lb. Her current weight of 130 lb is within the range. At delivery (week 40) the recommended weight is 145.0 to 155.0 lb. The metric defaults, 165 cm and 54 kg before pregnancy, give a BMI of 19.8, also normal weight.

About the week-by-week schedule

The table spreads the total range across all 40 weeks in two phases: a gain of 1 to 4 lb by the end of the first trimester (week 13), then a steady weekly rate to delivery. It is a smoothed approximation of the published guidance, meant to show the shape of a healthy trajectory, not a clinically exact target for each week. Actual recommendations vary by source and by person.

Twins

Twin pregnancies carry more baby, more placental tissue and more fluid, so the provisional ranges are higher: 37 to 54 lb for normal weight, 31 to 50 lb for overweight and 25 to 42 lb for obese. The Institute of Medicine gave no provisional range for underweight women with twins, and the calculator says so and suggests talking to a care provider.

If your weight is outside the range

The result tells you whether your current weight is below, within or above the range for your week. Being outside the range is common and not automatically a problem. Many things affect weight in pregnancy, including fluid, the size of the baby and any medical conditions. Mention it to your care provider, who can assess your situation, rather than trying to change your weight on your own.

What counts as the weight you gain

  • The weight gain covers the baby, placenta, amniotic fluid, larger breasts and uterus, extra blood and fluid, and fat stores for breastfeeding.
  • Pre-pregnancy weight should be the weight just before you conceived, not the weight at the first visit.
  • Weigh yourself in similar clothing, at the same time of day, on the same scale.

What the calculator does not do

  • It does not know your medical history, weight changes before pregnancy or the size of your baby.
  • It uses the Institute of Medicine’s general ranges, which may differ from the advice your clinic gives.
  • It does not advise on diet or exercise in pregnancy. Ask your care provider.

Common mistakes

  • Using weight at the first appointment as the “before pregnancy” weight.
  • Comparing yourself with a week that does not match your stage.
  • Treating the range as a target to hit exactly.
  • Using the single-baby range for a twin pregnancy, or the other way round.

How to read your result

The large range is the recommended weight for your week, followed by whether your current weight is within it, the range at delivery, the total recommended gain and your pre-pregnancy BMI. Common questions

What if my current weight is outside the recommended range?

It is common and not automatically a cause for concern. Many factors affect weight gain in pregnancy. Bring it up with your care provider, who can assess your situation.

Why is the twin-pregnancy range higher?

Carrying twins means more total fetal weight, more placental tissue and more amniotic fluid, so the Institute of Medicine's ranges for twins run notably higher than for a single baby.

How is the pre-pregnancy BMI calculated?

Weight in pounds times 703, divided by height in inches squared, or weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared.

How much should I gain in the first trimester?

Typically a small amount, about 1 to 4 pounds in total. Most of the gain comes in the second and third trimesters.

Are these ranges the same everywhere?

The Institute of Medicine ranges are widely used in the US, but other countries and clinics may use different guidance. Follow your own provider's advice.

Why is there no twin range for underweight women?

The Institute of Medicine did not publish a provisional twin range for the underweight category, so the calculator suggests discussing a target with your care provider.
