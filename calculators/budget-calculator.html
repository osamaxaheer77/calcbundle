Quick answer. A budget compares after-tax income with spending. The default example earns $83,000, pays 28% tax, spends $59,840 and has -$80 left over a year. Its debt-to-income ratio is 27.7%, in the good range.

How the budget is worked out

Enter each income and expense either per month or per year, and the calculator converts everything to a yearly and a monthly figure. Income tax, at a single flat rate you choose, is taken from total income to find after-tax income. Your expenses are subtracted from that to show what is left over, or the shortfall if you spend more than you earn. Expenses are grouped into eight categories so you can see where the money goes.

Worked example with the default numbers

The defaults are an $80,000 salary, $1,000 of investment income and $2,000 of other income, so gross income is $83,000. At a 28% tax rate, after-tax income is $59,760. Expenses come to $59,840, which includes $1,400 a month of rent, $250 a month of utilities, a $250 car payment and a $250 student loan payment, plus $10,000 a year into a 401(k) (counted at its after-tax cost of $7,200). The result is -$80 a year, or -$7 a month: the default budget is balanced to within a few dollars.

Before-tax savings

Contributions to a 401(k), an IRA or a college savings plan usually come out of your pay before tax. Because that money is not taxed, the calculator reduces these contributions by your tax rate so they are compared with after-tax income on the same basis as everything else: $10,000 into a 401(k) costs $10,000 × (1 − 0.28) = $7,200. Stocks, bonds and other savings are counted at their full amount. Financial planners often suggest saving at least 15% of income.

Debt-to-income ratio

The debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is the share of your gross income that goes to housing and debt payments each year. Housing is mortgage or rent, property tax, insurance and HOA fees; debts are the car, credit card, student and other loan payments. In the default budget, housing costs are $17,000 and debt payments are $6,000 ($3,000 car loan and $3,000 student loan), so DTI is $23,000 ÷ $83,000 = 27.7%. A ratio under 35% is generally considered good, 35% to 49% leaves room for improvement, and 50% or more is very high. The front-end ratio looks at housing alone and is 20.5% here.

What small changes do

Try changing one number at a time. Cutting meals out by $100 a month adds $1,200 to the yearly result. A 25% tax rate instead of 28% adds $2,190. Raising the 401(k) to 15% of income ($12,450) costs $1,764 more after tax. And $300 more rent a month turns the result into -$3,680 a year.

Building a realistic budget

  • Start from after-tax income and list fixed bills first, then estimate variable spending.
  • Track a full month of real spending before filling in the boxes, because estimates are usually too low.
  • Include yearly and occasional costs, such as car insurance, gifts and holidays, by entering them per year.
  • Give savings a line in the budget so it is paid first, not what is left over.
  • If income varies, base the budget on the lowest amount you can reasonably expect.

What this calculator does not do

  • It uses one flat tax rate and does not know your real tax situation.
  • It does not track actual spending over time.
  • Utilities, maintenance and living costs are not part of the DTI ratio here.
  • It is a plan from the numbers you enter, not a tax or lending decision.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving out irregular costs such as insurance, repairs and holidays.
  • Entering a monthly amount with the yearly setting, or the reverse.
  • Counting before-tax savings at their full amount.
  • Treating a balanced budget as enough without any emergency savings.

How to read your result

The large number is what is left each month after tax and expenses, in red if it is negative. Below it are the annual and monthly totals, the DTI ratios, a category table with each share of after-tax income, and a donut chart. If the result is negative, look first at the largest categories, then at the ones you control most.

Common questions

How is my after-tax income calculated?

The calculator adds up all your income for the year, then removes the income tax rate you enter. Everything is treated as taxable at that one flat rate.

Why are my 401(k) and IRA amounts smaller in the breakdown?

These contributions are made before tax, so they cost you less than their face value. The calculator multiplies them by one minus your tax rate to show what they cost you after tax.

What is a good debt-to-income ratio?

Below 35% is generally seen as good. From 35% to 49% there is room for improvement, and 50% or higher is very high and may put you at financial risk.

What counts toward the debt-to-income ratio here?

Mortgage, rent, property tax, home insurance, HOA fees, and auto, credit card, student and other loan payments. Utilities, home maintenance and living costs are not included.

What is the front-end DTI ratio?

It is your yearly housing costs divided by your gross yearly income. Lenders use it alongside the full DTI ratio.

How much should I save?

Financial planners often suggest putting aside at least 15% of your income. The right amount depends on your goals, debts and emergency fund.
