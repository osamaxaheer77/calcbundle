Rent Calculator

Work out what rent fits comfortably within your monthly budget, based on your income and existing debt.

Where the 28% and 36% figures come from

Landlords and lenders both lean on the same rule of thumb: no more than 28% of gross income spent on housing alone, and no more than 36% once every other recurring debt is added in. It's the same 28/36 guideline banks use to size a mortgage, borrowed here for rent — the logic is identical whether the monthly housing payment goes to a landlord or a lender. Staying near the lower end leaves more breathing room for savings, emergencies, and the debts that inevitably show up over a lease term.

Why debt shrinks your rent budget dollar-for-dollar

Both figures this calculator shows are really one number — a cap on total monthly obligations — with your existing debt subtracted out first. A $500 car payment doesn't just cost you $500; it costs you $500 of rent-affordability too, since lenders and landlords are ultimately judging your total monthly commitments against your income, not rent in isolation.

The one-third rule some landlords apply is stricter than the 36% ceiling — it's worth checking a specific listing's income requirement before assuming the higher number applies.

Common questions

Is the "affordable" figure a guarantee I'll be approved?

No — it's a guideline based on common income-to-rent ratios. Individual landlords set their own requirements, which can be stricter (or occasionally looser) than the numbers shown here.

Should I use my gross or net income?

Gross (pre-tax) income — that's what both landlords and the 28/36 rule are measured against, even though it's not the amount that actually lands in your bank account.

Why is the recommended number lower than the max?

The maximum reflects the outer limit landlords and lenders typically tolerate; the recommended figure leaves more of your income free for savings, irregular expenses, and future debt without stretching your budget to its ceiling.
