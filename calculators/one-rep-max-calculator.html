Quick answer. Your one rep max is the most you can lift once. It can be estimated from a lighter set: lifting 100 kg for 5 reps gives about 116.7 kg with the Epley formula (100 × (1 + 5 ÷ 30)), and the calculator turns that into working weights by percentage.

What a one rep max is

Your one rep max (1RM) is the heaviest weight you can lift for one clean repetition of an exercise. It is specific to each lift, so your bench press, squat and deadlift all have different 1RMs. Knowing it lets you pick training weights as a percentage of your best and track strength over time. Testing a true 1RM is hard on the body and should only be done with a spotter, so many lifters estimate it from a lighter set of several repetitions.

The three formulas

  • Epley: 1RM = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)
  • Brzycki: 1RM = weight × 36 ÷ (37 − reps)
  • Lombardi: 1RM = weight × reps0.10

All three agree closely at low reps and drift apart as reps increase. Epley and Brzycki give exactly the same answer at 10 reps (a multiplier of 1.333). Estimates work best when the set was between 2 and 10 reps and you went close to failure; fewer reps usually means a more reliable estimate.

Worked example with the default numbers

You lift 100 kg for 5 repetitions. With the Epley formula: 100 × (1 + 5 ÷ 30) = 100 × 1.1667 = 116.7 kg. The calculator also shows 90%, 80% and 70% of that max: 105.0, 93.3 and 81.7 kg.

Using your 1RM in training

  • Around 1 to 5 reps at roughly 85% to 100% of your max is commonly used for building maximal strength.
  • Around 6 to 12 reps at moderate weights is commonly used for muscle growth.
  • 15 or more reps with lighter weights builds muscular endurance.
  • These ranges overlap and good programmes mix them. They are general patterns, not rules.
  • Adults are advised to include muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days a week.

Safety and accuracy

  • Estimates are not a measured max. Fatigue, sleep, technique and exercise choice change real results by several percent.
  • Use a spotter or safety bars for any heavy set, and stop if your form breaks down.
  • Do not attempt a new max straight from an estimate. Warm up and build up in steps.
  • Estimates from more than about 10 reps are less reliable, which is why the calculator limits the input to 1 to 10.

Common mistakes

  • Using a set that was far from failure. If you could have done many more reps, the estimate is too low.
  • Switching formulas between workouts and mistaking the difference for progress.
  • Comparing a machine lift with a free-weight lift. Each exercise has its own 1RM.
  • Mixing units. Check the kilograms or pounds setting before reading the answer.

How to read your result

Treat the estimate as a starting point. Recalculate every few weeks from a recent hard set, and use the percentage tables to choose working weights. If your estimated max is changing quickly, shorter test cycles give a clearer picture; if it barely moves, look at sleep, nutrition and programming before changing the exercise.

This calculator gives an estimate and is not coaching or medical advice. Lift within your ability and ask a qualified trainer or doctor if you are unsure.

Common questions

Which one rep max formula is the most accurate?

None is best for everyone. Epley, Brzycki and Lombardi agree closely at low reps. Use one formula consistently so that changes in your estimate reflect real changes in strength.

How many reps should I use for the estimate?

Between 2 and 10, taken close to failure. The fewer reps, the closer the estimate usually is. Estimates from higher reps are less reliable.

Is it safe to test a true one rep max?

It is demanding and risky without a spotter or safety equipment, which is why many lifters estimate it from a lighter set instead.

Why do my results differ by exercise?

Each lift has its own max. Muscle groups, leverage and technique all differ, so your squat, bench press and deadlift have separate 1RMs.

What percentage of my max should I train at?

It depends on your goal. Heavier weights and fewer reps are used for strength, moderate weights for muscle growth and lighter weights for endurance. The tables show the weight for each percentage.

How often should I recalculate my 1RM?

Every few weeks, using a recent hard set. As you get stronger the number changes, and your working weights should follow it.
