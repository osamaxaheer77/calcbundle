Value Comparison of Claiming Ages

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Social Security pays less if you claim before full retirement age and more if you wait up to 70. For someone born in 1970 (full retirement age 67), age 62 pays 70% of the full benefit and age 70 pays 124%. With a life expectancy of 83, a 5% return and 3% COLA the best claiming age is 68.

How claiming age changes the check

Social Security retirement benefits can start as early as 62, but the monthly amount depends on when you start. Your full retirement age (FRA) is the age at which you get 100% of your calculated benefit, called the primary insurance amount (PIA). Claiming earlier shrinks the check for life; claiming later, up to 70, makes it larger for life.

The reduction and the bonus

  • Early claiming: the benefit falls by 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months before FRA and 5/12 of 1% for every month beyond that.
  • Delayed claiming: each month after FRA adds delayed retirement credits of 2/3 of 1%, which is 8% a year, up to age 70.
  • There is no credit for waiting beyond 70.

How the calculator finds the best age

A bigger check later is not automatically better, because you collect it for fewer years and money received later is worth less. For each claiming age from 62 to 70, the calculator adds up the benefits you would collect until your life expectancy. Each year’s amount grows with the cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) and is then discounted back to age 62 at your investment return. The age with the largest total scores 100%, and the others show how close they come.

Worked example with the default numbers

Born in 1970, so the full retirement age is 67. Life expectancy 83, investment return 5% and COLA 3%. The best age is 68, where the benefit is 108.0% of the full amount. Age 62 scores 94.7% of the best and age 70 scores 98.5%.

Comparing two claiming ages

The second mode compares two options directly. Enter the age and monthly payment for each, for example $1,600 at 62 against $2,810 at 70, plus a return and COLA. It finds the break-even age, the life expectancy at which the later claim has caught up. With the default numbers that is about age 82 and 11 months. If you expect to live past it, waiting wins; if not, the earlier claim does.

What is not included

  • Spouse and survivor benefits, which can make waiting more valuable for the higher earner.
  • Taxes on benefits and the earnings limit if you claim early while still working.
  • Your own health, other income and need for cash now.
  • Changes to Social Security law.

Common mistakes

  • Using life expectancy from a table instead of what fits your health and family history.
  • Assuming a high investment return that you may not earn.
  • Ignoring a spouse who may depend on your benefit later.
  • Claiming at 62 because of fear about the program, without comparing the numbers.

How to read your result

Treat the best age as a starting point. Look at the table to see how close the neighboring ages are: if two ages are within a few percent, other factors such as health, spouse benefits and cash flow should decide. For your actual benefit amount, use your statement from the Social Security Administration.

Common questions

What does relative value mean in the results?

For each claiming age the calculator adds up the benefits you would collect through your life expectancy, adjusting for the cost-of-living increase and discounting later payments at your investment return. It scales the best age to 100%, so every other age shows how close it comes.

Why does the best claiming age change with life expectancy?

Waiting trades smaller checks now for larger checks later. If you expect a shorter life, the larger checks have less time to pay off, so claiming early wins. If you expect a long life, the higher monthly amount eventually makes up for the years you waited.

What is the break-even age?

It is the life expectancy at which claiming later has collected the same total value as claiming earlier. If you expect to live past it, the later claim comes out ahead.

Does this show my actual Social Security benefit?

No. It compares claiming ages as a share of the benefit at your full retirement age. You can enter that monthly amount to see dollar figures, and your personal estimate is on your statement from the Social Security Administration.

Does the calculator include spouse or survivor benefits?

No. It looks only at your own retirement benefit and ignores spousal and survivor benefits, taxes on benefits and the earnings limit for people who claim early while working.

Is there any reason to wait past 70?

No. Delayed retirement credits stop at age 70, so waiting longer does not raise your benefit.
