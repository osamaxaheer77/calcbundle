Quick answer. APR turns a loan's interest rate and fees into one yearly rate. A $100,000 loan at 6% over 10 years with $2,500 of upfront fees has an APR of 6.563%. A $280,000 mortgage at 6.2% with $3,500 in fees and 0.5% points has an APR of 6.367%.

Interest rate versus APR

The interest rate is the price of borrowing the money. The annual percentage rate (APR) also counts the costs of getting the loan, such as origination fees and points, and expresses everything as one yearly rate. Because fees only add cost, the APR is never below the interest rate. When two loans have similar rates, the APR shows which one is really cheaper.

How the calculator finds the APR

It first works out your payment from the amount borrowed, the rate and the payment schedule. It then finds the yearly rate at which those same payments would repay only the cash you actually received, which is the amount financed minus the upfront fees and points. That rate is the APR. With no fees, the APR equals the interest rate.

Tool 1: any loan

The default is a $100,000 loan at 6% over 10 years, compounded and paid monthly, with $2,500 of upfront fees paid out of pocket. The payment is $1,110.21 a month, total interest is $33,225, and the APR is 6.563%, which is 0.563 points above the 6% rate. Without fees the APR would be 6.000%.

Loaned fees versus upfront fees

The calculator separates two kinds of fees. Loaned fees are added to the balance, so you pay interest on them and your payment rises. With $2,500 of loaned fees instead of upfront fees, the amount financed is $102,500 and the payment is $1,137.96. Upfront fees are paid separately in cash: the loan balance and payment do not change, but you effectively receive less than you borrow, so the APR still rises.

Why term matters

A fee is spread over the life of the loan, so on a short loan it weighs more per year. The same $2,500 fee on a $100,000 loan at 6%:

2 years8.503%
3 years7.708%
5 years7.058%

10 years6.563%

Compounding and payback frequency

Interest can compound on a different schedule than you pay. The “Compound” and “Pay Back” settings convert the nominal rate into an equivalent rate for your payment schedule, so the payment is calculated correctly. Most loans compound and are paid at the same frequency, and then the distinction does not matter.

Tool 2: mortgage APR

The mortgage tool starts from a house value, a down payment and the loan terms. The defaults are a $350,000 house, 20% down (a loan of $280,000), 30 years at 6.2%, $3,500 of loan fees and 0.5% points ($1,400). The monthly principal-and-interest payment is $1,714.91, total interest is $337,369, and the APR is 6.367%. With no fees or points it would be 6.200%.

PMI and the 20% rule

Private mortgage insurance applies only while your down payment is below 20%. In the calculator, PMI is included in the payment and the APR until the loan balance falls to 80% of the home value, then it drops out, the way it would with a lender. With 20% down, as in the default, PMI is not charged.

Comparing two offers

Offer X is a $280,000 30-year loan at 5.5% with $4,000 of fees: payment $1,589.81, APR 5.631%. Offer Y is the same loan at 5.75% with $500 of fees: payment $1,634.00, APR 5.766%. If you keep the loan for all 30 years, Offer X has the lower APR. If you sell or refinance after five years, the payments plus fees come to $99,389 for X and $98,540 for Y, so Offer Y is cheaper in that case. APR assumes you hold the loan for its full term, so consider how long you will keep it.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing interest rates when the fees differ.
  • Comparing a loan’s APR with another loan’s interest rate.
  • Forgetting that APR assumes you keep the loan for its full term.
  • Leaving out points or loan fees, which understates the APR.

How to read your result

The large number is the APR. Compare it with the interest rate to see what the fees cost in rate terms. Then compare the total of payments and fees across offers, and think about how long you will keep the loan. Your lender’s own disclosures show the legal APR, which can differ slightly because of how it treats certain charges.

This calculator gives estimates. Lenders must follow federal rules for the APR they disclose, so use the figure on your loan documents for decisions. It is not financial advice.

Common questions

Should I compare loans by interest rate or APR?

APR, whenever fees are involved. Two loans with identical interest rates can have very different true costs if one carries higher upfront fees. APR is built to make that comparison fair.

Why does financing fees into the loan change the payment, but paying them upfront does not?

Financed fees increase the balance you borrow, so they raise your payment. Upfront fees are paid separately in cash, so they do not touch the loan balance or payment, but they reduce what you effectively received, which still pushes the APR up.

Is a lower APR always the better deal?

Usually, but not always. A loan with a higher APR and lower upfront cost can be cheaper overall if you pay it off quickly, because APR assumes you hold the loan for its full term.

Why is the APR always above the interest rate?

Fees and points only add cost. The APR spreads them over the loan, so it is at or above the interest rate and equals it only when there are no fees.

When does PMI drop out of the mortgage APR?

Only while your down payment is below 20%. In the calculator it is included until the balance reaches 80% of the home value and then removed.

Is this the same APR my lender discloses?

It is a close estimate. Lenders follow federal disclosure rules that treat some charges in specific ways, so the figure on your documents may differ slightly.
