How pensions pay out

The most common pension is a defined-benefit plan, where your employer promises a set income in retirement based on your pay and years of service. Most plans let you take that promise in one of two forms: a one-time lump sum, sometimes called the commuted value, or a stream of monthly payments that lasts for life. The calculator helps with three decisions that usually cannot be undone once you make them: lump sum or monthly pension, single-life or joint-and-survivor, and retiring now or working longer. Choose the comparison at the top of the page.

Lump sum or monthly pension

A monthly pension is guaranteed income that cannot run out the way savings can. A lump sum is flexible: you can invest it, spend it as you like and leave what remains to heirs. To compare them the calculator adds up the pension payments you would collect up to each possible life expectancy, lets them grow with the cost-of-living adjustment (COLA), and discounts every year back to your retirement age at your investment return. The break-even age is the first age at which the pension is worth at least as much as the lump sum. Live past it and the pension comes out ahead.

Worked example with the default numbers

The page opens on retirement at age 65, a lump sum of $800,000, a pension of $5,000 a month, an investment return of 5% a year and a COLA of 3.5%. Year one of the pension is 12 × $5,000 = $60,000, so a simple count says the lump sum equals about 13.3 years of pension. But money received later is worth less today, and the COLA raises each year’s payment by 3.5%. Each year’s payments are discounted at 5% back to age 65 (taken at mid-year). With those assumptions the break-even is age 81: if you expect to live to 81 or older, the monthly pension is worth more, and otherwise the lump sum is.

What moves the break-even age

Investment return. The more you expect to earn on a lump sum, the less the pension is worth in today’s terms, so the break-even age rises. Lump sum size. A larger offer needs a longer life to beat. COLA. A pension that rises each year is worth more, so the break-even age falls as COLA rises.

Single-life or joint-and-survivor

A single-life pension pays the most each month but stops when you die. A joint-and-survivor pension pays less and keeps paying your spouse afterwards. The default compares $5,000 a month single-life with $3,000 a month joint, for a retiree of 65 with a life expectancy of 77 and a spouse aged 62 with a life expectancy of 82. The monthly difference is $2,000. The calculator takes two views.

  • Insurance view. If you died at retirement, your spouse would need about $657,173 to replace the survivor pension of $3,000 a month for their expected 20 years. If you can buy a term life policy for that amount for less than the pension difference, the single-life pension plus insurance may be better.
  • Investment view. Take the single-life pension and invest the $2,000 difference each month. By the end of age 77 you would have about $514,709, against about $428,530 of survivor payments still to be paid to your spouse, so the single-life pension comes out ahead on these numbers.

Retire now or work longer

The third comparison weighs retiring early on a smaller pension against working longer for a larger one. The default compares retiring at 60 for $2,500 a month with retiring at 65 for $3,800 a month, an extra $1,300 a month for waiting. Counting only the pensions and valuing them at age 60, the later retirement wins if you expect to live to 87 or older. The comparison leaves out the salary you would earn by working longer, which is a real gain, so the true case for working longer is stronger.

What the calculator does not include

  • Taxes. A lump sum rolled into an IRA is not taxed up front, but pension income is usually taxable. If a lump sum would be taxed, enter the after-tax amount.
  • Plan rules. Plans differ in how they set lump sums and survivor reductions. Use the numbers on your own statement.
  • Plan strength. A pension is only as safe as the plan behind it. In the US, many private plans are insured by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, within limits.
  • Health and family. Your health, your spouse’s health and your other income all matter more than any formula.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing the lump sum with a few years of pension without discounting.
  • Using a return rate that is too high. A small change moves the break-even by years.
  • Forgetting the COLA, which is often a big part of a pension’s value.
  • Ignoring the spouse in the single-life decision.
  • Treating the break-even age as a forecast. It is a comparison point.

How to read your result

For each comparison, the large figure is the break-even age or the better choice, with a sentence that explains it in words. The chart shows the value of each option at every life expectancy, and the table repeats it every five years. Try different returns and COLAs to see how sensitive the answer is.

This is a planning estimate, not a quote or advice. Pension plans differ, so confirm your options in writing and consider a financial adviser before making an irreversible choice.

Common questions

What is the break-even age?

It is the first age at which the monthly pension is worth at least as much as the lump sum, with each year of payments discounted back to your retirement age. If you live to that age or longer, the pension comes out ahead.

Should I take the lump sum or the monthly pension?

It depends on your health, other income, and your confidence in investing a lump sum. A pension is guaranteed for life, while a lump sum is flexible and can be left to heirs. The calculator shows the age at which the pension wins.

What is a joint-and-survivor pension?

It pays a lower monthly amount while you are alive and continues paying your spouse after you die. A single-life pension pays more but stops at your death.

What does the cost-of-living adjustment do?

It raises the pension each year by the percentage you enter. A larger COLA makes the pension worth more, so the break-even age falls.

Does the calculator include taxes?

No. A lump sum rolled into an IRA is not taxed up front, but if it would be taxed, enter the after-tax amount. Pension income is generally taxable.

Is the pension safe?

It is only as safe as the plan behind it. In the United States, many private pensions are insured by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation up to certain limits.
