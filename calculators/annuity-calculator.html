Quick answer. An annuity's accumulation grows from your principal, additions and growth. With $20,000 to start, $10,000 added at the start of each year and 6% growth, the balance after 10 years is $175,533, of which $55,533 is growth.

What this calculator shows

An annuity is a contract, usually with an insurance company, that turns money you pay in into income later. It has two stages. During the accumulation phase you pay in and the balance grows. During the payout phase the contract pays you back. This calculator covers the first stage: how large the balance becomes after a number of years, given a starting principal, regular additions and a growth rate. For the income stage, use the Annuity Payout Calculator.

Worked example with the default numbers

A $20,000 starting principal, $10,000 added each year at the beginning of the year, and 6% growth for 10 years. The growth rate is turned into an equivalent monthly rate, so 6% a year grows the balance by exactly 6% over twelve months. The end balance is $175,533.38. You put in $100,000 of additions on top of the $20,000 principal, so $55,533.38 is growth. As a simple check, with 0% growth the balance is just what you put in: $120,000.

A quick sanity check from the page: $5,000 to start and $100 a month for one year at 0% growth gives $6,200.

Beginning or end of the period

In an annuity due money goes in at the start of the period and earns interest straight away. In an ordinary annuity it goes in at the end, so it sits idle until the next period. The beginning-of-period choice always ends a little higher, and the gap grows with the term and the rate. With the default inputs, adding at the beginning gives $175,533 and at the end $167,625, a difference of $7,908.

Annual or monthly additions

You can add a yearly amount, a monthly amount or both. The annual addition lands in the first month of each year (or the twelfth, if you chose the end of the period), and a monthly addition goes in every month. Putting $10,000 a year as $833.33 a month instead gives $171,871 rather than $175,533, because the money starts earning sooner than an annual lump sum paid at the end of the year but later than one paid at the start.

Fees and surrender charges

Real annuities often carry fees, surrender charges, caps on growth and tax rules on withdrawals, none of which are included here. Even a yearly cost of 1 percentage point matters: if a 6% return becomes 5% after fees, the default plan ends at $164,646, which is $10,888 less; at 4% it ends at $154,468. Read the contract and compare costs before buying.

Types of annuity

  • Fixed: the insurer credits a stated rate.
  • Variable: the balance depends on the investments you choose, and you can lose money.
  • Indexed: growth is linked to a market index, usually with caps and limits.
  • Immediate or deferred: payouts can start right away or after an accumulation period, which is what this calculator models.

What the calculator does not do

  • Taxes. Growth in an annuity is generally tax-deferred, but withdrawals can be taxed.
  • Fees, surrender charges, caps and guarantees.
  • Inflation: the balance is in future dollars.
  • The payout stage. Use the Annuity Payout Calculator for that.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a high growth rate for a fixed annuity.
  • Ignoring fees and surrender periods.
  • Putting the annual addition in the monthly box.
  • Treating the result as a quote from an insurer.

How to read your result

The large number is the end balance. Below it are the starting principal, total additions and total return, with a donut chart and a stacked bar chart for every year. The table can be shown by year or month. Compare the beginning and end settings to see the effect of timing.

This is an estimate for planning, not a quote. Annuities have fees, charges and tax rules that vary by contract. It is not financial advice.

Common questions

What is the accumulation phase of an annuity?

The accumulation phase is the period when you pay money into an annuity and it grows, before any income is paid out. This calculator covers that phase. The income phase is a separate calculation.

What is the difference between an annuity due and an ordinary annuity?

An annuity due takes each addition at the beginning of its period, so it earns interest for the whole period. An ordinary annuity takes it at the end, so it earns nothing until the next one. Adding at the beginning always gives a slightly larger balance.

How is the growth rate applied?

The annual growth rate is converted to an equivalent monthly rate, so a 6% rate grows a balance by exactly 6% over twelve months. Interest is added to the balance every month.

Can I use it for a fixed or variable annuity?

Yes, as a projection. Enter the rate you expect, using a lower one for a fixed annuity and a cautious one for a variable annuity, which can lose value.

Does it include fees and surrender charges?

No. Real annuities often have fees, surrender charges and caps that lower the result, so subtract them from your growth rate to see their effect.

Are annuity withdrawals taxed?

Growth is generally tax-deferred, but withdrawals can be taxed. The calculator does not include tax. Ask a tax professional.
