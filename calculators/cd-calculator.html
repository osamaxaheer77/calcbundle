Quick answer. A CD earns a fixed rate over a fixed term. $10,000 at 5% compounded annually for 3 years grows to $11,576.25, which is $1,576.25 of interest. Compounding more often adds a little, and tax on the interest reduces what you keep.

How the CD calculator works

A certificate of deposit (CD) pays a fixed rate if you leave the money untouched for a set term. The calculator takes your deposit, rate, compounding choice, term and tax rate, and builds the balance month by month. Each month interest is added to the balance, so later interest is earned on earlier interest. If you enter a marginal tax rate, tax is taken out of each month's interest and only the after-tax interest is reinvested.

The compounding setting tells the calculator how the quoted rate is expressed. Annually (APY) means the rate is already the effective yearly yield; the other options treat the rate as a nominal rate (APR) that compounds more often, which gives a slightly higher effective yield.

Worked example with the default numbers

A $10,000 deposit at 5% compounded annually for 3 years: 10,000 × 1.053 = $11,576.25. Interest earned is $1,576.25. With no tax entered, that is the full result.

Does compounding frequency matter?

Slightly. The same 5% rate and 3-year term give different end balances depending on how often interest is added:

CompoundingEnd balanceInterest earned
Annually (APY)$11,576.25$1,576.25
Semiannually$11,596.93$1,596.93
Quarterly$11,607.55$1,607.55
Monthly (APR)$11,614.72$1,614.72

Monthly compounding of a 5% APR equals an effective yield (APY) of about 5.12%. Because banks must show the APY, compare CDs by APY and the difference between most offers is small compared with the difference in rate or term.

Term and growth

Taxes reduce what you keep

CD interest is generally taxed as ordinary income in the year it is earned, even if you do not withdraw it. In a taxable account with a 22% marginal rate, the 5-year example above ends at $12,097 instead of $12,763, so you keep about $666 less (the tax itself plus the compounding it removes). The calculator's tax field lets you see the after-tax result. Tax rules vary by country and account type (for example, a CD held inside a retirement account is treated differently).

CD ladders

A ladder splits money across CDs that mature at different times, so you get some higher long-term rates and regular access to cash. With $10,000 in each of three CDs (the rates below are only an illustration):

CD termRate (APY)Value at maturity
1 year4.50%$10,450.00
2 years4.75%$10,972.56
3 years5.00%$11,576.25

One CD matures every year. When it does, you can renew it at the then-current rate or use the cash. Together the three CDs mature to about $32,999 on a $30,000 start. Run each CD through the calculator to see its value.

Safety and early withdrawal

  • Insurance: deposits at an FDIC-insured bank are insured to at least $250,000 at each bank. Credit unions have a similar insurance through the NCUA.
  • Early withdrawal penalty: most banks charge a penalty, often a number of months of interest. On the default CD, three months of interest is about $125, and the penalty can be larger than the interest earned if you leave early. Check the exact terms before opening.
  • Rate risk: if rates rise after you lock in, your money earns less than new CDs. If they fall, you are protected for the term.
  • Inflation: a CD that pays less than inflation loses buying power even though the balance rises. The Inflation Calculator shows the effect.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing an APR with an APY. Convert both to APY before choosing.
  • Ignoring the penalty. Never put emergency money into a long CD.
  • Forgetting tax on the interest, or assuming interest is only taxed when withdrawn.
  • Letting a CD auto-renew without checking the new rate.

How to read your result

The end balance is what the CD is worth at maturity before any penalty. Compare it with the same deposit in a high-yield savings account or a bond, remembering that a savings rate can change at any time while a CD rate is fixed. For savings you may need soon, a shorter CD or a ladder keeps options open.

This calculator gives estimates and does not account for penalties, bank-specific rules or tax law. It is not financial advice.

Common questions

What happens if I withdraw from a CD early?

Most banks charge an early withdrawal penalty, commonly a forfeiture of a few months of interest. The exact terms vary by bank and by CD length, so read them before you commit money you might need.

Should I ladder CDs instead of buying one large one?

Laddering splits money across CDs that mature at different dates. It balances the higher rates of longer terms against having some money become available regularly.

Is CD interest taxed differently from savings account interest?

Generally no. Both are usually taxed as ordinary income in the year the interest is earned, unless the money is in a tax-advantaged account.

What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR is the stated yearly rate before compounding. APY includes compounding and shows what you actually earn in a year. A 5% APR compounded monthly is about 5.12% APY.

How are CDs insured?

At an FDIC-insured bank, deposits are insured to at least $250,000 at each bank. Credit unions have equivalent coverage from the NCUA. Always check that the institution is insured.

Is a CD better than a savings account?

A CD usually pays a fixed, higher rate in exchange for locking in your money. A savings account is flexible but the rate can fall. The better choice depends on whether you will need the money before the CD matures.
