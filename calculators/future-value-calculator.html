Future Value Calculator

How much could a starting balance and regular deposits become after a set number of periods? Enter your starting amount, periodic contribution and interest rate to estimate future value. Try different deposit timings to compare scenarios.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Future value is what money will be worth after it earns interest. $1,000 plus $100 a year for 10 years at 6% grows to $3,108.93, of which $1,108.93 is interest.

What future value captures

Future value answers the mirror-image question to present value: given money you have today, and possibly add to over time, how much will it be worth at a set point in the future once compound interest has had time to work? It is the maths behind savings projections, retirement estimates and investment growth charts. The formula for a starting amount PV and a deposit PMT each period is FV = PV × (1 + r)n + PMT × ((1 + r)n − 1) ÷ r, where r is the rate per period and n the number of periods.

Worked example with the default numbers

Start with $1,000, add $100 at the end of each year for 10 years, and assume 6% interest per year. The starting $1,000 grows to $1,790.85. The deposits grow to $1,318.08. Together the projected future value is $3,108.93. You contributed $2,000 in total, so about $1,108.93 comes from growth. The “PV” figure the calculator shows, $1,736.01, is the value today of the whole stream: the starting amount plus the present value of every deposit, which always discounts to less than the future value.

Beginning or end of the period?

The deposit can be made at the end or the beginning of each period. Deposits made at the beginning earn one extra period of interest. With the default inputs, beginning-of-year deposits give $3,188.01 instead of $3,108.93, a gain of $79.08 from the extra year of interest on each deposit.

A handy check is the rule of 72: at 6% a sum doubles in about 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years.

Reading the period-by-period table

The table under the result lists every period. In year 1 of the default plan, the $1,000 earns 6% = $60.00 of interest and the $100 deposit is added at the end, so the balance goes from $1,000.00 to $1,160.00. In year 2, interest is calculated on the larger $1,160.00 balance, and so on. Watching how the interest column grows each row is the quickest way to see compounding at work, and why starting early matters more than the size of any one deposit.

A savings-goal example

Suppose you want to know what $200 a month might become. Use 12 periods a year: enter the number of months, the monthly rate (the yearly rate divided by 12) and a deposit of $200. For 5 years at 6% a year that is 60 periods at 0.5%, giving about $13,954 from $12,000 of deposits. Using it for real plans

  • Try more than one reasonable rate rather than treating a single forecast as certain.
  • Taxes, fees and inflation reduce what the eventual balance is worth. See the Inflation Calculator for the purchasing-power effect.

What if there are no deposits?

Leave the periodic deposit at 0 and the calculator simply compounds the starting amount. The default $1,000 at 6% for 10 years becomes $1,790.85.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing periods: an annual rate with monthly deposits gives a wrong answer. Use matching periods.
  • Assuming a high rate will last for decades.
  • Forgetting taxes and inflation.
  • Entering the rate as a decimal, such as 0.06. The box takes a percentage.

How to read your result

The large number is the projected future value. Below it are the present value of the cash flows, the total of your deposits and the total interest, with a chart and a period-by-period table showing the start balance, deposit, interest and end balance. Change one input at a time to see which assumption moves the answer most.

Common questions

What if I only want a lump sum with no recurring deposits?

Leave the periodic deposit at 0. The calculator then simply compounds the starting amount at the given rate for the given number of periods.

Does timing (beginning vs. end of period) matter?

Yes. Deposits made at the beginning of each period earn one extra period of interest, producing a slightly higher future value.

What does the PV figure in the results mean?

It is the value today of the whole cash flow stream: the starting amount plus the present value of every future deposit. It is always less than the future value.

How do I choose an interest rate?

Use a cautious, long-term figure and test a lower one. Actual returns change from year to year, and savings rates differ from investment returns.

Do periods have to be years?

No, but the rate must match the period. For monthly deposits, use the monthly rate and the number of months.

Does it include taxes or inflation?

No. The result is before tax and in future dollars. Use the Inflation Calculator to see what it could buy in today's terms.
