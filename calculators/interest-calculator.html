Quick answer. With compound interest, your balance earns interest on its own interest. $20,000 plus $5,000 added at the start of each year at 5% compounded annually grows to $54,535.20 after 5 years: $9,535.20 of it is interest, and after 3% inflation it is worth about $47,043 in today's money.

Simple and compound interest

Simple interest is charged only on the original amount. Compound interest is charged on the principal plus the interest already added, so interest starts earning interest. Most savings accounts, investments and loans compound. The calculator is built for compound growth, with regular deposits, taxes and inflation.

How the calculator grows your money

It steps forward one month at a time. Each month the balance earns interest at the effective monthly rate that matches your yearly rate and compounding choice (annually, monthly, daily, continuously and so on). If you enter a tax rate, tax is taken from each month’s interest. Deposits are made either at the beginning of each period, which earns one extra period of interest, or at the end.

Worked example with the default numbers

Start with $20,000, add $5,000 at the beginning of each year, and earn 5% compounded annually for 5 years. The ending balance is $54,535.20. You paid in $45,000 ($20,000 plus five deposits of $5,000), so $9,535.20 is interest. After 3% inflation the balance has the buying power of about $47,042.54 in today’s money.

Tax and inflation

Interest from savings accounts, CDs and taxable bonds is generally taxed as income. With a 22% tax rate entered, the default example ends at $52,259 instead of $54,535, because tax removes interest that would otherwise keep earning. The inflation setting shows the same balance in today’s terms: dividing $54,535 by 1.03 five times gives $47,043. Real returns after tax and inflation are what matter for planning.

Regular monthly deposits

Regular deposits do most of the work over long periods. Saving $500 a month at 6% compounded monthly for 30 years gives $502,258. You paid in $180,000, so $322,258 comes from interest. If the deposits are made at the beginning of each month instead of the end, the balance is $504,769, about $2,511 more, because each deposit earns one extra month of interest.

The rule of 72

A quick mental check for how long compounding takes to double money is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly rate. At 4% that is about 18 years, at 6% about 12 years, at 8% about 9 years and at 12% about 6 years. Exact doubling times are slightly different (at 6% annual compounding it is 11.9 years), but the rule is close enough for planning.

Compounding frequency

More frequent compounding raises the result a little: the same rate compounded monthly instead of annually adds a small amount in year one that grows with time. Deposits matter far more than frequency. If you want to compare frequencies for a single rate, the Compounding Frequency Converter shows the effective yearly rate for each.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up the annual and monthly contribution boxes. Use one or both, but not the same money twice.
  • Assuming returns are guaranteed. A savings rate or a long-term investment return can differ from the rate entered.
  • Ignoring tax and inflation, which lower the real growth.
  • Forgetting that deposits at the beginning of the period earn one extra period of interest.
  • Entering 0.05 instead of 5 for the rate.

How to read your result

Compare the ending balance with what you paid in to see how much growth you get from interest. Try different rates, because future returns are uncertain. Then look at the inflation-adjusted balance to see what the money could buy. For goal-based planning, use the Investment Calculator.

Common questions

Why does the calculator ask for a tax rate?

Interest from savings accounts, CDs and taxable bonds is often taxed as ordinary income. Applying a tax rate shows the after-tax growth of the balance rather than the gross figure.

What does the inflation-adjusted buying power figure mean?

It discounts the ending balance by the assumed inflation rate, showing what that future balance would be worth in today's purchasing power. Leave inflation at 0% to see the unadjusted ending balance.

Does contributing at the beginning or end of the period matter?

Yes. A deposit made at the beginning earns one extra period of interest, so the ending balance is slightly higher. The difference grows with the number of deposits.

What is the difference between annual and monthly contributions?

The annual box adds a lump sum once a year and the monthly box adds a smaller amount every month. Both can be used together. Use the schedule under the result to see the exact timing.

How much does compounding frequency matter?

A little. More frequent compounding helps, but regular deposits and the length of time have a much bigger effect on the result.

Is the result guaranteed?

No. It is a projection from the rate you enter. Real returns on investments vary, and savings rates can change.
