How the calculator works

Retirement has two stages: building savings while you work, and spending them afterwards. The calculator models both. While you work, your balance grows each year with investment returns plus what you add. After you retire, each month a withdrawal comes out, the amount rising with inflation once a year, and what is left keeps earning returns. The amount you need at retirement is the lump sum that would pay those inflation-adjusted withdrawals until your life expectancy, with the balance running down to about zero.

There are four questions it can answer: How much do I need to retire? How much should I save? How much can I withdraw? How long will my money last?

Worked example: how much do I need?

The default person is 35, plans to retire at 67 and expects to live to 85. Income is $70,000 growing 3% a year, so by 67 it is $180,256. If retirement spending is 75% of that, the first-year need is $135,192. With 6% investment returns and 3% inflation, the lump sum needed at 67 is $1,877,194 (in today’s dollars, about $728,984).

The plan saves 10% of income each year from a current balance of $30,000. That projects to $1,098,539 at age 67, which is 59% of the need, a shortfall of $778,655. Closing it would take about $8,567 a year or $695 a month on top of the plan.

Worked example: how much should I save?

To reach $600,000 at 67 from $30,000 today at a 6% return, your current savings grow to $193,602 on their own, leaving a gap of $406,398. Closing it needs $363 a month (or $4,471 a year) until 67.

Worked example: how much can I withdraw?

With $30,000 now and $500 added each month until 67, the balance reaches $753,779. That supports a first monthly withdrawal of about $4,524 at 67, rising with 3% inflation each year until 85. A fixed monthly amount over the same 18 years would be about $5,648, but it would lose buying power over time.

Worked example: how long will my money last?

$600,000 with $5,000 withdrawn each month and a 6% return lasts about 15.1 years (181 monthly withdrawals), assuming a constant withdrawal. Other withdrawal lengths for the same balance: 10 years at $6,613 a month, 20 years at $4,244 a month, 30 years at $3,536 a month.

Choosing the assumptions

  • Spending in retirement: 70% to 80% of pre-retirement income is a common starting point, because work costs and retirement saving fall away. Travel, a mortgage or high healthcare costs may justify more. You can enter an exact dollar amount instead.
  • Investment return: returns are never guaranteed. A more cautious figure is reasonable as you near retirement.
  • Inflation: many economies have seen around 2% to 3% a year, but it varies. Test a higher rate.
  • Life expectancy: plan for a longer life than average, because outliving savings is the larger risk.

The return assumption matters a lot: for the default first-year withdrawal, the lump sum needed at 67 is $2,204,561 at a 4% return, $2,031,051 at 5%, $1,877,194 at 6% and $1,740,377 at 7%.

What the calculator does not include

  • Taxes on withdrawals and on investment income.
  • Social Security, pensions and other income, unless you enter them as other income after retirement.
  • Market ups and downs. Real returns arrive unevenly.
  • One-off costs such as long-term care or a home purchase.

Common mistakes

  • Using a high return and low inflation, which makes the target look easy.
  • Ignoring inflation when picking a monthly withdrawal.
  • Starting too late. Saving earlier is cheaper than catching up.
  • Forgetting that the result is in future dollars unless the “today’s dollars” line is shown.

How to read your result

Look at the amount needed, your projected savings and the gap. Then change one assumption at a time: save more, retire later, spend less. Compare the result with the funded percentage rather than chasing exact figures, and review it every year or two. For employer matching and workplace plans use the 401(k) Calculator, and for tax treatment compare the Roth IRA and IRA calculators.

This calculator gives estimates for planning and is not financial advice. It does not model taxes, changing markets or one-off expenses. Consider speaking with a qualified adviser.

Common questions

How much do I need to retire?

It depends on your spending, how many years retirement lasts, returns and inflation. The calculator finds the lump sum at retirement that would fund your inflation-adjusted spending until your life expectancy.

What percentage of income do I need in retirement?

A common planning range is 70% to 80% of pre-retirement income, because work costs and saving drop away. Your own figure may be higher or lower, and you can enter an exact amount.

What return and inflation should I use?

Use cautious assumptions, such as a lower return than the market has averaged and inflation around 2% to 3%, then test higher inflation. Small changes in these two numbers move the result a lot.

Why is my result in future dollars?

Retirement is decades away, so amounts are shown in future dollars with a line giving the value in today's dollars. Compare the today's-dollar figure with your current spending.

Does the calculator include Social Security?

It does not estimate benefits automatically. If you expect Social Security or a pension, enter it as other income after retirement in today's dollars to reduce the withdrawals needed.

How long will my money last?

Use the last mode with your savings and monthly withdrawal. The result assumes a constant withdrawal and a fixed return. If growth covers the withdrawal, the money can last indefinitely.
