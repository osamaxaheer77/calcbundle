Dew Point Calculator

Calculate dew point — the temperature at which air becomes saturated with moisture — from air temperature and relative humidity.

The Magnus formula

This calculator uses the Magnus-Tetens approximation, a widely used formula for estimating dew point from temperature and relative humidity: γ = ln(RH/100) + (17.625T)/(243.04+T), then dew point = 243.04γ/(17.625−γ), with T in °C. It's accurate to within about ±0.4°C for typical atmospheric conditions.

Why dew point is a better humidity gauge than RH

Relative humidity depends on temperature — the same amount of moisture in the air gives a different RH% reading depending on how hot it is. Dew point measures actual moisture content directly, so a dew point of 65°F feels muggy whether the air temperature is 70°F or 95°F, while the RH% for that same muggy air would read very differently at each temperature.

As a rule of thumb: dew points below 55°F feel comfortable, 55–65°F feels somewhat humid, and above 65°F starts to feel muggy or oppressive.

A worked example

At an air temperature of 70°F with a relative humidity of 65%, the dew point is 57.7°F, which is 14.3°C. That is the temperature at which the air would be saturated and dew would start to form. A dew point in the upper 50s feels fairly comfortable to most people, while one above 65°F begins to feel sticky.

Tips for reading dew point

Dew point is a more direct measure of how humid it feels than relative humidity, because it does not change with the time of day. As a rule of thumb, below 55°F feels dry, 55 to 65°F is comfortable, 65 to 70°F is muggy and above 70°F is oppressive. If the night-time temperature is expected to fall to the dew point, expect fog or dew. Gardeners also use it to judge frost risk.

Common questions

Can dew point be higher than air temperature?

No — dew point can equal air temperature (at 100% relative humidity, meaning the air is fully saturated) but never exceed it.

How does dew point relate to fog and condensation?

When the air temperature drops to the dew point, the air becomes saturated and excess moisture condenses out — as dew on surfaces, or as fog if it happens throughout a layer of air.
