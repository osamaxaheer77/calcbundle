Cash Back or Low Interest Calculator

Auto manufacturers often offer either a cash rebate or a below-market interest rate — rarely both. Compare the two to see which one actually saves you more.

Quick answer. A rebate is a fixed discount, while a low rate saves more the bigger and longer the loan. On a $50,000 car over 60 months, a 2% rate beats $1,000 cash back at 5% by $2,092 in total cost.

Why these two offers are almost never equal

A cash rebate is a fixed, one-time discount: its value does not depend on how long you finance the car. A low interest rate is worth more the bigger the loan and the longer the term, because it saves interest every month. That is why the better offer flips with your loan amount and term, not just the headline numbers. The calculator builds both loans and compares their total cost, counting the price, interest, sales tax and fees.

Worked example with the default numbers

A $50,000 car with a $10,000 down payment, 7% sales tax, $2,000 of fees and a 60-month loan. Cash back offer: $1,000 off the price at a 5% rate. The loan is $39,000 and the payment is $735.98. Interest over 60 months is $5,158.69. Low interest offer: no rebate, but a 2% rate. The loan is $40,000 and the payment is $701.11, with interest of $2,066.62. The low rate saves $3,092.06 in interest, which is more than the $1,000 rebate, so the low-interest offer is better by $2,092.06. The total cost of the car is $59,658.69 with cash back and $57,566.62 with the low rate.

When cash back tends to win

Cash back has the edge on shorter loans, smaller loan amounts or when the gap between the two rates is narrow. With a 4% rate on the cash back loan and 3% on the low-rate loan, the default car saves only $970 in interest, so the $1,000 rebate wins by $30. A larger down payment, or paying cash, shrinks the loan the low rate has to work on, which can tip the choice towards the rebate.

Other things to check

  • Taxes: the calculator charges sales tax on the full price in both cases. Some states tax the price after the rebate, which would favour cash back.
  • Fees and add-ons: they are the same in both scenarios here, so they do not change the comparison.
  • Qualification: the lowest advertised rates usually need top credit scores, and a short term. Check that you qualify.
  • Cash flow: a lower payment may matter more to you than the total cost.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing only the monthly payments, not total cost.
  • Using the advertised low rate when you will actually be offered a higher one.
  • Forgetting that the rebate also lowers the amount you borrow.
  • Not asking whether negotiating the price affects eligibility for either offer.

How to read your result

The headline says which offer is better and by how much interest. Below it, two columns show the loan, payment, interest and total cost for each offer. If the result is close, think about your cash flow, how long you will keep the car and whether the dealer will let you negotiate the price as well.

Common questions

Can I negotiate the price and still get one of these offers?

Often yes, but dealers sometimes treat manufacturer incentives as tied to the sticker price. Ask explicitly whether negotiating the price affects your eligibility for either offer.

Does a longer loan term always favor the low interest rate?

Generally yes. Interest accrues over the life of the loan, so a longer term gives a lower rate more time to out-save a fixed cash rebate.

Should I always take whichever offer this calculator says is better?

It is a strong financial signal, but also consider your cash flow. Cash back reduces what you need to finance today, while a low rate spreads savings over the loan.

Why does the rebate also lower the loan in the calculator?

Because the rebate comes off the price, you borrow less in the cash-back case. The comparison counts that, using the total cost of each scenario.

How is sales tax treated?

The calculator charges sales tax on the full price in both scenarios. In states that tax the price after the rebate, cash back would look slightly better than shown.

What does "include all fees in loan" do?

It adds the sales tax and fees to the amount borrowed, so you pay interest on them, instead of paying them upfront.
