Heat Index Calculator

Calculate the heat index — how hot it actually feels — from air temperature and relative humidity.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. The heat index combines temperature and humidity into a feels-like temperature. At 85°F and 70% humidity it is 92.7°F (33.7°C), in the extreme caution band. At 40% humidity the same air feels like 84.3°F.

What the heat index is

The heat index, sometimes called the “feels like” temperature, combines air temperature and relative humidity to estimate how hot it feels to the body. Sweat cools you by evaporating, and high humidity slows evaporation, so the same air temperature feels hotter as humidity rises. The calculator accepts the temperature in °F, °C or kelvin and a humidity from 0 to 100%.

Worked example with the default numbers

The page opens on 85°F and 70% relative humidity. The result is 92.7°F, which is 33.7°C or 306.9 K. That falls in the National Weather Service’s extreme caution band (90°F to 102°F), where heat cramps and heat exhaustion are possible with prolonged exposure or activity. The same 85°F air at 40% humidity gives 84.3°F, which feels close to the real temperature.

How it is calculated

The tool uses the Rothfusz regression, the equation behind the National Weather Service heat index tables. First it computes a simple estimate, 0.5 × (T + 61 + (T − 68) × 1.2 + RH × 0.094). If the average of that estimate and the air temperature is below 80°F, the simple value is the answer, as in NWS practice. Otherwise it uses the full regression, with two adjustments at the edges of its range: a reduction when humidity is below 13% and the temperature is 80°F to 112°F, and an increase when humidity is above 85% and the temperature is 80°F to 87°F. Temperatures given in °C or K are converted to °F first.

Celsius and kelvin

Enter 30°C with 60% humidity and the tool converts to 86°F, giving a heat index of 91.1°F (32.8°C). Entering 303.15 K gives the same result, since 303.15 K is 30°C.

Shade, sun and wind

The heat index assumes shade and light wind. Standing in direct sunshine can add up to 15°F to the perceived temperature, so the default 92.7°F could feel like 107.7°F in full sun, which is in the danger band.

Dry heat and very humid warmth

Dry air changes the picture. At 100°F with only 10% humidity the tool gives 94.1°F, a little less than the air temperature, because the humidity adjustment applies. Very humid warm air at 82°F and 95% humidity gives 94.0°F. Mild weather uses the simple estimate: 70°F at 50% humidity gives 69.0°F.

Staying safe

  • Drink water before you feel thirsty, and take breaks in the shade or air conditioning.
  • Wear light clothing, and plan hard outdoor work for the early morning.
  • Children, older adults, pets and people with health conditions are at higher risk.
  • Never leave a person or pet in a parked car.
  • Treat the heat index as a guide. Your own tolerance, fitness and acclimatisation matter.

Who uses it

Coaches use the heat index to decide whether to hold practice outdoors, employers use it to manage outdoor work, and event planners and gardeners use it to plan around the hottest part of the day.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the humidity as a decimal such as 0.7. Use a percentage from 0 to 100.
  • Mixing up the heat index with the temperature shown by a thermometer in the sun.
  • Using it for cold weather. Below about 80°F the figure is a simple estimate and the heat index is not meaningful for safety.
  • Forgetting that sun adds to the felt temperature.
  • Choosing the wrong unit in the drop-down.

How to read your result

The large number is the “feels like” temperature in °F, with the same value in °C and kelvin and a line that names the risk band. Compare it with the table above, and add up to 15°F if you will be in direct sun.

This is an estimate based on the National Weather Service formula, assuming shade and light wind. It is not medical advice. If someone shows signs of heat illness, get help.

Common questions

What counts as dangerous heat index?

The National Weather Service calls 90 to 102 degrees Fahrenheit extreme caution, 103 to 124 danger and 125 or more extreme danger. Heat cramps and heat exhaustion become likely in the danger band.

Why does humidity make it feel hotter?

Sweat cools you by evaporating. High humidity slows evaporation, so your body cannot shed heat as well and the same air temperature feels hotter.

Does the heat index include sunshine?

No. It assumes shade and light wind. Direct sunlight can add up to 15 degrees Fahrenheit to the perceived temperature.

Why is the heat index equal to a simple number at low temperatures?

Below about 80 degrees Fahrenheit the full regression is not used. The calculator uses a simpler estimate, as the National Weather Service does.

Can I enter Celsius?

Yes. Choose degrees Celsius or kelvin in the drop-down, and the tool converts to Fahrenheit before applying the formula, then shows the result in all three units.

Who is most at risk from heat?

Children, older adults, pets and people with health conditions. Outdoor workers and athletes are also at higher risk when the heat index is high.
