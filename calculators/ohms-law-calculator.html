Ohm's Law Calculator

Ohm's law, V = I × R, relates voltage, current and resistance in an ohmic circuit. Electrical power is P = V × I.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Ohm's law is V = I × R and power is P = V × I. Enter any two values. 110 V at 0.2 A is 550 Ω and 22 W, and 12 V across 6 Ω is 2 A and 24 W.

The equations behind the result

Ohm’s law relates voltage, current and resistance: V = I × R. Combined with the power equation P = V × I, any two of the four quantities are enough to find the other two. Substituting one equation into the other gives three forms of each: P = V × I = I² × R = V² ÷ R. Enter exactly two of voltage, current, resistance and power, and the calculator works out the other two.

Worked example with the default numbers

The page opens on 110 V and 0.2 A. The resistance is V ÷ I = 110 ÷ 0.2 = 550 Ω and the power is V × I = 110 × 0.2 = 22 W. That is a small heater or a bulb on a 110-volt supply. The result shows all four values, with the voltage as the large number.

Units and prefixes

Voltage can be entered in kV, V or mV, current in A or mA, resistance in Ω, kΩ or MΩ, and power in W, kW or mW. The tool converts everything to base units first. For example, 5 V across 2 kΩ gives a current of 0.0025 A (2.5 mA) and a power of 0.0125 W (12.5 mW). Results above a billion or below a millionth appear in exponent form.

Choosing an LED resistor

To run a 2 V LED at 20 mA from a 5 V supply, the resistor must drop the other 3 V. Enter 3 V and 20 mA: the resistance is 150 Ω and it dissipates 0.06 W, so a standard 0.25 W resistor is fine. Round the resistance to the nearest standard value above it.

How voltage and resistance change power

At a fixed resistance, doubling the voltage doubles the current and quadruples the power: the 550 Ω load at 220 V draws 0.4 A and 88 W, against 0.2 A and 22 W at 110 V. At a fixed voltage, a smaller resistance draws more current and more power.

Three ways to write the power

Because V = I × R, power can be found from any two of the other quantities. For 12 V across 6 Ω (2 A): P = V × I = 12 × 2 = 24 W, P = I² × R = 4 × 6 = 24 W, and P = V² ÷ R = 144 ÷ 6 = 24 W. All three agree, and the calculator uses the one that fits the two values you gave it. This also shows why a resistor’s power rating matters: it must be at least the power it dissipates, and a safe choice is about twice that.

Reading Ohm’s law backwards

Rearranged, the law gives I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I. If a 12 V supply drives 2 A, the load is 6 Ω. If a device marked 230 V and 2,000 W is plugged in, its running resistance is about 26.45 Ω and it draws about 8.7 A. A measured current that is much higher or lower than expected is a clue that something in the circuit has changed.

Where the law applies

The equations hold exactly for simple resistive circuits with no capacitors or inductors, under both DC and AC. Heaters and incandescent bulbs behave this way. Motors, transformers and many electronics are not simply resistive, and AC circuits with reactance need impedance instead. Resistance can also change with temperature, as in a light-bulb filament, so the resistance measured cold is lower than the working value.

Common mistakes

  • Filling in three or four boxes. Enter exactly two.
  • Mixing units, such as mA with Ω, without choosing the unit in the box.
  • Using the law for a circuit with large reactance.
  • Confusing power with energy. Power is a rate in watts, energy is watts over time.
  • Entering zero or a negative number. The calculator needs values greater than 0.

How to read your result

The large number is the voltage, with current, resistance and power listed below it. The two you entered are repeated, so you can check they match your inputs. If the screen says to enter exactly two values, clear the extra box.

Common questions

What is Ohm's law?

Ohm's law says voltage equals current times resistance, V = I x R. With the power equation P = V x I, any two of voltage, current, resistance and power give the other two.

How many values do I need to enter?

Exactly two of the four. If you fill in one, three or four, the calculator cannot tell which equations to use and asks for exactly two.

How do I find power from current and resistance?

Use P = I squared times R. A current of 0.02 amps through 220 ohms gives 0.088 watts.

Does Ohm's law work for AC?

Yes, for resistive loads such as heaters and incandescent bulbs. Circuits with capacitors or inductors need impedance, which this calculator does not handle.

Why does doubling the voltage quadruple the power?

Current doubles with the voltage, and power is voltage times current, so power rises four times. In symbols P = V squared divided by R.

Can I use milliamps and kilohms?

Yes. Choose the unit next to each box, and the calculator converts to base units first.
