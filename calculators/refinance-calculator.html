What a refinance calculator compares

Refinancing replaces your current mortgage with a new one, usually to lower the payment, shorten the term or take out cash. The calculator takes your current balance, payment and rate, then compares them with a new rate, term, points, closing costs and optional cash out. It reports the new payment, the effective APR including costs, the lifetime saving or extra cost, how much faster or slower the loan is paid off, and the break-even month.

Break-even: two ways to estimate it

Payment-based. Divide the upfront cost by the monthly payment saved. For the default example that would be $6,500 ÷ $8.92 = 728 months, which is misleading because the new loan also pays off faster. It works well only when the old and new loans have the same remaining term. For example, refinancing a $300,000 balance at 7% into a 30-year loan at 6% with $4,500 of closing costs lowers the payment from $1,995.91 to $1,798.65, a saving of $197.26 a month, so the payback is about 23 months.

Interest-based (what the calculator uses). It adds up the interest on each loan month by month and finds the first month when the difference reaches your upfront costs. It accounts for different terms, but it is still an estimate, not a guarantee.

A common rule of thumb in consumer guidance is to refinance only if you recover the costs in about two years or less, and only if you expect to stay in the home that long.

Points, closing costs and APR

  • One discount point equals 1% of the loan. On $300,000 that is $3,000. Points buy a lower rate, so they only pay off if you keep the loan long enough.
  • Closing costs on a refinance can be about as much as on the original purchase loan. Ask for the Loan Estimate and compare.
  • APR: the calculator estimates an effective rate that spreads the costs over the term. The official APR on your lender's Loan Estimate follows set rules and may include other charges, so use that for comparisons between offers.
  • Prepayment penalties: check whether your current loan has one before you refinance.

Cash-out refinancing

A cash-out refinance increases the new loan by the cash you take. Interest is charged on the larger balance, so the payment may rise even at a lower rate. When you compare offers, use the same cash-out amount and look at total interest and fees separately. Part of the bigger balance is cash you received, not a pure cost.

When refinancing makes sense, and when it does not

  • Good case: a meaningfully lower rate, you will stay long enough to pass break-even, and the new term does not push your payoff date far into the future.
  • Weak case: a small rate drop with high costs, or you plan to sell soon.
  • Watch out: restarting a 30-year clock on a loan with 15 years left. The payment falls, but total interest can rise.

Common mistakes

  • Looking only at the monthly payment and ignoring the cost and the term.
  • Forgetting points and closing costs.
  • Comparing advertised rates instead of APR and costs.
  • Ignoring how long you will stay in the home.
  • Rolling closing costs into the loan without checking the effect on total interest.

How to read your result

Check four numbers together: the new payment, the break-even month, the lifetime savings or cost, and the change in payoff time. If break-even is later than the time you expect to keep the loan, refinancing probably does not pay. Common questions

How do I know if refinancing is worth it?

Compare the monthly saving, the lifetime saving and the break-even month with how long you expect to keep the home. If you will stay past break-even and the new term does not stretch your payoff date too far, it may be worth it.

What does the break-even point mean?

It is the first month when the interest saved by the new loan equals your upfront costs. After that, the refinance has paid for itself. It is an estimate, not a guarantee.

What is a discount point?

One point is 1% of the loan amount, paid up front to get a lower interest rate. Points make sense only if you keep the loan long enough to recover them.

Why is my estimated APR different from my lender's?

The calculator spreads your entered costs over the term to estimate an effective rate. The lender's official APR follows specific disclosure rules and may include other charges. Use the Loan Estimate for comparing offers.

Is a cash-out refinance cheaper?

Not by default. It increases the loan balance, so you pay interest on the cash you take. Compare offers with the same cash-out amount and check total interest and fees.

Should I refinance into a 30-year loan to lower my payment?

It lowers the payment, but if you restart the clock the total interest often rises. Check the lifetime cost and payoff time shown in the calculator.
