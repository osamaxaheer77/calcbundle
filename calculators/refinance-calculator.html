When would refinancing pay for itself?

A lower interest rate can reduce your mortgage payment, but refinancing usually involves upfront costs. Enter the balance and payment on your existing loan, then test a new rate, term, points and closing fees. Compare the projected payment, interest and estimated break-even point instead of judging the rate alone.

A quick refinance payment example

Suppose your remaining mortgage balance is $250,000. For a new 20-year fixed-rate loan at 6%, the principal-and-interest payment is approximately $1,791.08 per month before taxes, insurance or lender fees. At 7% for the same 20-year term, the payment would be approximately $1,938.25. This illustrates the payment difference only; whether refinancing is worthwhile also depends on your current loan's remaining term and the new loan's costs.

What the refinance break-even estimate means

The calculator compares accumulated interest on the current and new loans and reports the first month in which the estimated interest difference reaches the entered upfront costs. This is an interest-based break-even estimate, not a guarantee that refinancing saves money overall. A different payoff term, a cash-out amount, or selling the home early can change the decision.

For a simple same-term comparison, you can also estimate a payment-based break-even as closing costs divided by monthly payment savings. This shortcut does not capture differences in loan balances or payoff dates.

APR, points and closing costs

One discount point is 1% of the new loan amount. Paying points and lender fees can make a lower advertised note rate less attractive. This tool estimates an effective rate from the loan amount and the costs you enter; it is not a substitute for the official APR in a lender's Loan Estimate, which follows specific disclosure rules and may include other charges.

Be careful with cash-out refinancing

Cash-out refinancing increases the new loan balance. When you compare total payments, remember that part of the larger balance represents cash received, not simply an additional borrowing cost. Compare offers with the same cash-out amount and check the total interest and fees separately.

Common questions

What's a "point," exactly?

One point equals 1% of the loan amount, paid upfront in exchange for a lower interest rate. Whether points are worth paying depends heavily on how long you plan to keep the loan — the longer you stay, the more likely the lower rate earns back its upfront cost.

Does refinancing reset my loan's clock?

It depends on the new term. Replacing a loan with 20 years remaining with a new 30-year mortgage extends the repayment schedule. A term closer to the remaining years can avoid that extension, though the monthly payment may be higher.

Is a lower monthly payment always a good sign?

No. Extending the loan term may reduce monthly payments but increase the total interest paid. Review the new loan term, interest, upfront costs and any cash-out amount before deciding.
