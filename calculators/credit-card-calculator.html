Quick answer. Each month a credit card adds interest at one twelfth of its APR and your payment covers that first. An $8,000 balance at 18% paid at $200 a month takes 62 months and costs $4,309 in interest; clearing it in 2 years needs $399.39 a month.

How credit card interest works

A credit card charges interest each month on the balance you carry. The calculator uses one twelfth of the yearly rate (APR) for each month: at 18% that is 1.5% a month. Each payment first covers that month’s interest, and only what is left reduces the balance. When the payment is small, most of it goes to interest and the balance falls slowly, which is why a card balance can take years to clear.

A payment that is only equal to the monthly interest never reduces the balance. The calculator warns you if your payment is too low for that reason.

Worked example with the default numbers

A balance of $8,000 at 18% APR with a payment of $200 a month. In the first month the interest is 8,000 × 1.5% = $120, so only $80 of the $200 reduces the balance. The card is paid off after 62 months (5 yr 2 mo), and you pay $4,309 in interest, for a total of $12,309.

Pay off by a deadline

The second option works backwards. To clear the same balance in 2 years (24 months) you need a payment of $399.39 a month, which costs $1,585 in interest, compared with $4,309 if you paid $200 a month.

The minimum payment trap

Many cards set the minimum at the monthly interest plus 1% of the balance, with a floor of around $15. On this $8,000 balance at 18%, paying only that minimum would take about 320 months (26 yr 8 mo) and cost around $11,423 in interest, because the minimum falls as the balance falls. Card issuers in the United States must show on your statement how long minimum payments would take and what payment would clear the balance in three years.

Ways to pay off faster

  • Pay more than the minimum. Even a small increase saves a lot of interest.
  • Pay early in the month. Interest accrues on your balance, so earlier payments help.
  • Stop adding new charges while you pay down the card.
  • With several cards, put extra money on the one with the highest rate (the avalanche method) or the smallest balance (the snowball method). The Credit Cards Payoff Calculator compares them.
  • Consider a balance transfer to a lower or zero introductory rate, but check the transfer fee and what the rate becomes afterwards.

Interest-free offers and penalty rates

A 0% introductory offer can be very valuable if you clear the balance before it ends, because every dollar goes to principal. If any balance remains when the offer ends, interest usually applies at the standard rate. A late payment can also trigger a penalty APR that is much higher than your normal rate, so set up automatic payments for at least the minimum.

What the calculator assumes

  • A fixed APR and one interest charge a month (many cards compute interest daily, so statements can differ a little).
  • No new purchases, fees or penalty rates.
  • A payment made every month in the same amount.

Common mistakes

  • Paying only the minimum and expecting the balance to disappear quickly.
  • Entering a monthly rate instead of the yearly APR.
  • Forgetting that new purchases add to the balance.
  • Ignoring a penalty APR after a late payment, which can be much higher.

How to read your result

Look at the payoff time and the total interest together. If the interest is a large share of the balance, increase the payment or switch to a deadline-based plan. Use the schedule under the result to see the month-by-month split between interest and principal, then update your budget so that the payment becomes automatic.

This calculator gives estimates and is not financial advice. Check your statement for exact figures.

Common questions

Why does it say my payment is too low?

If your payment is no more than one month of interest on the balance, the balance never falls, so the calculator cannot give a payoff time. Raise the payment above the interest shown.

What interest rate should I enter?

Enter the yearly APR from your statement. The calculator divides it by 12 for each month. Cards that compute interest daily may give slightly different figures.

How is the minimum payment worked out?

It varies by card. A common method is the monthly interest plus 1% of the balance, or 2% to 3% of the balance, with a floor of around $15. The quick buttons in the calculator fill in these amounts.

Does it include new purchases or fees?

No. It assumes a fixed rate and no new charges, late fees or penalty rates. Stopping new purchases is part of the plan the calculator illustrates.

Is it better to pay a fixed amount or pay off by a date?

A fixed date gives you a clear target and a set payment. A fixed amount is flexible. Try both: the deadline mode tells you the payment needed to be debt-free by that date.

Should I use a balance transfer?

It can help if the new rate is much lower and you can pay off the balance during the promotional period, but watch the transfer fee and the rate after the offer ends.
