How the calculator builds your plan

The calculator uses the debt avalanche method. You pay the minimum on every debt and put any extra money on the debt with the highest interest rate. When that debt is gone, its payment moves to the debt with the next-highest rate, and so on until everything is paid. Because the most expensive debt is attacked first, this approach usually costs the least interest.

  • Extra per month: a regular amount on top of all the minimums.
  • Once a year: a larger payment such as a bonus or tax refund, made in the first month of each year.
  • One-time: a single payment in a month you choose.
  • Fixed total: if you answer yes, the payment on a cleared debt keeps going to the others, so your monthly outlay never changes but debts disappear faster.

Worked example with the default debts

The calculator starts with four debts: an auto loan ($25,000 at 4.9%), a mortgage ($250,000 at 4%), and two credit cards ($6,000 at 18.99% and $3,000 at 16.99%), with $100 extra a month and a fixed total payment. The plan clears everything in 136 months (11 years 4 months) and costs $72,853 in interest.

What extra money and rollover are worth

Without the $100 extra, the same debts take 144 months (12 years 0 months) and cost $79,432 in interest. If you did not move the payments from cleared debts onto the others, even the $100 extra would leave you paying until month 178 and $88,796 in interest.

Avalanche vs snowball

The snowball method puts extra money on the smallest balance first for quick wins, while the avalanche method follows the highest rate. Take three debts: Card A $5,000 at 24%, Card B $2,000 at 12% and Loan C $8,000 at 6%, minimum payments $100, $50 and $150, and $200 extra a month.

Avalanche is cheaper by $378, but the difference is small compared with simply adding the extra money in the first place ($6,433 saved against minimums only). The best plan is the one you keep following, so pick snowball if early wins keep you motivated.

Tips for paying debt off faster

  • Pay at least the minimum on every debt, always, to avoid late fees and credit damage.
  • Find a regular extra amount you can sustain, even if small, and automate it.
  • Use windfalls (tax refund, bonus) as one-time or yearly payments.
  • Avoid new borrowing while the plan runs.
  • Consider consolidating or a balance transfer only if the total cost really falls. The Debt Consolidation Calculator checks that.

What the calculator assumes

  • Fixed interest rates, charged monthly (one twelfth of the yearly rate).
  • No new borrowing, fees or changes to the minimum payments.
  • Mortgages and car loans are included only as balances, rates and payments; special terms such as prepayment penalties are not modelled.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the whole payment of a mortgage including taxes and insurance as the “payment”. Use only the principal-and-interest payment.
  • Forgetting a debt, which makes the plan look cheaper than it is.
  • Entering a monthly rate instead of the yearly rate.
  • Choosing “no” for the fixed total and wondering why payoff takes so long.

How to read your result

Look at the total time and total interest first, then at the order table: it tells you which debt to attack and when each will be cleared. Compare a plan with and without extra payments to see how much each extra dollar saves, and re-run the calculator whenever a balance, rate or payment changes.

Common questions

What is the debt avalanche method?

It is a payoff plan where you make the minimum payment on every debt and send all extra money to the debt with the highest interest rate. When it is paid off, you move on to the next highest. It usually minimises the total interest.

What is the difference between the avalanche and snowball methods?

The snowball method pays the smallest balance first for quick wins. The avalanche method pays the highest rate first and usually saves more interest. The calculator uses avalanche, and the example above shows the size of the difference.

What does “fixed total amount” mean?

If you choose yes, the payment that was going to a cleared debt keeps going to the remaining debts, so your total monthly payment stays the same. If you choose no, each debt keeps only its own payment.

Why can the calculator say a payment is too low?

If a debt's payment is no more than one month of its interest, the balance never falls. Raise the payment or add an extra monthly amount.

Can I include a mortgage?

Yes, with its remaining balance, rate and principal-and-interest payment. Leave out taxes, insurance and fees that are not part of the loan.

Does paying off the highest rate always save the most?

In almost every case yes, because interest is charged on balances. The savings difference compared with other orders can be small, so choose the order you can stick with.
