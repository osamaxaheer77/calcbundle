What actually goes into an auto loan

The sticker price is just the starting point. Cash incentives and trade-in equity reduce what you need to borrow; sales tax and title/registration fees add to the total cost, whether or not you roll them into the loan itself. Financing those taxes and fees keeps more cash in your pocket at signing, but you'll pay interest on them for the life of the loan — the same trade-off as financing any other upfront cost.

Why trade-in equity (or debt) matters

A trade-in is really two numbers netted against each other: what the dealer credits you for the car, minus whatever you still owe on it. If you owe more than the trade-in is worth — being "underwater" — that shortfall gets added to what you need to finance on the new car instead of reducing it, which is worth knowing before assuming a trade-in automatically shrinks your loan.

Auto loans depreciate against an asset that's losing value the entire time you're paying it off. Being "upside down" — owing more than the car is worth — is common in the first couple of years of a long loan term, which is worth considering before choosing a 72- or 84-month term to shrink the monthly payment.

Common questions

Should I finance taxes and fees or pay them upfront?

If cash is tight, financing keeps the amount due at signing lower. If you can afford it, paying upfront avoids paying interest on taxes and fees that don't have anything to do with the car's value.

Does a longer loan term always mean a worse deal?

Not automatically, but it usually means more total interest paid and a longer stretch of being underwater on the loan, even though the monthly payment is lower. It's worth comparing total cost, not just the monthly number, across different terms.

What's the "Vehicle Price" mode for?

It works backward from a monthly payment you know you can afford to the vehicle price that would produce it — useful for budgeting before you've picked a specific car.
