Auto Loan Calculator

Estimate car payments including sales tax, fees, incentives, and trade-in value — or work backward from a target monthly payment to a vehicle price.

Quick answer. Your car payment is set by the amount financed, the interest rate and the number of months. For a $50,000 car with $10,000 down, 7% sales tax, a 60-month loan at 5%, the payment is about $755 a month, you pay about $5,291 in interest and the car costs about $60,791 in total.

How this auto loan calculator works

The calculator works out the amount you need to borrow, then applies the standard loan formula: payment = loan × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where r is the annual rate divided by 12 and n is the number of months. The loan amount is vehicle price − incentives − down payment − trade-in equity, where trade-in equity is the trade-in value minus anything you still owe on it.

Sales tax and fees are handled separately. By default you pay them at signing together with the down payment. Tick Include Taxes and Fees in Loan to add them to the loan instead, which lowers the cash you need up front but means you pay interest on them. A second mode, Vehicle Price, works backwards from a monthly payment you can afford to the price that payment supports.

Financing taxes and fees, and trade-ins

If you tick Include Taxes and Fees in Loan in the example above, the amount financed rises from $40,000 to $45,500, the payment to $858.64 and the total interest to $6,018. You keep $5,500 in your pocket at signing, and pay about $728 more in interest for it.

Trade-ins can work against you. If your trade-in is worth $12,000 but you still owe $15,000, you are $3,000 underwater. That shortfall is added to the new loan instead of reducing it: the example loan becomes $43,000 and the payment $811.46. The FTC warns that dealers sometimes advertise that they will pay off the old loan, but add the difference to the new one.

Working backwards from a monthly budget

Using the Vehicle Price mode with a $750 monthly payment, a 60-month term, 5% and $10,000 down, the loan supported is about $39,743, so the price you can afford is about $49,743 before sales tax and fees (these are paid at signing unless you include them in the loan).

Common mistakes

  • Shopping by monthly payment only. A low payment on a long term can hide thousands in extra interest.
  • Forgetting sales tax, title and registration, which can add several thousand dollars.
  • Rolling negative equity from the old car into the new loan without realising the debt carries over.
  • Comparing rates without comparing terms and fees. Compare the APR and the total cost.
  • Skipping pre-approval. Getting a quote from a bank or credit union before visiting a dealer gives you a rate to compare.

How to read your result

Look at the monthly payment, the total interest and the cash needed at signing together. A commonly quoted guideline is to put about 20% down, keep the term to four years or less and keep total car costs near 10% of gross income. It is only a guide, but a loan that fails on all three deserves a second look. Because cars lose value quickly, a shorter term also helps you stay out of negative equity.

This calculator gives estimates. Your lender will quote the exact APR, fees and total after checking your credit.

Common questions

Should I finance taxes and fees or pay them up front?

Financing them keeps the cash due at signing lower. If you can afford it, paying them up front avoids paying interest on costs that have no connection to the car's value. In the example above, financing them adds about $728 in interest.

Does a longer loan term always mean a worse deal?

Not automatically, but it usually means more total interest and a longer period of owing more than the car is worth, even though the monthly payment is lower. Compare total cost across terms, not just the payment.

What is the Vehicle Price mode for?

It works backward from a monthly payment you can afford to the price that payment supports, given your rate, term and down payment. It is useful for setting a budget before choosing a car.

What is negative equity?

It means you owe more on a car than it is worth. If you trade in a car with negative equity, the shortfall is usually added to your next loan, which raises the amount you finance and the interest you pay.

Is sales tax charged on the full price?

The calculator charges sales tax on the full vehicle price. Some states reduce the taxable amount by the trade-in value, so check your state's rule and adjust the tax rate if it applies.

What is a good interest rate on a car loan?

It depends on your credit, the loan term and whether the car is new or used. Compare offers from a bank or credit union with the dealer's financing and judge them by APR and total cost.
