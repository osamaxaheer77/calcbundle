Quick answer. Your monthly mortgage payment is principal and interest from the loan, plus property tax, insurance and sometimes PMI or HOA dues. On a $400,000 home with 20% down at 6.5% over 30 years, principal and interest is about $2,023 a month and the total is about $2,548 once typical tax and insurance are added.

How this mortgage calculator works

Your payment has two layers. The first is principal and interest, which depends only on the loan: the amount borrowed, the interest rate and the number of months. For a fixed-rate loan the formula is M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where P is the loan amount, r is the annual rate divided by 12 and n is the number of monthly payments.

The second layer is the cost of owning the home that lenders usually collect with the payment: property tax, homeowners insurance and, if your down payment is small, mortgage insurance. Lenders often hold these in an escrow account and pay the bills for you. The calculator adds each one, plus HOA dues and other yearly costs, and lets you set a yearly increase for each. It can also apply extra payments (monthly, yearly or one-time), show an estimate for biweekly payments and list the full amortization schedule.

Where each payment goes

Interest is charged on the balance you still owe, so the first payments are mostly interest. In month 1 the interest is $320,000 × 6.5% ÷ 12 = $1,733.33, and only $289.28 reduces the loan. The split does not reach 50/50 until payment 233 (about year 19.4), and the payment itself never changes.

Extra payments: a small amount makes a big difference

Every extra dollar goes straight to principal and stops accruing interest for the rest of the loan. Adding $200 a month to the example loan shortens it from 360 to 281 payments (about 6.6 years sooner) and saves roughly $105,429 in interest. Tell your servicer the extra should be applied to principal, otherwise some servicers hold it toward the next payment.

What changes the payment the most

Rate and term move the payment far more than most people expect. On the same $320,000 loan over 30 years, each half-point change in rate shifts the payment by roughly $100 to $110 a month:

Term matters too. A 15-year loan at the same 6.5% would cost about $2,788 a month instead of $2,023, but you would pay only about $181,758 in total interest rather than $408,142. In practice 15-year loans usually carry a slightly lower rate than 30-year loans.

PMI, escrow and what the payment leaves out

  • PMI (private mortgage insurance) protects the lender and usually applies when the down payment is under 20%. On many conventional loans you can ask to cancel it once the balance reaches 80% of the original home value, and it ends automatically at 78% if you are current. Rules differ by loan type, so confirm with your servicer.
  • Escrow amounts are estimates that your servicer re-checks every year. When tax or insurance rise, so does your payment even on a fixed-rate loan.
  • Not included: closing costs, moving costs, maintenance and utilities. A common planning allowance for upkeep is 1% of the home value a year.
  • HOA dues are paid separately from the loan in most cases, but they still count toward what the home costs you each month.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing only principal and interest. Two homes with the same loan can differ by hundreds of dollars a month in tax and insurance.
  • Using a rate seen in an ad. Advertised rates often assume points and a high credit score. Use a real quote from a lender.
  • Budgeting up to the maximum the bank approves. Lenders often use about 28% of gross income for housing and 36% for all debt as a guide; staying under that leaves room for repairs and surprises.
  • Assuming extra money is applied to principal automatically. Check the payment instructions.
  • Forgetting that taxes and insurance rise over time. Use the yearly-increase fields to see the effect.

How to read your result

Look at three numbers. First, the total monthly payment, which is what leaves your account each month. Second, total interest, which is the price of borrowing over the full term. Third, the payoff date, which moves earlier as soon as you add extra payments. A common check is whether the total monthly housing cost stays near 28% of gross monthly income. For a fuller test of what you can afford, use the House Affordability Calculator and read the guide on the 28/36 rule.

This calculator gives estimates. Your lender's official Loan Estimate will show the exact rate, fees, escrow amounts and mortgage insurance for your loan.

Common questions

Does a bigger down payment always lower the monthly payment?

Usually yes. A larger down payment reduces the amount borrowed, so the principal-and-interest payment falls when the rate and term stay the same. It can also reduce or remove mortgage insurance, depending on the loan type.

Why is total interest bigger than the loan itself?

Over 30 years at a rate near 6.5%, you pay interest on a large balance for a long time. In the example above interest is $408,142 against a $320,000 loan. A shorter term or extra payments cut it sharply.

What does an extra payment actually change?

It lowers the balance right away, which shortens the loan and saves interest. Your required monthly payment normally stays the same unless the loan is recast. Ask your servicer to apply the extra to principal.

Is the biweekly estimate exact?

It is a close estimate. Paying half the monthly amount every two weeks makes 26 half-payments a year, which equals 13 full payments instead of 12. That extra payment is where nearly all of the savings come from. Some servicers hold biweekly money until a full payment is reached, so ask how yours handles it.

Should I choose a 15-year or a 30-year mortgage?

A 15-year loan has a higher payment but far less total interest, and its rate is often lower. A 30-year loan keeps the payment affordable. Many buyers take the 30-year loan and make extra payments when they can, which keeps the flexibility of the lower required payment.

Does the calculator include closing costs?

No. Closing costs are one-time fees paid when you buy, commonly a few percent of the loan. Add them to the cash you need up front, separately from the monthly payment.
