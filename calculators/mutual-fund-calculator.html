Mutual Fund Calculator

Estimate the ending value and net return of a mutual fund investment after sales charges and operating expenses — including the net IRR after fees.

years months

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Mutual fund fees reduce your return every year. $20,000 plus $1,000 a month for 5 years at a 5% gross return, with a 2% sales charge and 0.5% expenses, ends at $90,077, a net IRR of 3.84%, with $2,926 paid in fees and charges.

Two very different kinds of fee

A sales charge (or “load”) is a one-time toll on your way in: a percentage taken from every dollar you contribute before it is invested. Operating expenses are the opposite: a small, continuous drag on the fund’s return, charged as a percentage of assets every year you hold it. A deferred sales charge, or back-end load, is paid when you sell instead of when you buy. Operating expenses are summed up in the expense ratio shown in a fund’s prospectus.

Worked example with the default numbers

Invest $20,000 up front, add $1,000 a month for 5 years, assume a 5% gross return, a 2% sales charge and 0.5% operating expenses. You contribute $80,000 in total ($20,000 plus $60,000). The ending value is $90,077.09. Net of everything, your gain is $10,077.09, a net IRR of 3.844% a year, below the 5% headline return. Fees and charges come to $2,925.83: sales charge $1,600.00 and operating expenses $1,325.83.

With no fees at all the same plan would end at $93,339.37, so the 2% charge and 0.5% expenses cost you $3,262.28 over five years.

Why net IRR matters more than the stated return

The rate of return you enter is the fund’s gross performance, before fees. Net IRR answers the question that matters: given what you actually put in and what you actually got back, what yearly return did you earn? It is often meaningfully lower than the headline, especially over short periods when the upfront sales charge has had no time to be diluted by growth.

Long horizons: fees matter most

Over decades the yearly expense ratio dominates. Starting with $10,000 and adding $500 a month at a 7% gross return for 30 years: with no fees you would have $660,849; in a low-cost fund (0.10% expenses) $648,079; in a typical 1.00% fund $544,691; and in a high-cost fund with a 5% load and 1.50% expenses $470,537.

How to compare funds

  • Look at the expense ratio in the prospectus, and any front-end or deferred loads.
  • No-load funds avoid the upfront charge, but a no-load fund with high expenses can still cost more over a long holding period than a load fund with very low expenses.
  • Deferred charges usually fall the longer you hold, to discourage early selling.
  • Fees are certain, while returns are not. A lower fee is one of the few things you can control.

What the calculator assumes

  • A steady return every year, with fund expenses taken from it monthly.
  • Contributions are added at the end of each month, with annual contributions at the end of each year.
  • No taxes, no inflation and no changes in fees.
  • Past performance does not predict future returns.

Common mistakes

  • Using the fund’s net return as the rate of return and then charging expenses again.
  • Ignoring the sales charge on every new contribution.
  • Comparing funds on past performance without comparing costs.
  • Forgetting that the deferred charge is applied to the whole balance at sale.

How to read your result

The large number is the ending value after all charges. Below it are the money you put in, the net return, the net IRR and each fee. The chart shows how much of the gain the fees took. To compare with a fee-free outcome, set the charges to zero in a second run.

This calculator gives illustrations, not forecasts. Investments can lose value. It is not investment advice.

Common questions

What's a deferred sales charge?

Also called a back-end load, it is a fee charged when you sell or withdraw rather than when you invest. It often declines the longer you hold the fund, to discourage early redemption.

Are no-load funds always better?

They avoid the upfront sales charge, but check the operating expense ratio too. A no-load fund with high ongoing expenses can cost more over a long holding period than a load fund with a very low expense ratio.

Why does the operating expense dollar figure grow faster than a flat percentage suggests?

Because it is charged against the fund's balance every year, and that balance is growing. The compounding that helps your returns also compounds the dollar cost of a fixed percentage fee.

What is the difference between the rate of return I enter and the net IRR?

The rate you enter is the fund's gross return before fees. Net IRR is the yearly return you actually earned on the money you put in, after sales charges and expenses.

Where do I find a fund's fees?

In the fund's prospectus and on its website. The expense ratio is the total annual operating expense as a percentage of assets, and any sales loads are listed alongside it.

Does the calculator include taxes?

No. Taxes on distributions and gains depend on your situation and are not included.
