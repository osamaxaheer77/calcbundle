RMD Calculator

Estimate a required minimum distribution using your age, account balance and applicable beneficiary details. RMD rules can depend on account type, year and individual circumstances, so confirm obligations using current IRS guidance.

Projected Balance and Distributions

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. An RMD is the minimum you must withdraw each year from a traditional IRA or 401(k): last December's balance divided by an IRS distribution period. For a person aged 75 with $300,000, the period is 24.6 and the RMD is $12,195.

What a required minimum distribution is

Money in a traditional IRA or a workplace plan such as a 401(k) has not been taxed yet. The IRS does not let it sit forever: once you reach a set age you must withdraw a minimum amount each year and pay income tax on it. That amount is the required minimum distribution (RMD). You can always withdraw more, never less.

How the amount is worked out

Take your account balance on December 31 of the previous year and divide it by a distribution period from an IRS table based on your age. Most people use the Uniform Lifetime Table. If your only beneficiary is a spouse who is more than 10 years younger, the Joint Life and Last Survivor table applies and gives a longer period and a smaller RMD. Both tables are in IRS Publication 590-B.

Worked example with the default numbers

Born in 1951, with a $300,000 balance on December 31, 2025, and a spouse born in 1953 (not more than 10 years younger, so the Uniform table applies). For 2026 you are 75, the distribution period is 24.6, and the RMD is $300,000 ÷ 24.6 = $12,195.12. That is 4.07% of the balance. The page opens with the current year; for 2025 (age 74, period 25.5) the RMD would be $11,764.71, and for 2027 (age 76, period 23.7) it would be $12,658.23.

When RMDs begin

Your first RMD is for the year you reach that age, but you may delay it until April 1 of the following year. If you do, you must also take that second year’s RMD by December 31, so two taxable withdrawals land in the same year. After that, each RMD is due by December 31. Start ages come from the SECURE 2.0 Act and could change again, so check the current rules.

The projection

If you enter an expected return, the calculator assumes you take only the RMD each year at year end and that the rest grows at that rate, running up to age 120. With 5% growth on $300,000 from age 75, the first RMD is $12,195, the biggest is $24,705 at age 95, and the account balance is $309,454 at 80, $299,027 at 85, $265,423 at 90 and $130,353 at 100. Leave the return blank to see just this year’s RMD.

A younger spouse

For an owner aged 75 whose sole-beneficiary spouse is 55, the joint period is 32.4 instead of 24.6, so the RMD on $300,000 is $9,259 instead of $12,195. The calculator uses the joint table only when you say a spouse is your sole beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger.

Planning points

  • RMDs are taxed as ordinary income, so they can push you into a higher bracket or affect other things that depend on income.
  • If you have several IRAs, calculate each, but you can take the total from any of them. For 401(k)s each plan is separate.
  • Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the owner’s lifetime.
  • Missing an RMD can bring a penalty. Check with your custodian or a tax professional.

What the calculator does not cover

  • Inherited accounts, which follow different rules.
  • Still working for the employer that sponsors your plan, which can delay RMDs from that plan.
  • Qualified charitable distributions and other special cases.
  • Taxes on the withdrawals.

Common mistakes

  • Using this year’s balance instead of the balance on December 31 of last year.
  • Forgetting that delaying the first RMD means two in one year.
  • Using the Uniform table when the joint table applies, or the reverse.
  • Entering age instead of the year of birth.

How to read your result

The large number is the RMD for the year you picked, with your age and the distribution period beside it. If you gave an expected return, the chart and table show every year to age 120. Treat it as an estimate and check the official figure with your plan or custodian.

Common questions

When do I have to start taking RMDs?

At 73 if you were born from 1951 through 1959, and at 75 if you were born in 1960 or later. Your first RMD is due by April 1 of the year after you reach that age.

What balance do I use?

The account balance on December 31 of the previous year. Divide it by the distribution period for your age.

What if my spouse is much younger?

If your spouse is your only beneficiary and is more than 10 years younger, the Joint Life and Last Survivor table applies. It gives a longer period and so a smaller RMD.

Do Roth IRAs have RMDs?

Not during the owner's lifetime. Traditional IRAs and most workplace plans do.

Can I take more than the RMD?

Yes. The RMD is a minimum. Anything you take above it is taxed as income too.

Does this cover inherited IRAs?

No. Inherited accounts follow different rules, which depend on the owner and the beneficiary.
