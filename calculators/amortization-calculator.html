Quick answer. Amortization splits each equal loan payment into interest and principal. A $200,000 loan at 6% over 15 years has a payment of $1,687.71 and $103,788 of total interest; in the first month $1,000.00 of the payment is interest.

What amortization means

Amortization is a plan for paying off a loan with a series of equal scheduled payments. Every payment is the same size, but what is inside it changes: part covers the interest owed for the period and the rest reduces the amount you borrowed. Because the balance keeps shrinking, the interest part shrinks with it, so more of each payment goes to principal as the loan matures. An amortization schedule lists every payment with its interest, principal and remaining balance.

The payment formula

For a loan of P at monthly rate r (the yearly rate divided by 12) over n months, the payment is P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n). Each month, interest is the balance times r, principal is the payment minus that interest, and the new balance is the old balance minus principal. At a 0% rate the payment is simply the loan divided by the number of months.

Worked example with the default numbers

Borrow $200,000 at 6% for 15 years (180 payments). The payment is $1,687.71 a month, you pay back $303,788.46 in total and $103,788.46 of that is interest. The first month’s interest is $200,000 × 0.5% = $1,000.00, so only $687.71 of the first payment reduces the balance.

Why the split changes over time

Interest is charged only on the balance still owed, so it is largest in the first year and smallest in the last. Two $200,000 loans at 6% show the effect: the first accrues $1,000 of interest in its first month; a loan with only $20,000 left accrues about $100. The payment is flat while the interest fades and the principal grows.

Comparing loan terms

For a 30-year loan at 6% the payment on $200,000 is about $1,199.10. In the first month about $1,000.00 is interest and $199.10 is principal. The longer the term, the lower the payment and the more interest you pay overall:

What extra payments do

Tick Optional: make extra payments to add an extra monthly amount, an extra yearly amount, and up to ten one-time payments, each with its own date. In this calculator the extra goes straight to principal after the scheduled payment, so the scheduled payment stays the same and the loan ends sooner. For the default loan:

Early extra payments save much more than late ones, because a dollar paid in year one avoids many years of interest, while a dollar paid in year fourteen avoids only one. In real life, ask your lender to apply extra money to principal and check for a prepayment penalty.

Monthly or annual schedule?

Both views contain the same numbers. The annual view adds each year’s interest and principal into one row, and the monthly view lists every payment with its date, so you can find the balance after a particular payment.

Which loans fit this calculator

  • Fixed-rate, fixed-term loans with level payments: mortgages, auto loans, student loans and personal loans.
  • It does not fit credit cards, where the balance and minimum payment move independently.
  • It does not fit interest-only or balloon loans, which do not spread principal evenly.
  • It excludes taxes, insurance and PMI. Use the Mortgage Calculator for the full home payment.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting an extra payment to lower the required monthly payment. It usually shortens the loan instead, unless the lender recasts it.
  • Comparing loans by payment alone and ignoring total interest.
  • Forgetting to tell the lender that extra money should go to principal.
  • Using this schedule for a variable-rate loan, where the rate and payment can change.

How to read your result

Check the monthly payment, the total interest and the date the balance reaches zero. Then open the schedule to see how much interest you have paid at each point, which tells you what you would save by paying early or refinancing. If you are weighing a different loan, compare total interest, not only the payment.

This calculator gives estimates for planning. Lenders may round differently or charge fees, so use your loan documents for exact figures. It is not financial advice.

Common questions

Does making an extra payment lower my required monthly payment?

Usually not. This calculator keeps the scheduled payment the same, applies extra amounts to principal and shows how a smaller balance can shorten the loan and reduce interest. Actual lender rules can differ.

Why is almost my entire early payment interest?

Interest is calculated on the full remaining balance, and early on that balance is close to the whole loan. As the balance falls, so does the interest, and the principal share grows to fill the gap.

What is the difference between the annual and monthly schedule?

They show the same numbers at different detail. The annual view totals each year into one row, and the monthly view lists every payment so you can see how the balance moves month to month.

Can I schedule more than one lump-sum payment?

Yes. The calculator supports up to ten separate one-time extra payments, each on its own date, on top of an optional recurring monthly or yearly extra payment.

Does the schedule include taxes, insurance or PMI?

No. It covers principal and interest on a fixed-rate loan only. For a home payment with those costs, use the Mortgage Calculator.

Can I use it for a loan with a changing rate?

Not accurately. The schedule assumes a fixed rate and level payments, so an adjustable-rate loan can differ once the rate changes.
