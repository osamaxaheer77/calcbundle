Half-Life, Mean Lifetime and Decay Constant

Give any one of the three to get the other two.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. After one half-life, 50% of a substance is left, then 25%, then 12.5%. With 100 units falling to 10 over 50 time units, the half-life is 15.0514997832. Carbon-14 (5,730 years) takes about 19,035 years to fall to 10%.

What the calculator does

The page has two tools. The first takes any three of the quantity remaining (Nt), the initial quantity (N0), the elapsed time (t) and the half-life (t½), and finds the fourth. The second converts between the half-life, the mean lifetime and the decay constant, so you only need to know one of them.

Worked example with the default numbers

The first tool opens with 10 units remaining out of 100 after 50 time units, and the half-life blank. The half-life is t½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N0 ÷ Nt) = 50 × 0.693147 ÷ ln 10 = 15.0514997832. The same result also shows the mean lifetime, 21.7147240952, and the decay constant, 0.0460517018599 per time unit. Check: 50 ÷ 15.0515 is 3.32 half-lives, and 100 × 0.53.32 is 10. The second tool opens with a half-life of 5,730, the figure for carbon-14, and returns a mean lifetime of 8,266.64258429 and a decay constant of 0.000120968094339.

The formulas

  • Remaining amount: Nt = N0 × (1/2)t / t½
  • Initial amount: N0 = Nt × 2t / t½
  • Elapsed time: t = t½ × ln(N0 ÷ Nt) ÷ ln 2
  • Half-life: t½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N0 ÷ Nt)
  • Decay constant: λ = ln 2 ÷ t½, and mean lifetime: τ = 1 ÷ λ = t½ ÷ ln 2

Finding how long it took

Leave the time blank and enter the other three. For a sample of carbon-14 with a half-life of 5,730 years, falling to 25% takes two half-lives, or 11,460 years. Falling to 10% takes t = 5,730 × ln 10 ÷ ln 2 = 19,035 years, not the 1.5 half-lives you might guess, because the decay slows as less remains. Radiocarbon dating uses this same relation to turn the share of carbon-14 left in a sample into an age.

Finding the starting amount

Leave the initial quantity blank. If 12.5 units remain after 3 half-lives, the starting amount was N0 = 12.5 × 23 = 100. Working backwards in this way is how a dose or a sample size at an earlier time is estimated.

Half-life, mean lifetime and decay constant

The decay constant λ is the fraction that decays per unit of time, in the continuous sense. The mean lifetime τ is the average time an atom survives, and equals t½ ÷ ln 2, which is about 1.4427 half-lives. After one mean lifetime, 1/e, or 36.8%, of the original remains.

What the tool does not cover

  • It models decay, so the remaining amount must be smaller than the initial amount and greater than zero.
  • It describes a very large number of atoms. It cannot say when one particular atom will decay.
  • Medicines leaving the body often follow the same pattern, but real bodies add absorption and other effects. Treat the result as a model, not medical advice.
  • Chains of decay, where the product is also radioactive, need more than a single half-life.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing time units, such as a half-life in years and a time in days.
  • Entering all four values and expecting a check. With all four filled, the tool recalculates the half-life from the other three.
  • Expecting a substance to be gone after two half-lives. A quarter remains.
  • Confusing the mean lifetime with the half-life. The mean lifetime is about 44% longer.
  • Entering the percent left as a decimal in one box and a count in the other. Use the same units for both quantities.

How to read your result

The large figure is the quantity you left blank, and a small line below it shows the formula that was used. When the half-life is calculated, the page also lists the mean lifetime and the decay constant. Large and small values are shown in the form 1.2 × 10n once they go outside a normal range.

This calculator models ideal exponential decay. For radiation safety, medical dosing or dating work, rely on measured data and a qualified professional.

Common questions

How do you calculate half-life?

Use t½ = t × ln 2 ÷ ln(N0 ÷ Nt), where N0 is the starting amount, Nt is the amount left and t is the time that passed. For 100 falling to 10 over 50 time units, the half-life is 15.05.

How much is left after 3 half-lives?

One eighth, or 12.5%, is left, because the amount is halved three times.

What is the difference between half-life and mean lifetime?

The half-life is the time for half of the substance to decay. The mean lifetime is the average survival time of an atom, equal to the half-life divided by ln 2, about 1.44 times longer.

What is the decay constant?

It is the fraction that decays per unit of time, equal to ln 2 divided by the half-life. It is the reciprocal of the mean lifetime.

Can a substance fully disappear after enough half-lives?

In the model it never reaches exactly zero, since each half-life halves what is left. In practice, only a few atoms remain after many half-lives, and the last ones decay at random times.

Can I use this for medicines?

Many drugs leave the body in a way that looks like exponential decay, so the same formulas apply as a model. Real dosing depends on more factors, so follow professional advice.
