Projected 401(k) Balance

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. A 401(k) grows from your contributions, your employer's match and investment returns. At age 30 with a $75,000 salary, 10% contributed, a 50% match up to 3% and a 6% return, the balance at 65 is $1,711,800. A $10,000 early withdrawal leaves you only $6,000.

How a 401(k) projection works

A 401(k) is a workplace retirement account. You choose a percentage of your pay to contribute, your employer may add a match, and the balance grows with investment returns. This page has three tools: a balance projection, an early-withdrawal cost estimator and a tool that finds the contribution range that earns your full employer match. Each year the salary rises by the increase you enter, you contribute your percentage of it, the employer adds its match, and the balance earns the annual return on the starting balance plus half of that year’s contributions (contributions arrive evenly through the year).

Worked example with the default numbers

Age 30, retiring at 65, with a $75,000 salary rising 3% a year, a $35,000 balance, 10% of salary contributed, a 50% match up to 3% of salary, and a 6% annual return. At 65 the balance is $1,711,800, which is $608,345 in today’s purchasing power at 3% inflation. Over 35 years you contribute $453,466, your employer adds $68,020 and investment returns add $1,155,315.

What the balance could pay in retirement

The calculator spreads the age-65 balance over the years to your life expectancy (85 here) at the same 6% return. That gives a fixed $12,264 a month ($4,358 in today’s dollars), or a payment that rises 3% a year starting at $9,511 a month. All figures are before tax and assume steady returns.

Understanding the employer match

A match is usually written like “50% up to 3%”. Your employer adds 50 cents for each dollar you contribute, but only on the first 3% of your salary. On a $75,000 salary that is at most $1,125 a year. Contributing less than 3% leaves part of the match unclaimed, which is why it is called free money.

Tool 3: maximize your match

Some plans match in two tiers. The defaults are 50% up to 3% of salary plus 20% on the next amount up to 6%. To earn the full match you must contribute at least 6% of salary: the match is then $1,575 a year ($1,125 from tier one plus $450 from tier two) on a contribution of $4,500. The upper end of the range is the IRS limit divided by salary: $24,500 of $75,000 is 32.7%. Contribute between 6% and 32.7% and you earn the full match without hitting the limit early.

The reason for an upper end: once your own contributions reach the IRS limit in a year, they stop, and so does the employer match for the rest of that year.

Contribution limits

The calculator uses the 2026 employee limit of $24,500, plus an $8,000 catch-up from age 50. The IRS also sets a higher catch-up at ages 60 to 63, which this tool does not model. In the projection the limit is assumed to rise with inflation. You can untick the box to ignore it.

Taking money out early

Withdrawals before about age 59½ are taxed as income and in most cases also face an extra 10% penalty. With a $10,000 withdrawal, a 25% federal rate and a 5% state rate, tax and penalty cost $4,000 and you receive $6,000.

The penalty is waived in some cases, such as a qualifying disability or leaving your employer in or after the year you turn 55. Income tax is still due. The tool asks four questions to decide whether the penalty applies.

What the calculator does not include

  • Taxes on withdrawals in retirement. A traditional 401(k) is taxed as income when you take money out; a Roth 401(k) is taxed up front instead.
  • Changing returns. It uses a steady return every year.
  • Employer vesting schedules, which can reduce the match you keep if you leave early.
  • Plan fees and the exact rules of your plan.

Common mistakes

  • Not contributing enough to earn the full match.
  • Assuming a high return that you may not earn.
  • Forgetting inflation. A balance in future dollars buys less.
  • Cashing out when changing jobs. Roll the account over instead.

How to read your result

Look at the balance at retirement and the monthly figure in today’s dollars. Then change one input at a time: contribute 1 or 2 points more, lower the return by a point, retire two years later. Common questions

How does the employer match work in this calculator?

Your employer adds the match percentage of what you contribute, up to the match limit, which is a percent of your salary. With a 50% match up to 3%, contributing 3% or more of your salary earns the most your employer will add, which is 1.5% of your salary.

Why would my employer stop matching partway through a year?

If your contributions reach the IRS yearly limit before the year ends, your own contributions stop, and so does the employer match for the rest of that year. The maximize-match tool shows the range of percentages that earns the full match without hitting the limit early.

Are the results before or after tax?

Before tax. Withdrawals from a traditional 401(k) are taxed as ordinary income, so what you keep depends on your tax bracket in retirement. A Roth 401(k) is taxed up front instead, so its withdrawals are generally tax-free.

What are the 401(k) contribution limits?

For 2026 you can contribute up to $24,500 of your own money, plus an $8,000 catch-up once you are 50 or older. A larger catch-up applies at ages 60 to 63, which this calculator does not model. The calculator assumes the limit rises with inflation in future years.

Is there a penalty for taking money out early?

Generally yes. Withdrawals before about age 59 and a half face a 10% penalty on top of income tax. Exceptions include a qualifying disability and leaving your employer in or after the year you turn 55, which waives the penalty on that employer's plan.

What return should I assume?

Use a cautious long-term figure and test a lower one. Returns vary from year to year, and the calculator applies the same return every year.
