Quick answer. Molecular weight is the sum of the atomic weights in a formula. Water, H2O, is 2 x 1.008 + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol, which is 88.8% oxygen by mass. Glucose, C6H12O6, is 180.156 g/mol.

What molecular weight is

The molecular weight of a compound is the sum of the atomic weights of all the atoms in its formula. Water, H2O, has two hydrogen atoms at 1.008 and one oxygen atom at 15.999, so its molecular weight is 18.015. In grams per mole it is the molar mass: 18.015 grams of water contain one mole, or 6.02214076 × 1023 molecules. The two terms are used interchangeably here, although molar mass is strictly the more correct name for the value in g/mol.

How to write a formula

  • Write each element symbol with a capital first letter, followed by a lowercase letter if it has one, and then the number of atoms: NaCl, H2SO4, C6H12O6.
  • Use round, square or curly brackets for groups, with the number after the closing bracket: Ca(OH)2 or Fe2(SO4)3.
  • For a hydrate, put a dot between the parts and the number of water molecules before the water: CuSO4.5H2O.
  • A number at the very start multiplies the whole part, so 2H2O has four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms.
  • Symbols are case-sensitive: Co is cobalt, but CO is carbon and oxygen.

Brackets. Ca(OH)2 has one calcium, two oxygens and two hydrogens: 40.078 + 2 × 15.999 + 2 × 1.008 = 74.092. In Fe2(SO4)3 the 3 multiplies S and O4, giving 2 iron, 3 sulfur and 12 oxygen atoms and a weight of 399.858.

Hydrates. CuSO4·5H2O is the salt plus five water molecules: 159.602 + 5 × 18.015 = 249.677.

Using the result: moles, mass and composition

  • Grams to moles: divide the mass by the molar mass. 100 g of NaCl (58.440 g/mol) is 1.711 mol.
  • Moles to grams: multiply. 2 mol of glucose is 2 × 180.156 = 360.312 g.
  • Molecules: one gram of water contains about 3.34e+22 molecules (Avogadro’s number divided by 18.015).
  • Purity and composition: the mass percent column gives the share of each element, which you can compare with an assay.

About the atomic weights

Atomic weights are weighted averages over the natural isotopes of an element, which is why they are not whole numbers. The calculator uses abridged standard atomic weights of the kind published by IUPAC’s Commission on Isotopic Abundances and Atomic Weights. For elements with no stable isotope, such as technetium, the value is the mass number of the longest-lived isotope. If you need more precision for a specific sample, use exact isotopic masses.

What it does not support

  • Charges and ions.
  • Isotopes such as D2O (heavy water).
  • Fractional subscripts.
  • Polymers and mixtures. Enter the formula of a single compound.

Common mistakes

  • Writing CO when you mean Co, or the reverse.
  • Forgetting that a number after a bracket multiplies everything inside.
  • Leaving out the water in a hydrate.
  • Using lowercase for the first letter of a symbol.

Common questions

How do you calculate molecular weight?

Add up the atomic weights of every atom in the formula. For H2O, that is 2 x 1.008 + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol.

What is the difference between molecular weight and molar mass?

They have the same number. Molar mass is the mass of one mole in g/mol, and molecular weight is the relative mass of one molecule. Chemists often use the terms for the same value.

How do I enter a hydrate such as CuSO4.5H2O?

Put a dot between the salt and the water, with the number of water molecules before the water formula. The calculator then adds the five waters to the salt.

Why does the calculator say a symbol is not an element?

Symbols are case-sensitive. Co is cobalt, but CO is carbon and oxygen, and a lowercase first letter is not accepted.

Where do the atomic weights come from?

They are abridged standard atomic weights of the kind published by IUPAC. Elements with no stable isotope use the mass number of the longest-lived isotope.

Can I use brackets?

Yes. Round, square or curly brackets can group atoms, with the number of groups after the closing bracket, as in Ca(OH)2 or Fe2(SO4)3.
