Quick answer. Calories burned equals the hourly rate for your activity at your body weight times the hours you do it. A 70 kg (154 lb) person walking slowly for 45 minutes burns about 110 calories, and 30 minutes of moderate running burns about 369 at 155 lb.

How the calculator estimates calories burned

Every activity has an energy cost that depends on how hard it is and how heavy you are. The calculator stores published hourly energy-use values for each activity at body weights of 125, 155 and 185 pounds and works out the values in between. Below 125 lb and above 185 lb it keeps the per-kilogram rate fixed. The core formula is simple: calories = calories per hour at your weight × hours.

Scientists describe effort in METs (metabolic equivalents). One MET is the energy used sitting quietly, so a 4-MET activity uses four times as much. The rough rule behind the tables is calories per hour ≈ MET × body weight in kg, which is why heavier people burn more for the same effort.

The page has two tools: by duration (pick an activity and how long you did it) and by distance (walking, running or cycling a set distance at a given speed).

Worked example: the default settings

The calculator starts with a 45-minute slow walk for a 70 kg (154 lb) person. A slow walk is about 147 calories an hour at that weight, so 45 minutes (0.75 hours) burns about 110 calories.

Body weight changes the result

The same 30 minutes of moderate running burns about 308 calories at 125 lb, 369 at 155 lb and 430 at 185 lb. Weight is the reason two people doing the same workout see different numbers.

By distance: walking, running and cycling

For distance-based activities the calculator finds the hourly rate for your speed by working between the slow, moderate, fast and very fast values, then multiplies by the time the distance takes. A faster pace burns more per hour but finishes sooner, so calories for a fixed distance rise much more slowly than the hourly rate. For a 70 kg person:

ActivityTimeCalories
Walk 5 km at 4 km/h75 min218

Distance is a better way to compare workouts than time, because it shows what the same trip costs at different speeds.

What affects the real number

  • Fitness: fitter people are more efficient and usually burn slightly less for the same effort.
  • Body composition and age: muscle burns more than fat, and metabolism changes with age.
  • Conditions: hills, wind, heat and cold change the effort.
  • Breaks: in games and sports, rests mean you were not working the whole time. Enter only the active minutes.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the whole session length for a sport that includes long rests.
  • Adding the "calories burned" back as extra food. Exercise numbers are rough, and over-eating after training is easy.
  • Ignoring the unit selector (pounds or kilograms).
  • Counting calories from activities of daily life twice if you also use a total-daily-energy figure such as TDEE.

How to read your result

Treat the figure as a good estimate, not a measurement. For weight management, a pound of body fat holds roughly 3,500 calories, so one hard workout does not change much on its own. What matters is the weekly total. The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans recommend that adults get at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week, which for a 155 lb person walking at a moderate pace is about 480 calories a week.

Calorie estimates are rough and are not medical advice. Check with a doctor or dietitian before making big changes to your diet or exercise.

Common questions

How many calories do I burn walking 5 kilometers?

It depends on your weight and speed. A 70 kilogram person walking 5 km at 4 km/h burns roughly 218 calories over about 75 minutes. Use the distance tool to try your own numbers.

What is a MET?

A MET, or metabolic equivalent of task, compares the energy an activity uses with the energy used at rest. A value of 2 means twice the energy of sitting quietly, and 8 means eight times as much.

Why do heavier people burn more calories?

Moving a larger body takes more energy. For the same activity and time, a heavier person burns more, which is why the result changes with body weight.

Does a faster pace burn more calories for the same distance?

Only a little. A faster pace burns more per hour but you finish sooner, so the total for a fixed distance rises much more slowly than the hourly rate.

How accurate is this calculator?

The numbers are averages for a typical person. Fitness, age, body composition, terrain and temperature all change the real value, so use the result as a planning guide.

Should I eat back the calories I burn?

Not automatically. The estimate is rough and it is easy to overestimate it. If you are trying to lose weight, adjust your intake by watching your weight trend over a few weeks instead of matching each workout.
