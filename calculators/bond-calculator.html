Bond Calculator

Solve for a fixed-rate coupon bond's price, face value, yield, time to maturity, or coupon — given the other four, valued on a coupon date.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. A bond is worth the present value of its coupons plus its face value at the market yield. A 5% coupon bond with 3 years left and a 6% yield is worth $97.33 per 100, a discount to face value. When yields rise, prices fall.

Why a bond price moves opposite to yield

A bond’s coupon payments are fixed the moment it is issued. When market yields rise above the bond’s coupon rate, investors will not pay full face value for a stream of below-market payments, so the price must fall until the bond’s effective return matches the market. When yields fall below the coupon rate, the fixed payments look attractive and the price rises above face value. This inverse relationship is one of the most fundamental facts in fixed-income investing: when interest rates rise, bond prices tend to fall, and vice versa.

The pricing formula

Price = coupon × [1 − (1 + r)−n] ÷ r + face value ÷ (1 + r)n, where r is the yield per coupon period (the annual yield divided by payments per year) and n is the total number of coupon periods to maturity. The calculator prices the bond on a coupon date, and it can solve for any one of price, face value, yield, time to maturity or coupon, given the other four.

Worked example with the default numbers

A bond with a face value of 100, a 5% annual coupon (so $5 a year), three years to maturity and a 6% yield. The coupons are worth 5 × 2.6730 = $13.37 today and the face value is worth 100 ÷ 1.063 = $83.96, so the price is $97.3270. Because the yield (6%) is above the coupon rate (5%), the bond trades at a discount to face value. Going the other way, if you enter a price of 97.327 and ask for the yield, you get 6.0000%.

Par, premium and discount

A bond priced at its face value trades at par, which happens when the yield equals the coupon rate: at a 5% yield the default bond is worth $100.00. If the yield is below the coupon it trades at a premium (at a 4% yield it is worth $102.78), and if the yield is above the coupon it trades at a discount. All three are snapshots of the same price-yield relationship.

Coupon frequency

Bonds can pay coupons annually, semiannually, quarterly or monthly. More frequent payments are reinvested sooner, so the price at the same nominal yield changes slightly: annually $97.3270, semiannually $97.2914, quarterly $97.2731, monthly $97.2607 per $100 for the default bond.

What yield means here

The yield is the yield to maturity: the total annual return if you buy at the price shown and hold to maturity, counting both the coupons and the difference between the price and the face value. The current yield (annual coupon divided by price) is a simpler figure: for the default bond it is 5 ÷ $97.33 = 5.14%, which ignores the gain as the price pulls up to $100.

What this calculator does not cover

  • Accrued interest for bonds bought or sold between coupon dates.
  • Taxes, fees and commissions.
  • Credit risk: the issuer may not pay, which is why riskier bonds offer higher yields.
  • Callable bonds, floating-rate bonds and inflation-linked bonds.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the coupon rate with the yield.
  • Entering the price as a percentage of face value in the wrong units. Use the same units for face value and price.
  • Assuming a high coupon always means a good investment.
  • Ignoring that long bonds can lose a lot of value if yields rise.

Common questions

Why does coupon frequency matter?

More frequent coupon payments mean your money is reinvested more often, which slightly changes both the bond's fair price and its effective yield compared with the same nominal rate paid less frequently.

What does "yield" mean here specifically?

It is the yield to maturity: the total annual return if you buy the bond today and hold it to maturity, counting both the coupon payments and any difference between the purchase price and the face value.

Is a higher coupon rate always better for an investor?

Not by itself. What matters is the yield relative to the price paid. A high-coupon bond bought at a steep premium can yield less than a low-coupon bond bought at a discount.

Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?

Existing coupons are fixed. When new bonds pay more, investors will pay less for the old ones, so the price falls until the old bond's yield matches the market.

What is the difference between coupon rate and yield?

The coupon rate is the fixed interest the bond pays on its face value. The yield is the return implied by the price you pay, so it changes as the price changes.

Does it include accrued interest?

No. The calculator prices the bond on a coupon date. Between coupon dates the buyer also pays accrued interest, which this tool does not cover.
