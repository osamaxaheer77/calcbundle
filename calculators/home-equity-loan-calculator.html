How Much Can You Borrow?

Estimate your maximum home equity loan amount from your home's value, your remaining mortgage, and a lender's loan-to-value limit.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. A home equity loan is a lump-sum second mortgage with a fixed rate. $150,000 at 8% over 15 years costs $1,433.48 a month and $108,026 in interest. On a $600,000 home with a $250,000 mortgage and an 80% lender limit, you may borrow up to $230,000.

A home equity loan is a second mortgage

A home equity loan lets you borrow against the part of your home you own outright. Your house is collateral a second time: the first lien is your main mortgage and the home equity loan sits behind it as a second lien. You get the money as a lump sum with a fixed rate and a fixed monthly payment, so it suits a one-time expense with a known cost. A HELOC, by contrast, is a revolving credit line, usually with a variable rate.

Tool 1: payment, interest and APR

Enter the loan amount, interest rate and term. With the defaults of $150,000 at 8% over 15 years, the payment is $1,433.48 a month, the total of 180 payments is $258,026.06 and interest is $108,026.06.

Closing costs and the APR

Tick Include Closing Costs to add a dollar or percentage fee. It can be deducted from the loan, so you borrow $150,000 but receive less, or paid upfront. With $7,500 deducted, you receive $142,500, the cost of the loan (interest plus closing costs) is $115,526 and the APR is 8.860% instead of 8%, because you repay the same payments on less cash. The APR is calculated the same way if you pay the costs separately.

Tool 2: how much can you borrow?

Lenders look at your combined loan-to-value ratio (CLTV): your mortgage plus the new loan divided by the home’s value. The calculator multiplies the value by the lender’s limit and subtracts what you owe. For a home worth $600,000 with a $250,000 mortgage, your current ratio is 41.7%. With an 80% limit the maximum combined debt is $480,000, so you may borrow up to $230,000.

This is an estimate based only on equity. Lenders also weigh your credit history and debt-to-income ratio; many look for a credit score of at least 630 and a debt-to-income ratio under 43%, and either can reduce the amount you are approved for.

Home equity loan or HELOC?

  • Home equity loan: one lump sum, fixed rate, fixed payment, repaid on a set schedule.
  • HELOC: borrow as needed up to a limit during a draw period, usually a variable rate, so payments can change. Use the HELOC Calculator to model it.

The risks

Because your home secures the loan, a lender can foreclose if you cannot repay, the same risk as your main mortgage. Borrowing also reduces the equity you have if home prices fall. Closing costs, appraisal fees and other charges add to the cost, so compare offers by APR.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the “may borrow up to” figure as an approval.
  • Using the lender’s LTV limit as if it were your current ratio.
  • Ignoring closing costs when comparing offers.
  • Choosing the longest term only for the lower payment.

How to read your result

In Tool 1, look at the payment, then the total interest and, if you added closing costs, the APR. In Tool 2, the headline is the largest loan the equity math allows. Compare it with what you actually need, since borrowing less keeps more equity in your home.

Common questions

Is a home equity loan the same as a HELOC?

No. A home equity loan is a single lump sum repaid on a fixed schedule, like a regular installment loan. A HELOC is a revolving credit line you can draw from and repay repeatedly during a draw period, usually with a variable rate.

What happens if I can't repay a home equity loan?

Because your home secures the loan, a lender can foreclose if you default, the same risk that applies to your main mortgage.

Does the "amount you can borrow" figure guarantee approval?

No. It is an estimate based on equity and the loan-to-value limit. Lenders also weigh your credit history and debt-to-income ratio, which can lower the approved amount.

What is combined loan-to-value (CLTV)?

It is your mortgage balance plus the new loan, divided by your home's value. Many lenders cap it around 80% to 85%.

Why is the APR higher than the interest rate?

Closing costs reduce the cash you effectively receive while you repay the same payments, so the real yearly cost is higher than the stated rate.

Does it matter if closing costs are deducted or paid upfront?

It changes the cash you receive at closing, but you repay the full loan either way. The APR captures the cost in both cases.
