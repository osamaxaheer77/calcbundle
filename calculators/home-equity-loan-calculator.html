A home equity loan is really a second mortgage

Borrowing against a home's equity means using the house itself as collateral a second time — the first lien is your primary mortgage, and a home equity loan sits behind it as a second lien. Because the lender's claim is subordinate to the original mortgage, the loan is priced (and limited) around how much of the home's value isn't already claimed by that first loan.

Why lenders cap the combined loan-to-value ratio

A lender extending a home equity loan is really asking: if this house had to be sold today, would there be enough left over after paying off both loans to make this a safe bet? Capping the combined balance at 80% (or whatever ratio a given lender uses) of the home's value keeps a cushion of built-in equity as protection against a market downturn or an unexpected sale at a discount.

A lump-sum home equity loan is a different tool than a HELOC. A home equity loan hands you the full amount upfront with a fixed rate and fixed payment — good for a one-time expense with a known cost. A HELOC works more like a credit line you draw against as needed, usually with a variable rate.

Why "deducted from loan" and "paid upfront" give different cash received

Either way, you're on the hook for repaying the full loan amount with interest — the only difference is whether the closing costs come out of the funds you receive at closing, or out of your pocket separately. The APR captures the real cost either way, since it accounts for the fact that the fees reduce what you effectively gained for the debt you took on.

Common questions

Is a home equity loan the same as a HELOC?

No. A home equity loan is a single lump sum repaid on a fixed schedule, like a regular installment loan. A HELOC is a revolving credit line you can draw from and repay repeatedly during a set draw period, typically with a variable rate.

What happens if I can't repay a home equity loan?

Because your home secures the loan, a lender can foreclose if you default — the same risk that applies to your primary mortgage. It's a real cost of the lower interest rates secured loans typically offer over unsecured borrowing.

Does the "amount you can borrow" figure guarantee approval?

No — it's an estimate based purely on equity and LTV. Lenders also weigh credit history and your existing debt-to-income ratio, either of which can reduce your final approved amount even if the equity math checks out.
