Why paying off a mortgage early actually saves money

When a lender applies an additional payment to your loan principal, the balance used to calculate future interest becomes smaller. That can reduce total interest and move your payoff date forward. Ask your servicer how to mark extra payments as principal-only, and check for any prepayment limits or fees. Savings depend on your rate, remaining balance, payment timing, and loan terms.

Lump sum, recurring extra, or biweekly — which actually helps more?

A lump sum reduces principal at one point in time, while monthly extras reduce it gradually. Which saves more depends on the amounts and payment dates, so compare scenarios in the calculator instead of assuming one method always wins. Paying half your usual monthly amount every two weeks totals 26 half-payments, or 13 monthly-payment equivalents a year, if the lender credits them as intended. Some servicers hold partial payments until a full installment is received or charge program fees.

Paying off a loan altogether today avoids every future interest charge for the remaining term — but it also uses cash that could otherwise be invested. Whether that trade is worth it depends on what your money could otherwise earn versus the loan's interest rate.

Example: what an extra $100 a month changes

Suppose you still owe $200,000 on a fixed-rate mortgage at 6% with 20 years remaining. The regular principal-and-interest payment is approximately $1,432.86 a month. Add $100 a month toward principal and you can compare the new payoff date and total interest in the results above. This example excludes taxes, insurance, fees and any prepayment penalty; actual lender schedules may vary slightly.

Why we ask for the remaining term two different ways

Some borrowers have their original loan paperwork handy and know exactly how many years are left. Others only have a recent statement, which lists the unpaid balance and payment but not a clean "years remaining" figure. Both are the same math underneath — a fixed balance being paid down at a fixed rate — so this calculator supports either starting point without forcing you to dig up documents you don't have.

Worked example: a useful baseline

With $12,000 remaining at 0% interest, $1,000 each month clears the balance in 12 months; adding $200 a month reduces the time to 10 months. Interest-bearing results vary.

Common questions

Does paying extra lower my required monthly payment?

No. Your scheduled payment stays the same; what changes is how quickly the balance disappears, which shortens the loan and cuts the total interest.

Is it ever better not to pay off a mortgage early?

Sometimes — if the loan's interest rate is lower than what you could reasonably earn investing that money elsewhere, or if you'd rather keep the cash as a safety net, paying the minimum and investing the difference can come out ahead financially, even though the mortgage costs more interest in isolation.

Should I check for a prepayment penalty first?

Yes — some loans charge a fee for paying off early or making large extra payments. It's worth confirming with your lender before committing to an aggressive payoff plan, since a penalty can offset some of the interest savings.

Why does the "payback altogether" option show less total interest than the original schedule?

Because it stops interest from accruing the moment you pay off the balance — every month of interest the original schedule would have charged from today forward simply never happens.

Is the biweekly estimate exact?

It's a close approximation. True biweekly billing accrues interest every two weeks instead of monthly, which shifts the numbers slightly. We model the main effect — 26 half-payments a year works out to 13 full monthly payments instead of 12 — which is what drives nearly all of the real-world savings.
