Compounding Frequency Rate Converter

Have an interest rate quoted with monthly compounding but need to compare it with a quarterly or annual rate? Convert between compounding frequencies while keeping the equivalent effective annual rate the same. For savings growth with deposits, use our Interest Calculator instead.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. The same interest rate grows money at different speeds depending on how often it compounds. 6% compounded monthly is equivalent to 6.16778% compounded annually, which is the effective annual rate, or APY.

Why the same rate can look different

A 6% rate compounded monthly does not grow money at exactly 6% a year. It grows slightly faster, because each month’s interest starts earning its own interest straight away instead of waiting for the year to end. This converter answers one question: what rate, at a different compounding frequency, produces exactly the same growth? It keeps the effective annual rate the same and restates it for the frequency you choose.

The two formulas

  • Effective annual rate = (1 + r ÷ n)n − 1, where r is the nominal rate and n the number of compounding periods a year. For continuous compounding it is er − 1.
  • Equivalent nominal rate for m periods a year = ((1 + effective)1/m − 1) × m. For continuous compounding it is ln(1 + effective).

Worked example with the default numbers

The default is 6% compounded monthly, converted to annual compounding. The monthly rate is 6% ÷ 12 = 0.5%, and (1.005)12 − 1 = 0.0616778, so the effective annual rate is 6.16778%. Expressed with annual compounding, that is the output rate. This figure is also called the APY.

APR versus APY

APR (annual percentage rate) is the nominal rate: the periodic rate multiplied by the number of periods in a year, ignoring compounding. APY (annual percentage yield) is the effective rate after compounding. APY is always equal to or higher than the APR for the same underlying rate, and the gap widens as compounding gets more frequent and as the rate rises.

What the difference is worth

On $10,000 at a 6% nominal rate for 10 years with no deposits, annual compounding grows to $17,908, monthly to $18,194 and continuous compounding to $18,221. The frequency matters far less than the rate or the time, which is why comparing rates correctly matters more than chasing the most frequent compounding.

Comparing offers fairly

Three savings accounts: Bank A pays 5.95% compounded daily, Bank B pays 6% compounded quarterly and Bank C pays 6.05% compounded annually. Their effective rates are 6.1301%, 6.1364% and 6.0500%. Bank B earns the most, even though its headline rate is not the highest. Always compare on the same basis, and prefer the APY when it is quoted.

Loans work the other way

For a loan, a higher effective rate costs you more. A credit card quoting 20% compounded monthly has an effective annual rate of 21.94%, which is what you really pay if the balance is carried for a year. The APR Calculator and Loan Calculator show how fees and compounding change the real cost.

Why continuous compounding exists

Continuous compounding is the mathematical limit of compounding infinitely often. It gives the highest possible effective rate for a given nominal rate and appears in some theoretical finance and a few real products. For 6% it is e0.06 − 1 = 6.1837%.

What this tool does not do

This calculator converts rates only. It does not project a balance over time or include deposits. For growth with regular deposits use the Investment Calculator or the Interest Calculator.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing an APR on one account with an APY on another.
  • Assuming that more frequent compounding is worth much more than a higher rate.
  • Entering the rate as a decimal such as 0.06 instead of 6.
  • Using the converter to project growth. It only converts the rate.

How to read your result

The large number is the equivalent rate at your output frequency. Below it you see the input rate, the effective annual rate and the output rate with its compounding. If you are comparing two accounts, set the output to Annually and compare the results directly.

Common questions

Why does "continuously" compounding exist as an option?

It is the mathematical limit of compounding infinitely often, used in some theoretical finance and a few real products. It produces the highest possible effective rate for a given nominal rate.

Which should I use to compare two accounts, APR or APY?

APY whenever it is available, because it already accounts for compounding and reflects what you would actually earn or pay over a year. Comparing raw APRs with different compounding frequencies can be misleading.

Is APY always higher than APR?

APY is equal to or higher than the APR for the same rate. They are equal only when interest compounds once a year.

Does this calculator show how my balance grows?

No. It only converts a rate between compounding frequencies. For growth over time with deposits, use the Investment Calculator or the Interest Calculator.

How much does compounding more often matter?

Less than the rate or the time. At 6%, moving from annual to monthly compounding adds about 0.17 percentage points, and going to continuous adds only a little more.

Does compounding frequency matter for loans?

Yes. A higher effective rate means more interest. A card at 20% compounded monthly costs about 21.94% a year if the balance is carried.
