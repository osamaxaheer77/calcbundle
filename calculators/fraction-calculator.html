What a fraction is actually measuring

Picture a chocolate bar scored into 8 equal squares. If you break off 3 of those squares, you've taken 3/8 of the bar — the bottom number (the denominator) records how many equal pieces the whole bar was cut into, and the top number (the numerator) records how many of those pieces you're talking about. Nothing about a fraction is about "3" or "8" on their own; it's the relationship between them that means something.

A fraction never changes value if you scale the numerator and denominator by the same amount — 3/8 and 6/16 are the same size piece of chocolate, just cut into finer crumbs.

Whole numbers, mixed numbers, and improper fractions

A mixed number like 2 3/4 means "two whole bars, plus three quarters of another." An improper fraction says the same thing differently: 11/4, where the numerator is allowed to be bigger than the denominator. Both describe the identical amount — our calculator above lets you type the whole-number part directly instead of forcing you to convert it yourself first, or use a separate tool just for mixed numbers.

Adding and subtracting: why the denominators have to match first

Think of denominators as different currencies. You can't add 3 euros and 2 dollars and get a meaningful total until you convert one into the other. Fractions work the same way: 1/3 and 1/4 are sliced differently, so before you can combine them you rescale both to a shared "currency" — the smallest denominator they both divide into evenly. Only once the pieces are the same size can the numerators simply be added or subtracted.

Multiplying and dividing: no matching required

Multiplication skips that step entirely — multiply the numerators, multiply the denominators, done. Division is really multiplication in disguise: dividing by 1/2 asks "how many halves fit into this?", and it turns out that's the same question as multiplying by the flipped fraction, 2/1. That's the entire reason the "flip and multiply" rule works — it isn't an arbitrary trick.

Why we simplify the answer

10/20 and 1/2 point to the exact same quantity, but 1/2 is the version everyone recognizes at a glance. Reducing a fraction means dividing the numerator and denominator by the largest number that divides evenly into both, until nothing but 1 divides them both. It doesn't change the value — it just states it in the plainest possible terms.

Common questions

Can a denominator be zero?

No. A denominator of zero would mean splitting something into zero pieces, which is undefined — there's no such quantity. Our calculator will flag this rather than guess at an answer.

How do negative fractions work?

Exactly like positive ones, except one part of the fraction carries a minus sign. You can put the sign on the whole number, the numerator, or think of it as applying to the fraction as a whole — the calculator normalizes it either way and always returns the denominator as positive.

Is the decimal version always exact?

Only when the denominator's prime factors are limited to 2s and 5s (like 4, 8, 20, 25). Otherwise the decimal repeats forever — 1/3 becomes 0.333..., which we round to a fixed number of places for display rather than showing an infinite string of digits.

Does simplifying ever change what a fraction means?

Never. Simplifying only rewrites the fraction in smaller numbers — the amount it represents is identical before and after, the same way "one dozen" and "twelve" refer to the same quantity.
