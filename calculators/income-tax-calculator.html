Deductions and Credits

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Federal income tax is worked out in layers: income minus deductions gives taxable income, which is taxed bracket by bracket, then credits and withholding are subtracted. A single filer with $80,000 of wages in 2025 has $64,250 of taxable income and owes about $9,049 in tax, an effective rate of 11.3%.

How federal income tax is calculated

The calculator follows the same steps as a U.S. federal return:

  • Total income: wages, self-employment income, interest, dividends, capital gains and other income.
  • Adjusted gross income (AGI): total income minus adjustments such as IRA contributions, student loan interest and half of self-employment tax.
  • Taxable income: AGI minus the larger of the standard deduction or your itemized deductions, plus the special deductions for tips, overtime, car loan interest and seniors where they apply.
  • Tax before credits: taxable income run through the progressive brackets. Qualified dividends and long-term gains use their own 0%, 15% and 20% rates.
  • Credits reduce the tax directly. Payments (withholding and estimated tax) are then subtracted to give a refund or a balance due.

“Progressive” means each rate applies only to the slice of income inside its bracket. Moving into a higher bracket never reduces your take-home pay, because only the extra dollars are taxed at the higher rate.

Example: self-employed income

A single filer with $60,000 of net business income and no wages. Self-employed people pay self-employment tax (12.4% Social Security up to a wage limit plus 2.9% Medicare) on 92.35% of net earnings, and can deduct half of it. Here that is $8,478 of self-employment tax. Income tax comes to $3,603 after the standard deduction and the 20% qualified business income deduction the engine applies to business income. With $6,000 of estimated tax already paid, the balance due is about $6,080.

This is why self-employed people make quarterly estimated payments: nothing is withheld from business income.

What the calculator includes and leaves out

  • Included: standard or itemized deductions (mortgage interest, charity, real estate tax, state and local tax with the cap), the child tax credit, credit for other dependents, child and dependent care credit, American Opportunity credit, earned income credit, taxation of Social Security, net investment income tax, additional Medicare tax and alternative minimum tax.
  • Not included: state income tax owed, foreign income, medical expense deductions, health savings accounts, energy credits and many special situations.
  • Some rules depend on facts the calculator cannot check, such as who qualifies as a dependent.

Refund or balance due: managing withholding

A large refund means you lent the government money all year; a large balance due can bring penalties. If the result is far from zero, adjust your withholding on Form W-4 with your employer, or change your estimated payments. Run the calculator again after any change in income, filing status, or family size.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing marginal and effective rates. A 22% bracket does not mean 22% of all income is taxed.
  • Forgetting that credits reduce tax while deductions only reduce taxable income.
  • Leaving out self-employment tax, which is separate from income tax.
  • Entering withholding for only one spouse on a joint return.
  • Using the wrong tax year or filing status.

How to read your result

Start with the balance: a negative number means you owe, a positive one means a refund. Then look at total tax and your effective rate to see what share of income goes to federal tax. The marginal rate shows the effect of extra income or deductions at the top of your income. Use the result to plan, and confirm any numbers on your return against IRS forms and instructions.

This calculator gives federal estimates for planning and is not tax advice. Tax rules change and many personal circumstances are not covered. Check with the IRS or a tax professional.

Common questions

What is the difference between a marginal and an effective tax rate?

The marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of income, such as 22%. The effective rate is your total tax divided by your total income, such as 11.3% in the example. Because of progressive brackets, the effective rate is always lower than the top marginal rate.

Does moving into a higher bracket reduce my take-home pay?

No. Only the income above the bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate, so extra income always leaves you with more money after tax.

What is the difference between a tax credit and a deduction?

A deduction lowers the income that is taxed. A credit reduces the tax itself, dollar for dollar. A $1,000 credit is worth more than a $1,000 deduction.

Why do self-employed people owe extra tax?

They pay both the employee and employer shares of Social Security and Medicare tax (self-employment tax), and nothing is withheld from business income, so estimated payments are needed.

Does this include state income tax?

No. It estimates U.S. federal income tax only. State and local income tax paid can affect itemized deductions, up to the cap, but the state tax itself is not calculated.

How accurate is the estimate?

It is designed for planning. The result can differ from your return because of situations the calculator does not model and facts it cannot check. Use IRS forms and instructions to prepare your return.
