Margin and markup

Margin and markup both describe profit, but against different bases. Margin is profit as a share of revenue, the selling price. Markup is profit as a share of cost. If an item costs $120 and sells for $160, the profit is $40, the margin is 25% and the markup is 33.3%. Markup is always larger than margin for the same sale, and the two are easy to confuse when you set prices.

A second example: a product costing $60 and selling for $100 earns $40 of gross profit. Gross margin is $40 ÷ $100 = 40% and markup is $40 ÷ $60 = 66.7%.

Tool 1: profit margin

Enter any two of cost, revenue, margin and profit and the calculator finds the rest, plus the markup. The defaults, cost $120 and revenue $160, give a profit of $40.00, a margin of 25.00% and a markup of 33.33%. The formulas are margin = profit ÷ revenue and markup = profit ÷ cost. To find the price for a target margin, enter the cost and the margin: price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). Margin must stay below 100%, since profit can never be the whole of the revenue.

Converting between margin and markup

Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin) and margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup).

Tool 2: margin in stock trading

In investing, margin means borrowing from a broker to buy more than your cash allows. The stock tool shows the cash you must hold: the value of the shares times the margin requirement. With the defaults, 100 shares at $18.30 are worth $1,830.00, a 30% requirement means you must hold $549.00 and you borrow $1,281.00. Under US rules brokers can lend up to half the purchase price of marginable stock, and firms must keep a minimum equity in the account, often higher than the minimum.

If the shares fall 20% to $1,464.00, the loan stays at $1,281.00, so your equity drops to $183.00, only 12% of the position. Falling below the broker’s maintenance level triggers a margin call: you must add money or securities, or the broker can sell your holdings without asking.

Tool 3: margin in currency trading

The currency tool shows the amount you need to hold for a leveraged position. With an exchange rate of 1.3, 100 units and a 20:1 margin ratio, the position is worth $130.00 and the margin required is $6.50, which is 5% of the position. The margin ratio, such as 20:1, says how much you can control for each unit of your own money. US rules have set minimum deposits of 2% for major currency pairs and 5% for others, and the calculator offers ratios up to 50:1.

Risks of trading on margin

  • You can lose more than you put in.
  • Brokers set their own margin rules and can change them, sometimes quickly.
  • Interest on the borrowed money adds to the cost.
  • A margin call can force you to sell at a bad time.

These calculators do simple arithmetic and are not investment advice.

Common mistakes

  • Using markup and margin interchangeably when setting prices.
  • Forgetting that margin is based on revenue, so a 50% margin is a 100% markup.
  • Ignoring discounts when estimating profit.
  • Treating a margin requirement as the maximum you can lose.

Common questions

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit divided by revenue, while markup is profit divided by cost. For the same sale, the markup is always bigger than the margin. A 25% margin is the same as a 33.3% markup.

How do I price an item for a target margin?

Divide your cost by one minus the margin as a decimal. To reach a 25% margin on a cost of 120, divide 120 by 0.75 to get a price of 160. Enter the cost and the margin to do this.

Why can margin not be 100% or more?

Margin is the share of revenue that is profit. If the margin were 100%, the cost would be zero, and a margin above 100% is impossible.

How is the stock margin amount worked out?

Multiply the share price by the number of shares to get the position value, then multiply by the margin requirement as a percentage. The result is the cash you must hold, and the rest is borrowed from the broker.

What does a 20:1 margin ratio mean?

You can control 20 units of currency for every 1 unit of your own money, so the margin required is 5% of the position.

What is a margin call?

If your account equity falls below the broker's maintenance level, you must add cash or securities. If you do not, the broker can sell your holdings without your consent.
