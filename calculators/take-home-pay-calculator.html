Take-Home-Paycheck Calculator

See what actually lands in your account after taxes and deductions, and get the numbers to fill in steps 3 and 4 of your W-4. Mainly for U.S. employees.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Take-home pay is gross pay minus federal income tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), state and city tax and pre-tax deductions. On $80,000 in 2026 with $6,000 of pre-tax deductions, a single employee takes home about $60,430 a year, or $2,324.23 per bi-weekly paycheck.

How your paycheck is worked out

Your yearly pay is split into paychecks by how often you are paid. From each one the calculator takes out federal income tax, Social Security tax, Medicare tax, any state and city tax, and the pre-tax deductions you choose. What is left is your take-home (net) pay.

  • Federal income tax is estimated for the whole year from your filing status, dependents, deductions and credits, then spread evenly over your paychecks.
  • Social Security tax is 6.2% of pay up to a yearly wage limit ($184,500 in 2026).
  • Medicare tax is 1.45% of all pay, with an additional 0.9% on high earners.
  • State and city tax are flat percentages that you enter, because rules vary widely.
  • Pre-tax deductions such as 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums and HSA deposits come out before income tax.

Payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare) apply to your gross pay and are not lowered by 401(k) contributions, which is why a retirement contribution reduces income tax but not those two taxes.

Worked example with the default numbers

A single employee in 2026 earning $80,000 a year, paid bi-weekly, with $6,000 a year in pre-tax deductions:

ItemPer yearPer bi-weekly paycheck
Gross pay$80,000$3,076.92
Federal income tax$7,450$286.54
Social Security tax (6.2%)$4,960$190.77
Medicare tax (1.45%)$1,160$44.62
State and city tax$0$0.00
Pre-tax deductions$6,000$230.77
Take-home pay$60,430$2,324.23

Taxable income is 80,000 − 6,000 − the standard deduction of $16,100 = $57,900, which puts the top of the income in the 22% bracket. The federal tax of $7,450 is an effective 9.3% of gross pay.

Pay frequency changes the cheque, not the year

The same $60,430 of yearly take-home pay arrives in different sized paychecks:

How often you are paidTake-home pay per paycheck
Weekly (52)$1,162.12
Bi-weekly (26)$2,324.23
Semi-monthly (24)$2,517.92
Monthly (12)$5,035.83

Bi-weekly pay has 26 cheques a year, so in two months of each year you receive three. Semi-monthly pay has 24 cheques, always two a month.

Pre-tax contributions cost less than they look

Money that goes into a traditional 401(k) or similar plan is taken out before income tax. On the default example, a $6,000 contribution cuts federal tax by about $1,320 and a $12,000 contribution cuts it by about $2,640, so your cheque shrinks by far less than the contribution.

Example: a family

A married couple filing jointly in 2026, one earner at $90,000 with $6,000 of pre-tax deductions, two children under 17 and a 4% state tax. Federal tax is only $1,320 after the child tax credit, Social Security is $5,580, Medicare $1,305 and state tax $3,600. Take-home pay is $72,195 a year, or $2,776.73 per bi-weekly paycheck.

Filling in your W-4

The W-4 tells your employer how much federal tax to withhold. Step 3 is where you claim the child tax credit and credit for other dependents. Step 4 adjusts for other income, extra deductions and any extra withholding you want, which matters most when you have a second job or a working spouse. The calculator shows suggested values for Steps 3 and 4, and the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator is the official tool for checking them.

What the estimate leaves out

  • Union dues, wage garnishments, and benefits not covered by the options above.
  • Differences between your employer's withholding tables and a yearly estimate, so check your first paychecks.
  • State and local taxes beyond a flat percentage, and state disability or family leave contributions.
  • Bonuses, which are often withheld at a flat rate.

Common mistakes

  • Entering take-home pay instead of gross pay.
  • Forgetting that 401(k) contributions do not reduce Social Security and Medicare tax.
  • Using the wrong pay frequency, which changes the per-cheque amount.
  • Leaving out a spouse's income or a second job and then being under-withheld.
  • Expecting the estimate to match a payslip to the dollar.

How to read your result

The large figure is your net pay for one paycheck, and the table shows where the rest goes. Compare it with your first real payslip. If your withholding is too low or too high, adjust your W-4 and rerun the numbers. For the full-year tax picture, use the Income Tax Calculator.

Common questions

How is federal income tax estimated per paycheck?

The calculator works out your estimated federal income tax for the whole year, including deductions and credits, and divides it evenly across your paychecks. Your employer's payroll system uses its own withholding tables, so your actual withholding can differ a little.

Why does take-home pay depend on how often I am paid?

The yearly total is the same, but it is split into a different number of paychecks. Bi-weekly pay has 26 cheques a year and semi-monthly pay has 24, so each bi-weekly cheque is smaller. Some years a bi-weekly schedule gives you a 27th cheque.

What counts as a pre-tax deduction?

Common ones are traditional 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums and health savings account deposits. They are taken out before income tax, so they lower your tax bill, although 401(k) contributions do not reduce Social Security and Medicare tax.

Why is my payslip different from the estimate?

Employers use withholding tables, not a yearly calculation, and your payslip may include dues, garnishments, or benefits not covered here. Treat the result as a close estimate.

Does the calculator include state tax?

Yes, as a flat percentage you enter, plus an optional city percentage. States with no income tax can be entered as 0.

How do I use the W-4 values?

Step 3 is the dependent credit amount, Step 4(a) other income, Step 4(b) extra deductions and Step 4(c) extra withholding per paycheck. They are suggestions for the form; the IRS withholding estimator can double-check them.

Sources: IRS: Topic no. 751, Social Security and Medicare withholding rates; IRS: Tax Withholding Estimator
