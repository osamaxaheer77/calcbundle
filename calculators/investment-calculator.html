Quick answer. An investment plan connects a starting amount, regular contributions, a return rate and time. $20,000 plus $1,000 a month at 6% for 10 years grows to $198,290, and the calculator can solve backwards for the contribution, rate, starting amount or time needed to reach a goal.

The four numbers behind every investment plan

Every plan is a relationship between four things: a starting amount, regular contributions, a return rate and time, which together give an end amount. Change any one and the end balance shifts, sometimes dramatically. This calculator can solve for any one of the five if you give it the other four, so it answers “how much will I have?” as well as “how much do I need to contribute?” and “what return do I need?”.

Worked example with the default numbers

Start with $20,000, add $1,000 at the end of each month for 10 years and earn 6% a year (compounded annually). The end balance is $198,290. You contributed $120,000 on top of the starting amount, so about $58,290 is interest. If the contributions are made at the beginning of each month instead, the balance is $199,081, about $791 more, because each deposit earns one extra month.

The return-rate answer is shown as an effective annual rate. The table shows how big the gap between a hope and a plan can be: reaching $1,000,000 in 10 years with these inputs would need an unrealistic return, while giving it 29 years makes it reachable.

Why the starting amount and the timing both matter

A larger starting amount works for you the whole time. In the default example, the $20,000 grows to about $35,817 by itself over 10 years at 6%, which is 18% of the ending balance. Regular contributions add to that, and because each contribution has less time to grow than the one before it, early deposits are worth the most. That is why starting sooner with a small amount often beats starting later with a larger one.

What matters more than compounding frequency

Monthly versus daily compounding at the same stated rate makes a small difference. The bigger levers are the rate itself, how long the money compounds and how much you add. Contributing at the beginning of each period gives every deposit one extra period of growth, which adds up over long horizons.

Choosing a return rate

The calculator does not predict returns. It does the arithmetic for the rate you enter. Many long-term investors start from a long-run historical average for the type of investment and then test a lower rate as well, because returns vary from year to year and are never guaranteed. Test several rates to see how sensitive your goal is.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming a high return will definitely happen. Check the result at a lower rate.
  • Ignoring inflation and tax, which lower the real value of the end balance.
  • Entering the annual contribution when you meant a monthly one (check the Month/Year switch).
  • Treating the solved return rate as a promise. It tells you what the goal requires, not what you will get.
  • Starting late and expecting contributions alone to catch up.

How to read your result

Look at how much of the end balance is your own money and how much is growth. If a goal needs an unrealistic return or contribution, change the time or the target instead. For a view that includes taxes and inflation, use the Interest Calculator or the Savings Calculator.

Common questions

What return rate should I assume?

The calculator does not predict future returns. Many long-term investors start with a long-run historical average for the asset class and test a lower rate as well, remembering that past returns do not guarantee future ones.

Why does the Return Rate mode give an annual rate?

It is reported as the effective annual rate regardless of how often interest compounds, so that different results can be compared on the same basis.

What is the difference between contributing at the beginning and the end?

A contribution made at the beginning of each period earns one extra period of growth. Over many deposits that gives a noticeably larger balance.

Can I solve for how long it will take?

Yes. Choose the Investment Length option, enter the target, starting amount, contribution and rate, and the calculator tells you how many years it takes.

Does compounding frequency matter much?

A little. More frequent compounding helps slightly, but the rate, the time and the size of your contributions matter much more.

Is the result guaranteed?

No. It is a projection from the rate you enter. Actual investment returns vary and can be negative.
