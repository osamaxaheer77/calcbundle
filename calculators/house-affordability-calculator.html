How lenders decide what you can borrow

Affordability depends on your monthly payment compared with your income, not on the size of the house. Lenders measure this with the debt-to-income (DTI) ratio: the share of gross monthly income that goes to debt payments, which is monthly debt divided by gross monthly income. Two ratios are used:

  • Front-end ratio: housing costs only (loan payment, property tax, insurance, HOA).
  • Back-end ratio: housing costs plus every other recurring debt, such as car loans, student loans and credit cards.

The well-known 28/36 rule says housing should be no more than 28% of gross income and total debt no more than 36%. It is a planning guideline, not a universal lending limit.

Worked example with the default numbers

A household earning $120,000 a year with no other debts, a 30-year loan at 6.5%, 20% down, property tax of 1.5% and insurance of 0.5% of the price a year (the calculator’s starting values), using the conventional 28/36 rule. Monthly income is $10,000, so the front-end limit is $2,800 and the back-end limit is $3,600. The tighter one, $2,800 a month, becomes the housing budget.

The calculator then solves for the price where principal and interest, tax and insurance together equal that budget: price ≈ $416,468. That means a $83,294 down payment, a $333,174 loan, principal and interest of $2,105.89, tax of $520.58 and insurance of $173.53 a month.

Example: income and other debts

For $90,000 of income and $500 a month of other debts, monthly income is $7,500. 28% allows $2,100 for housing and 36% minus the debts allows $2,200. The lower number, $2,100, is the housing budget, which supports a home of about $312,351 with the same assumptions.

Starting from a monthly budget instead

The second tool skips income and debts. If you know what you are comfortable spending, enter it and the calculator works out the price. Affording a house is more than qualifying

  • A lender may approve more than you are comfortable paying. Leave room for savings, travel and surprises.
  • Budget for maintenance (often about 1% to 2% of the home value a year), utilities and furnishing.
  • Keep an emergency fund after the down payment and closing costs.
  • Think about job security and expected changes in income or family size.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the result as a pre-approval or a lender offer.
  • Leaving out other debts such as student loans or credit cards.
  • Forgetting property tax, insurance and HOA fees, which can change the price by tens of thousands of dollars.
  • Using an interest rate from an advertisement instead of a real quote.
  • Stretching to the maximum allowed by FHA or VA ratios without a cushion.

How to read your result

The headline price is a budgeting scenario, not a promise. Change the rate, down payment and taxes to see how fragile it is, then compare the monthly cost with your actual spending. Common questions

What is the 28/36 rule?

It is a planning guideline: spend no more than 28% of gross monthly income on housing and no more than 36% on all debt together. Actual lender limits depend on the loan program and your finances.

Why do FHA and VA give a different result?

The calculator uses different ratio presets: 31% and 43% for FHA, and 41% total for VA. These are simplified assumptions. Real approvals use more factors, including compensating strengths and, for VA, residual income.

Why does the calculator ask for taxes, insurance and HOA?

They are part of your monthly cost, so they reduce the loan payment you can afford. Higher taxes or fees mean a lower home price at the same budget.

Does the result mean I will be approved?

No. It is a budgeting scenario. A lender looks at your credit, income, reserves and other factors before approving a loan.

How do other debts change the result?

They count toward the back-end ratio. Small debts may not matter at first, but once the back-end limit is the tighter one, each extra dollar of debt reduces your housing budget.

Should I buy the most expensive home I can afford?

Not necessarily. Leave room in your budget for savings, repairs and changes in income. A lender’s maximum is not a recommended spending target.
