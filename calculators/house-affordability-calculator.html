How lenders decide what you can borrow

Affordability isn't really about the size of the house — it's about the size of the monthly payment relative to your income. Lenders formalize this with a debt-to-income (DTI) ratio: the share of your gross monthly income that goes toward debt. A front-end ratio covers housing costs alone (loan payment, property tax, HOA, insurance); a back-end ratio adds in every other recurring debt — car loans, student loans, credit cards. The familiar 28/36 rule is a planning guideline, not a universal lending limit. Actual qualifying ratios depend on the loan program, lender, borrower profile, and underwriting.

Why the loan type changes the math

This calculator provides conventional 28/36, FHA 31/43, and VA 41% debt-to-income presets for comparison. They are simplified assumptions, not universal approval cutoffs. FHA and VA underwriting can use additional factors, compensating strengths, and program-specific requirements. In particular, VA qualification also considers residual income. Confirm eligibility and current requirements with a lender instead of treating the result as a preapproval.

This calculator works backward from an estimated monthly housing limit to an approximate home price. The result is a budgeting scenario—not a lender offer or a guarantee that you can qualify for that amount.

Why two different starting points

Not everyone thinks in DTI ratios. Some people simply know what they're comfortable spending each month and want to see what that translates to in house price — which is exactly what the fixed-budget version of this calculator does, skipping the income and debt inputs entirely.

Example: estimate a housing budget from your income

Suppose your gross household income is $90,000 a year and you pay $500 per month toward other debts. That is $7,500 in gross monthly income. Using the conventional 28/36 planning preset, 28% of income allows $2,100 for housing, while 36% minus $500 in other debt allows $2,200. The stricter amount is $2,100 a month for the calculator’s housing-cost estimate. This is not a recommended spending target or a mortgage approval.

The estimated home price still depends on your down payment, interest rate, loan term, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and any HOA charges. If you are shopping for a specific property, compare its potential monthly payment with the Mortgage Calculator. To see whether the payment leaves enough room for other living costs, compare the result with your personal budget too.

What this estimate does not include

A loan-qualification estimate is different from a comfortable household budget. This tool separately estimates home maintenance and closing costs using simplified percentages, but actual expenses vary. Mortgage insurance, changing taxes and insurance premiums, repairs, utilities, and lender fees may affect the real total. Use the Down Payment Calculator to explore upfront cash needs and the Rent vs. Buy Calculator when comparing long-term housing choices.

Common questions

Why is the "estimated maintenance" cost included if it doesn't count toward my loan approval?

Lenders don't factor ongoing maintenance into their DTI math, but it's still a real cost of owning a home — repairs, upkeep, appliance replacement. We include a rough 1.5%-of-price estimate so the "total monthly cost" figure reflects what you'd actually pay, not just what a lender would approve.

Does a bigger down payment let me afford a more expensive house?

A larger down payment lowers the amount you need to borrow for the same home price. It may also affect loan terms or mortgage-insurance requirements, although a better interest rate is not guaranteed. Remember to keep enough cash for closing costs and reserves.

Why does raising my monthly debt lower my affordable house price?

Because the selected back-end DTI assumption covers housing costs plus other recurring debts. Higher existing monthly payments leave less room in the estimated housing budget. Actual lender requirements can differ.

Is the VA loan estimate exact?

It uses a simplified 41% DTI assumption, but VA underwriting also considers residual income and other eligibility factors. The estimate does not fully model fees or individual lender requirements. Confirm details with a VA-approved lender.
