BTU Calculator

Use room size and relevant conditions to estimate an initial heating or cooling capacity. Actual equipment sizing also depends on insulation, windows, climate and a professional load calculation.

BTU for a Room

Describe the room to estimate the cooling or heating capacity it needs.

BTU for a Whole House

Heat loss or gain through the walls and ceiling for a given temperature difference between inside and outside.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. A 200 sq ft bedroom with an 8 ft ceiling needs about 6,500 BTU per hour (1,905 watts): 20 BTU per square foot plus a 2,500 base. A 20 by 15 ft house with a 45°F difference needs 15,480 BTU per hour on average insulation.

What a BTU is

A BTU, or British thermal unit, is a measure of heat. One BTU is about the heat needed to warm one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. Air conditioners and heaters are rated in BTU per hour, which says how much heat they can move in an hour. One BTU per hour is about 0.293 watt, and 12,000 BTU per hour is called one ton of cooling.

What the calculator does

The page has two estimators. The room estimate works from the floor area, ceiling height, number of people, type of room, insulation, sun and climate. The whole-house estimate adds up the wall and ceiling surface and multiplies by the temperature difference and an insulation factor. Each shows the answer in BTU per hour, in watts and in tons.

Worked example: the room estimate

The room tool opens on a 200 square foot bedroom with an 8 foot ceiling, 2 people, and average insulation, sun and climate. The estimate is 20 BTU per hour for every square foot plus a base of 2,500: 20 × 200 + 2,500 = 6,500 BTU per hour. That is 1,905 watts, or 0.54 tons. Ceilings of 8 feet or less add nothing, two people add nothing, and the average settings multiply by 1.

A fuller example

A 300 square foot living room with a 9 foot ceiling and 4 people, very sunny and in a hot climate. The base is 20 × 300 + 2,500 = 8,500, and the taller ceiling adds 1,000, making 9,500. The sun (× 1.1) and the hot climate (× 1.2) take it to 12,540. The two extra people add 1,200 and the living room adds 1,000, for 14,740 BTU per hour, about 4,320 watts. A 150 square foot kitchen with an 8 foot ceiling, in contrast, comes to (3,000 + 2,500) + 4,000 = 9,500.

Ceiling height

Ceilings above 8 feet add extra load. Up to 10 feet it adds 1,000 BTU per hour for each extra foot. Above 10 feet the add-on is steeper, and in larger rooms it is scaled up as well (by up to 3 times at very large areas). The table shows the add-on in BTU per hour.

Worked example: the whole-house estimate

The house tool opens on a house 20 feet wide and 15 feet long with an 8 foot ceiling, a 45°F temperature difference and average insulation. The surface is the floor or ceiling area (20 × 15 = 300) plus the four walls (2 × (20 + 15) × 8 = 560), which is 860 square feet. The load is the temperature difference times the insulation factor times the surface: 45 × 0.40 × 860 = 15,480 BTU per hour, which is 4,537 watts or 1.29 tons. Use the difference between the hottest or coldest day and the temperature you want inside. If you enter the difference in °C, the tool multiplies it by 1.8.

Choosing a unit

A unit that is too small runs all day and never catches up. A unit that is too large switches on and off too often, which wastes power and removes less moisture, leaving the room cool but damp. Round the estimate up to the nearest size sold, and for a whole house have an installer carry out a full load calculation, since windows, doors and air leaks matter a great deal.

What it costs to run

An air conditioner’s efficiency is given as EER, the BTU per hour divided by the watts it draws. The default 6,500 BTU per hour unit at an EER of 10 draws about 650 W, which is 5.2 kWh over an 8-hour day of continuous running. At an EER of 12 it draws about 542 W. Use the Electricity Calculator with your own rate to turn that into a cost.

Common mistakes

  • Using floor area alone. Ceilings, sun, people and insulation all change the load.
  • Sizing for the coldest or hottest day of the century instead of a typical peak.
  • Rounding down to a smaller unit to save money. It will run constantly and still fall short.
  • Using the room method for an open-plan floor or whole house. Use the house method, or ask an installer.
  • Forgetting that a kitchen adds a lot of heat from cooking.

How to read your result

The large number is the capacity in BTU per hour, with the same figure in watts and, for the house method, in tons. Treat it as a starting point, then round up to a size that is sold.

These are rough estimates for planning. For a whole house, ask an installer for a full load calculation.

Common questions

How many BTU do I need per square foot?

A common rule of thumb is about 20 BTU per hour for each square foot of floor space. This calculator adds a base of 2,500 and adjustments for the ceiling, insulation, sun, climate, people and the type of room.

How many BTU is one ton?

One ton of cooling is 12,000 BTU per hour, which is about 3,517 watts.

What happens if the unit is too big?

It cools the room quickly and switches off before it has removed much moisture, so the room can feel cool but damp. It also cycles on and off, which wastes power.

How is the whole-house estimate worked out?

It adds up the wall and ceiling surface, then multiplies by the temperature difference and a factor for insulation: 0.24 for good, 0.40 for average and 0.84 for poor. A 20 by 15 foot house with 8 foot ceilings and a 45 degree difference comes to 15,480 BTU per hour.

Do I use the room or the house method?

Use the room method for one room, such as a bedroom or kitchen. Use the house method for a whole house, and ask an installer for a full load calculation before buying central equipment.

What does a kitchen add?

The calculator adds 4,000 BTU per hour for a kitchen because cooking produces a lot of heat. A living room adds 1,000.
