Wind Chill Calculator

Calculate the wind chill temperature — how cold it actually feels — from wind speed and air temperature.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Wind chill is how cold the air feels on exposed skin. At 10°F with a 15 mph wind, the National Weather Service formula gives −6.6°F (−21.4°C). It applies at or below 50°F with wind above 3 mph.

The National Weather Service formula

The calculator uses the North American wind chill formula adopted by the US National Weather Service and Environment Canada: WC = 35.74 + 0.6215T − 35.75V0.16 + 0.4275TV0.16, where T is the air temperature in °F and V is the wind speed in mph. The index is defined only for temperatures at or below 50°F and wind speeds above 3 mph, so outside that range the calculator shows a message instead of a number.

Worked example with the default numbers

At an air temperature of 10°F with a 15 mph wind: 150.16 = 1.542, so WC = 35.74 + 6.215 − 55.13 + 6.59 = −6.6°F, which is −21.4°C or 251.7 K. It feels about 16.6 degrees colder than the thermometer says. Wind chill is the same whatever unit you enter: 15 mph is 24.1 km/h, 6.7 m/s or 13.0 knots.

Why wind makes it feel colder

Moving air strips away the thin layer of warmed air your body keeps near the skin, speeding up heat loss through convection. Faster wind means faster heat loss, which your body perceives as a lower temperature even though the air has not changed. Wind chill describes the effect on exposed skin. It does not lower the actual temperature of objects such as water pipes or cars, which can cool only down to the air temperature.

What the risk message means

  • Wind chill at or below −19°F: frostbite possible in as little as 30 minutes on exposed skin.
  • Wind chill at or below 0°F: frostbite risk with extended exposure; cover exposed skin.
  • Above 0°F: low frostbite risk, but dress warmly for comfort.

These thresholds follow the National Weather Service chart. Frostbite times shrink as it gets colder, and sooner for children, older adults and people with poor circulation.

Sun, humidity and the limits of the formula

  • Bright sunshine can raise the wind chill temperature by 10 to 18°F, according to the National Weather Service.
  • Humidity does not enter the formula: wind chill depends only on temperature and wind speed. Humidity matters in warm weather through the heat index.
  • Different countries use slightly different formulas, so figures can differ a little between weather services.
  • The formula is not valid above 50°F or for winds under 3 mph.

Alerts and warnings

Weather services use the wind chill, together with local conditions, to decide when to issue cold weather advisories or extreme cold warnings. The thresholds differ from region to region because people in colder climates are used to it, so check the alerts for your own area as well as the number from this calculator. Wind chill values in forecasts are for exposed skin at about face height, not for a person who is dressed for the cold.

Planning for work and play outdoors

Use the chart to plan: a morning run, a walk to school or a day on the slopes can feel very different at 20°F with a 5 mph breeze (13°F) than with a 30 mph wind (1°F). If the number is close to a frostbite threshold, shorten the time outside, add a wind-proof layer, or move the activity indoors.

Cold-weather tips

  • Dress in layers, with a wind-proof outer layer.
  • Cover ears, hands, face and neck, and keep skin dry.
  • Limit time outside when the wind chill is below 0°F, and watch for numbness or pale skin.
  • Check on neighbours and bring pets in.

Common mistakes

  • Using the calculator above 50°F. For hot weather use the Heat Index Calculator.
  • Entering the temperature in the wrong unit. Check the selector.
  • Expecting wind chill to freeze things that are above freezing.
  • Ignoring wet clothing, which makes any wind chill more dangerous.

How to read your result

The large number is the “feels like” temperature in °F, with the same value in °C and kelvin below it and a frostbite note. Compare it with the air temperature to see the wind effect, and use the chart above to see how it changes if the wind picks up.

Common questions

Why doesn't this work above 50°F?

The formula was derived from cold-weather skin heat-loss models and is not defined at warmer temperatures. For hot, humid weather, see the Heat Index Calculator.

Does humidity affect wind chill?

No. Wind chill depends only on temperature and wind speed. Humidity's effect on perceived temperature is captured by the heat index, which is used in warm weather.

What does the frostbite warning mean?

At a wind chill of about -19°F or lower, exposed skin can freeze in about 30 minutes. At 0°F or lower the risk grows with longer exposure.

Does wind chill make pipes or cars colder than the air?

No. Wind chill describes the cooling effect on exposed skin. Objects only cool to the actual air temperature.

Why does the calculator reject low wind speeds?

The formula is defined only for winds above 3 mph. Below that, there is no meaningful wind chill.

Can sunshine change the wind chill?

Yes. The National Weather Service says bright sunshine may raise the effective wind chill by 10 to 18 degrees F.
