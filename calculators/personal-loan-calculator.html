Personal Loan Calculator

Quick answer. A $20,000 personal loan at 10% over 5 years costs $424.94 a month and $5,496 in total interest. A 5% origination fee taken from the proceeds lifts the APR to 12.24%.

How a personal loan payment is worked out

A personal loan is repaid in equal monthly payments. Each month interest is charged on the remaining balance at one twelfth of the yearly rate, and the rest of the payment reduces the balance. The calculator finds the payment that brings the balance to exactly zero after the last month and builds a schedule with a date for each payment, shown by year or by month.

Worked example with the default numbers

Borrow $20,000 at 10% for 5 years (60 payments). The monthly payment is $424.94, you repay $25,496.45 in total, and $5,496.45 of that is interest. The first payment includes $166.67 of interest (10% ÷ 12 × $20,000) and $258.27 of principal; the share going to principal grows each month.

Fees, insurance and the real cost

The interest rate alone does not show what a loan costs. Tick Add fee and insurance to include an origination fee (a percentage of the loan or a fixed amount) and monthly insurance. The fee can be deducted from the loan, so you borrow $20,000 but receive less cash, or paid upfront from your own money.

With a 5% fee ($1,000) deducted from the default loan you receive $19,000 in cash but still repay $424.94 a month for 60 months. The cost of the loan, interest plus fee, is $6,496 and the APR is 12.239% instead of the 10% rate. Adding $15 a month of insurance raises the cost of the loan to $7,396 and the APR to 13.781%.

Comparing two loan offers by APR

Offer A is a $20,000 loan at 9% with an 8% fee ($1,600) taken from the proceeds. Offer B is the same loan at 11% with no fee. Offer A has the lower rate, but its payment is $415.17 and the cost of the loan is $6,510 with an APR of 12.63%. Offer B has a payment of $434.85, costs $6,091 and has an APR of 11.00%. Offer B is cheaper overall, which the rate alone would not show.

How the APR is calculated here

The APR is the yearly rate (monthly rate times 12) at which your payments, including any monthly insurance, repay the cash you actually receive after the origination fee. If there is no fee or insurance, the APR equals the interest rate.

Payoff date

The first payment is due at the end of the start month you choose, and the payoff date is the month after the last payment. A 60-payment loan that starts in January 2027 has its last payment in December 2031 and a payoff date of January 2032.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing loans by interest rate when the fees differ.
  • Choosing the longest term only for the lower payment.
  • Forgetting that an origination fee deducted from the loan reduces the cash you receive.
  • Not checking the lender’s rules on prepayment or late fees.

How to read your result

Look at the payment you can afford, then at the total interest and cost of the loan. If you see fees, compare offers on APR. Try a shorter term and see whether the payment is still comfortable: it nearly always saves interest. Use the schedule to see how the balance falls over time.

This calculator gives estimates. A lender may round differently or charge other fees, so use your loan agreement for exact figures. It is not financial advice.

Common questions

What is an origination fee?

An origination fee is a charge for processing a loan, usually a percentage of the amount. Lenders often deduct it from the loan, so you receive less cash than you borrow but still repay the full amount.

What is the difference between the interest rate and the APR?

The interest rate is the cost of borrowing the balance. The APR also counts fees and insurance, spreading them over the life of the loan, so it shows the true yearly cost. When there are no fees or insurance, the two are the same.

Why does the cash received differ from the loan amount?

If the origination fee is deducted from the loan, the lender pays out the loan amount minus the fee. You still owe the full loan amount, and interest is charged on that full amount. If you pay the fee separately, you receive the full loan amount.

How is the payoff date worked out?

The first payment is due at the end of the start month, and the loan is paid off after the last payment. The payoff date is the month after the last payment month, counted from the start date you enter.

Does a shorter loan term save money?

Yes. A shorter term means a higher monthly payment but less time for interest to build, so total interest is lower. Try different terms in the calculator to see the trade-off.

Is the first payment mostly interest?

Early payments are mostly interest because the balance is highest, and later payments are mostly principal. The schedule under the result shows the split for every payment.
