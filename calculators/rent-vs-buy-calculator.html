Quick answer. Buying costs more per month for a short stay because of the down payment and fees, and less for a long stay as equity builds. With the default numbers (a $500,000 home against $3,000 rent) buying becomes cheaper if you stay 9 years or longer.

Why this is not just rent against a mortgage payment

Comparing a rent cheque with a mortgage payment leaves out most of the money. Buying ties up a down payment and closing costs that a renter could invest instead. It also adds property tax, insurance, maintenance and eventually selling costs. On the other side, a buyer builds equity and may gain from home price growth, while rent is gone for good. The calculator puts all of that into one number: the average monthly cost of each option for every length of stay from 1 to 30 years.

How the comparison is built

  • Buying cost = down payment + closing costs + mortgage payments + property tax + insurance + HOA + maintenance, minus what you would get back when you sell (home value, less selling costs, less the remaining loan).
  • Renting cost = upfront cost + rent + renter’s insurance, minus the investment growth you earn on the money you did not spend on a down payment and closing costs.
  • Rent, property tax and other costs rise each year at the rates you enter, and the home grows at the appreciation rate.
  • Each total is divided by the months of the stay to give the average monthly cost.

What the calculator does not include

  • Tax effects such as the mortgage interest deduction or property tax deductions, which depend on whether you itemize. For high earners who itemize, including them usually favours buying.
  • Mortgage insurance (PMI) for down payments under 20%.
  • Moving costs, renovations, and the value of stability or flexibility.
  • Changes in your income, family or job location.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing the first-year payment only. The deal is decided over many years.
  • Using an optimistic appreciation rate. Homes do not always rise 3% a year.
  • Leaving out maintenance, which is commonly budgeted at 1% to 2% of the home value a year.
  • Forgetting that selling costs are paid at the end, and can be 6% to 8% of the sale price.
  • Ignoring how long you will realistically stay.

How to read your result

The headline tells you the stay length at which buying becomes cheaper. Compare it with how long you expect to stay and treat it as a range, not a point: change rent, appreciation and returns to see how fragile it is. If the answer flips with small changes, other factors such as flexibility and personal preference matter more than the numbers. Common questions

Why does a higher investment return favour renting?

The money you would use for a down payment and closing costs can be invested if you rent. A higher return makes that alternative more valuable, which pushes the break-even point later or removes it.

Why does the break-even point change so much?

Rent, appreciation and returns each have a large effect. Test several values to see the range, rather than relying on a single result.

Does the calculator include tax deductions?

No. Mortgage interest and property tax deductions depend on your tax situation and whether you itemize. Including them would usually favour buying for people in higher tax brackets who itemize.

What if I will not stay long?

Renting usually wins for stays of a few years, because the up-front and selling costs of buying are spread over few months.

How much should I assume for maintenance?

A common planning figure is 1% to 2% of the home value per year. Older homes can need more. The default in the calculator is 1.5%.

Does paying off the mortgage change the result?

Yes, over very long stays. After the loan ends, the buyer's monthly cost drops to tax, insurance and maintenance, while rent keeps rising.
