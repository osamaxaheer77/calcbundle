Protein Calculator

Find the daily protein recommended for your body weight and activity level, using guidelines from health authorities.

Exercise means 15 to 30 minutes of raised heart rate activity. Intense exercise is 45 to 120 minutes, and very intense exercise is 2 hours or more.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Most adults need roughly 0.8 to 1.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day, depending on activity. A lightly active 60 kg person gets 50 g from the WHO safe level, 60 to 108 g from the Academy range for active people and 55 to 193 g from the CDC percentage range.

Why protein matters

Protein is the nutrient your body uses to build and repair muscle, skin, bones and enzymes and to make hormones. Adults need it every day, because the body does not store protein the way it stores fat. Children, people who train hard, older adults and people who are pregnant or breastfeeding usually need more than the basic recommendation. People with kidney or liver disease may need to limit it and should follow a doctor’s advice.

The three guidelines in the calculator

  • Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics (formerly the American Dietetic Association): about 0.8 to 1.0 g per kg of body weight for people who are not very active, and 1.0 to 1.8 g per kg for people who exercise regularly.
  • CDC / dietary guidance: protein should be 10% to 35% of daily calories, so the grams depend on how many calories you need.
  • World Health Organization: a safe lower limit of 0.83 g per kg of body weight.

Worked example with the default numbers

A 25-year-old man who weighs 60 kg and is lightly active (exercise 1 to 3 times a week). His energy need is a BMR of 1,605 calories times 1.375 = 2,207 calories a day. The three guidelines give:

Choosing a number in the range

  • Sedentary adults: the lower part of the range is enough.
  • Strength training or endurance training: aim for the middle to upper part of the active range.
  • Losing weight: a higher protein intake helps hold on to muscle while you eat fewer calories.
  • Older adults: many experts suggest the upper half of the basic range, because muscle is harder to maintain with age.

For example, a 96 g day (1.6 g per kg for a 60 kg person) could be eggs and yogurt at breakfast, chicken or lentils at lunch and dinner, and a snack of cottage cheese or nuts.

Quality and variety

A mix of animal and plant sources, such as fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, tofu and nuts, provides a good range of amino acids. Plant-only eaters can meet their needs by combining different plant proteins through the day and by eating enough in total.

When to be careful

  • Kidney or liver disease: ask a doctor before eating more protein.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: needs are higher and have specific advice.
  • Very high intakes: more is not automatically better, and supplements are not required if you eat enough food.
  • Children and teenagers: needs are based on growth and age, so use a paediatric source.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing grams of food with grams of protein. A 100 g steak is not 100 g of protein.
  • Eating most of the day’s protein at one meal.
  • Using the CDC percentage range without knowing your calories.
  • Treating the highest number in the range as a requirement.

How to read your result

Pick the guideline that matches your life. If you want a single target, take the middle of the active range, then adjust for hunger, training and how you feel. For the split of calories between protein, carbohydrate and fat, use the Macro Calculator.

These figures are general guidance for healthy adults, not medical advice. Ask a doctor or dietitian about your own needs.

Common questions

How much protein do I need per day?

A common guide is about 0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight for a sedentary adult, and 1.0 to 1.8 grams for people who exercise regularly. The calculator shows several guidelines so you can choose a sensible range.

Why does the calculator show three different ranges?

Different authorities define needs in different ways: grams per kilogram, a percentage of calories, or a minimum to prevent deficiency. They overlap but are not identical.

Do I need protein powder?

Not if you eat enough protein from food. Powders are convenient, but whole foods supply the same nutrient plus others.

Is a high-protein diet safe?

For most healthy adults, eating towards the upper end of the range is considered safe, but people with kidney disease or certain other conditions should get medical advice first.

Should I eat protein before or after exercise?

Total protein across the day matters most. Spreading it over meals, including one after training, works well, but the exact timing is less important than the daily total.

Does age change protein needs?

Older adults often benefit from the upper half of the range because muscle is harder to maintain with age. Children and teenagers have separate recommendations.
