Voltage Drop Calculator

Voltage drop occurs because conductors have resistance. Longer wire runs and higher current generally increase the drop; wire material and cross-section matter.

By Osama · Last reviewed November 6, 2026

Quick answer. Voltage drop = 2 × current × resistance per length × one-way distance. The default 500 ft run at 1 A and 120 V drops 0.3658 V (0.3048%). A 100 ft run of 12 AWG copper at 15 A drops 4.76 V (3.97%).

What the calculator does

Enter the wire’s resistance, the source voltage, the supply type, the one-way distance and the load current. The calculator returns the voltage drop in volts, the drop as a percentage of the supply and the voltage left at the load. If the drop is above 5%, it adds a warning. You can enter resistance in Ω/km, Ω/m, mΩ/m, Ω/1000 ft, Ω/ft or mΩ/ft, and distance in feet, metres, miles or kilometres.

The formula

Voltage drop = factor × current × resistance per length × one-way distance ÷ parallel sets. The factor is 2 for DC and single-phase AC, because current flows out along one conductor and back along the other, so the wire in the loop is twice the distance. For balanced three-phase AC the factor is √3, about 1.732. Parallel conductor sets per phase share the current, so they divide the resistance: two sets halve the drop.

Worked example with the default numbers

The page opens with 1.2 Ω/km, 120 V, single-phase AC, one conductor set, 500 feet and 1 A. Convert the distance: 500 ft × 0.3048 = 152.4 m, and 1.2 Ω/km is 0.0012 Ω/m. Then 2 × 1 A × 0.0012 Ω/m × 152.4 m = 0.3658 V. As a share of 120 V that is 0.3048%, leaving 119.6342 V at the load. This is far below the 5% warning level, which is why no warning shows. The drop is small because the current is only 1 A.

A realistic example: 12 AWG copper at 15 A

Wire tables give resistance in ohms per 1,000 feet. At 20°C, 12 AWG copper is about 1.588 Ω/1000 ft. On a 100 ft run at 15 A and 120 V single-phase: 2 × 15 × 1.588 × 0.1 = 4.764 V, or 3.97%, leaving 115.24 V. Switch to 14 AWG (2.525 Ω/1000 ft) and the drop becomes 7.575 V, or 6.31%, which trips the 5% warning. With 10 AWG (0.9989 Ω/1000 ft) it falls to 2.997 V, or 2.5%.

How distance and current change the result

Both distance and current scale the drop in a straight line. On 12 AWG copper at 15 A, a 100 ft run drops 3.97%, a 200 ft run 7.94%, and a run of about 76 ft reaches 3% while about 126 ft reaches 5%. A lighter load shortens the effect in the same way.

Supply type and parallel conductors

At the same current, distance and wire, a three-phase circuit drops 86.6% of the single-phase figure, because the factor is √3 rather than 2. DC and single-phase give the same number. Adding a second parallel conductor set per phase halves the resistance: the 12 AWG example drops to 2.382 V instead of 4.764 V with two sets.

A higher supply voltage lowers the percentage, not the volts. The same 12 AWG run at 240 V loses 4.764 V, which is 1.99% of the supply instead of 3.97% at 120 V.

How much drop is acceptable?

A common guide is 3% for a branch circuit and 5% for feeder and branch together. In the US National Electrical Code these figures appear as informational notes, which are recommendations for reasonable efficiency, not hard limits. The calculator’s warning uses 5%. Excessive drop dims lights, slows motors and makes them run hotter, and reduces heater output, so many designers aim for 3% or less on long runs.

What the tool does not check

  • Safe current. It only estimates the drop. Whether the wire can carry the current without overheating is a separate ampacity question.
  • Temperature. Copper resistance rises by roughly 0.4% for each degree Celsius, so use a value for the operating temperature if you need accuracy.
  • Power factor and reactance. The calculation uses resistance only, which suits DC and short or small AC runs. Large AC cables also have reactance.
  • Material. Aluminium has higher resistance than copper for the same size, so enter its own figure.

Common mistakes

  • Entering the round-trip distance. The tool expects one-way distance and already doubles it for the return path.
  • Using a resistance in the wrong unit, such as Ω/1000 ft entered as Ω/km. Check the unit box.
  • Forgetting the parallel set count when wires are doubled up.
  • Treating a result under 5% as proof that the wire is safe.

How to read your result

The large number is the voltage lost along the wire. Below it, the percentage compares that loss with the supply, and the last line gives the voltage the load actually receives. A red note appears when the drop is above 5%. If it does, shorten the run, use thicker wire, add a parallel set or raise the supply voltage.

This is an estimate for planning. Follow your local electrical code and have installation work checked by a qualified electrician.

Common questions

How do I calculate voltage drop?

Multiply the current by the wire resistance per unit length and by the one-way distance, then by 2 for DC or single-phase circuits or by 1.732 for three-phase. For 12 AWG copper at 15 A over 100 feet on 120 V, the drop is about 4.76 volts.

Why is the distance multiplied by two?

Current has to travel out to the load and back again, so the conductor length in the circuit is twice the one-way distance. You enter only the one-way distance and the calculator handles the return.

What voltage drop is acceptable?

A common guide is 3% on a branch circuit and 5% overall. These are recommendations, not hard limits. The calculator warns when the drop is above 5%.

Where do I find my wire's resistance?

Look it up in the manufacturer datasheet or a wire gauge table. Resistance depends on the material, size and temperature. For example, 12 AWG copper is about 1.588 ohms per 1000 feet at 20 degrees Celsius.

How do I reduce voltage drop?

Use thicker wire, shorten the run, lower the current, add parallel conductor sets or use a higher supply voltage.

Does the calculator check that the wire can carry the current?

No. It only estimates the voltage drop. Safe current depends on the wire size, insulation and installation, so check an ampacity table or your local code.
