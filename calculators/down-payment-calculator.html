Quick answer. A down payment is the cash you pay up front; the rest of the price is your loan. For a $500,000 home, 20% down is $100,000 and 3% closing costs add $15,000, so you need about $115,000 in cash and borrow $400,000.

What a down payment is

The down payment is the part of the purchase price you pay in cash. The rest is the loan, so loan = price − down payment. A larger down payment means a smaller loan, a smaller monthly payment and less interest over the years. It also affects whether you need mortgage insurance and sometimes the rate a lender offers.

Closing costs are separate

Closing costs are the fees for getting the loan and transferring the property, such as lender fees, title and appraisal charges, and prepaid items. The CFPB describes the cash you need at closing as your down payment plus closing costs, minus any deposit already paid and any seller credits. The calculator lets you enter closing costs as a percentage or a dollar amount; its default is 3% of the price.

Three ways to use the calculator

  • Find the home price you can afford from the cash you have and the percentage you want to put down.
  • Find the cash you need for a home at a given price and down payment percentage.
  • Find your down payment percentage from a price and the cash you have available.

Worked examples with the default numbers

1. Price from cash. With $100,000 of cash, a 20% down payment and 3% closing costs, the cash must cover 23% of the price, so the price is 100,000 ÷ 0.23 = $434,783. That means a $86,957 down payment, $13,043 of closing costs, a $347,826 loan and a payment of $2,198.50 a month (6.5%, 30 years).

2. Cash from price. A $500,000 home with 20% down and 3% closing costs needs $100,000 down plus $15,000 in closing costs: $115,000 in cash. The loan is $400,000 and the payment is $2,528.27.

3. Percentage from cash. With $100,000 of cash on a $500,000 home, closing costs of $15,000 leave $85,000 for the down payment, which is 17.0%. The loan is $415,000 and the payment $2,623.08. Because the down payment is under 20%, the calculator reminds you that lenders usually require mortgage insurance (PMI).

The 20% target

Putting down at least 20% of the price usually lets you avoid private mortgage insurance (PMI) on a conventional loan. PMI protects the lender, not you, and adds a monthly cost, often a fraction of a percent to more than one percent of the loan a year depending on your credit and down payment. 20% is a useful target, not a requirement. Many programs accept far less: FHA loans allow as little as 3.5% for qualifying borrowers, and some conventional programs allow 3% to 5% with PMI.

Should you put down more than 20%?

  • Benefit: a smaller loan, a lower payment and less interest.
  • Cost: the cash is no longer available for emergencies, repairs, moving costs or investing.
  • Balance: keep an emergency fund after closing. A very large down payment can leave you house-rich and cash-poor.

Common mistakes

  • Saving only for the down payment and forgetting closing costs, moving costs and a reserve.
  • Assuming 20% is mandatory and delaying a purchase longer than necessary.
  • Using up every dollar of savings, which leaves no cushion for repairs.
  • Ignoring PMI when the down payment is under 20%, which makes the real payment higher than the calculator's principal and interest.

How to read your result

Check the cash you need, not just the percentage. Then compare the loan payment with your monthly budget using the Mortgage Calculator, which adds taxes, insurance and PMI. If the payment feels tight, a larger down payment, a cheaper home or a longer saving period may be better than stretching.

This calculator gives estimates and does not include property tax, insurance, PMI or loan-program rules. Your lender's Loan Estimate will show actual figures.

Common questions

Should I put down more than 20% if I can afford to?

Not always. Beyond 20%, extra cash lowers your loan and payment, but it is no longer liquid. Some buyers are better off keeping a larger cash reserve or investing the difference.

Do closing costs vary a lot by location?

Yes. They depend on local taxes, title insurance rates and lender fees, which is why the calculator treats closing costs as an adjustable input instead of a fixed amount.

Can I buy a home with less than 20% down?

Yes. Many loan programs allow smaller down payments, such as FHA loans at 3.5% for qualifying borrowers. They usually come with mortgage insurance or an equivalent cost until you build equity.

What is PMI and when does it go away?

Private mortgage insurance protects the lender when your down payment is under 20% on a conventional loan. On many loans you can ask to cancel it once your balance reaches 80% of the original home value, and it ends automatically at 78%.

What counts toward the down payment?

Cash from savings, and in many cases gifts from family. Closing costs are separate. Lenders have rules about where the money comes from, so ask yours.

Does a bigger down payment get me a lower rate?

It can. A larger down payment lowers the lender's risk, which may qualify you for a better rate or remove mortgage insurance. Compare quotes at several down payment levels.
