Savings Calculator

Estimate how an initial deposit grows when you add money each month or year. Change your deposit amounts, interest assumptions and saving period to compare different plans. The result is a projection, not a guaranteed account balance.

Quick answer. Savings grow from deposits plus interest. $20,000 with $5,000 added each year, rising 3% a year, at 3% interest for 10 years becomes $92,117: $77,319 is money you put in and $14,798 is interest.

How the savings calculator works

The calculator projects how an initial deposit grows when you add money regularly. It has two separate contribution streams: a monthly amount added at the start of each month and an annual amount added at the end of each year, each with its own yearly increase. You also set the interest rate, how often it compounds, the years to save and a tax rate on interest. It then builds the balance month by month.

Why two contribution streams?

Real saving rarely follows one pattern: an automatic monthly transfer plus an occasional lump sum from a bonus or tax refund is common. Letting each stream grow at its own rate (a raise might lift the monthly amount 3% a year, while a bonus may not grow) makes the projection closer to what people actually do.

Worked example with the default numbers

Start with $20,000, add $5,000 at the end of each year increasing 3% a year, and earn 3% compounded annually for 10 years. The balance reaches $92,117. You contributed $77,319 in total (the starting deposit plus $57,319 of yearly deposits), so $14,798 is interest.

Monthly or annual deposits?

The same $5,000 a year goes in either way, but timing matters. Saving $5,000 at the end of each year for 10 years at 3% gives $57,319. Saving it as $416.67 a month (at the start of each month) gives $58,247, about $927 more, because the money starts earning earlier.

Raising contributions as you earn more

A small yearly raise in the monthly amount adds $39,995 over 20 years compared with keeping it flat, with $32,978 more paid in.

Why tax changes the projection

Interest in a taxable account is usually taxed as income each year, even if you do not withdraw it, so only the after-tax interest compounds. Enter your tax rate to see the real growth. Tax-advantaged accounts such as a 401(k), IRA or HSA do not tax interest as it is earned, so leave the rate at 0% for those.

Does compounding frequency matter?

Only a little at savings-account rates. With the default plan, annual compounding ends at $92,117 and daily compounding at $92,365, a difference of $248 over ten years. The interest rate, the amount you save and the number of years matter far more than how often interest is credited. Compare accounts by APY, which already includes compounding, so two offers can be judged on one number.

Saving toward a goal: an emergency fund

If you start with $2,000 and add $500 a month at 4% interest, you reach an $18,000 emergency fund (six months of $3,000 spending) in about 31 months (2.6 years). Use the Investment Calculator to solve backwards for the monthly amount needed by a deadline.

Where to keep savings

  • Savings accounts and CDs at banks insured by the FDIC are covered up to at least $250,000 per bank.
  • Money for short-term goals belongs in low-risk accounts; money for long-term goals can take more risk but can lose value.
  • Compare rates by APY, and check fees and minimum balances.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the current interest rate will last. Savings rates change.
  • Ignoring tax and inflation.
  • Entering a monthly amount in the annual box, or both streams for the same money.
  • Letting savings sit idle in an account that pays almost nothing.

How to read your result

Compare the ending balance with what you paid in to see how much growth you are getting, then try small changes: a higher monthly amount, a yearly increase, a better rate. The schedule under the result shows the balance year by year. For a view of compounding with different frequencies, see the Compounding Frequency Converter.

Common questions

Why does compounding frequency matter if I also contribute monthly?

They are independent settings. Compounding frequency controls how often interest is calculated and added, and your contribution schedule controls when new money goes in. The calculator combines both.

What does the contribution increase do?

It raises the amount each year by the percentage you enter, for example a 3% raise in your monthly saving. Set it to 0 to keep contributions flat.

Should I enter a tax rate?

Yes for a taxable savings account or brokerage account, because interest is usually taxed each year. Leave it at 0% for tax-advantaged accounts such as a 401(k), IRA or HSA.

When are the deposits made?

Monthly deposits go in at the start of each month and annual deposits at the end of each year, which the calculator uses to build the schedule.

How accurate is the projection?

It assumes a steady interest rate. Actual savings rates and investment returns change, so use the result as a planning estimate.

Are my savings insured?

Deposits at FDIC-insured banks are insured up to at least $250,000 per bank. Investments are not insured against loss. Check that your institution is covered.
