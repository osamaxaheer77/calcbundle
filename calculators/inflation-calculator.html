Value over time

Quick answer. Inflation is the rise in prices over time. The calculator multiplies your amount by the price index of the later date and divides by the index of the earlier date. For example, $100 in January 2000 has the same buying power as about $198 in August 2026.

How this inflation calculator works

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time, so each dollar buys less. To compare money from different dates the calculator uses the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) published monthly by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. The index covers every month from January 1913 and the latest data point in this calculator is August 2026.

The formula is simple: value now = amount × CPI of the later date ÷ CPI of the earlier date. If the index rose 26% between two dates, $100 in the earlier month equals $126 in the later one. For gaps over a year the calculator also shows the average yearly rate, which is the steady rate that would give the same total change.

  • CPI mode: pick an amount and two dates (a month, or the average for a whole year).
  • Forward mode: apply one steady inflation rate to see what a price could become in the future.
  • Backward mode: use one steady rate to see what today's amount was worth in the past.

Worked example: $100 from January 2000

The index was 168.8 in January 2000 and 334.98 in August 2026. So $100 × 334.98 ÷ 168.8 = $198.45. Prices rose 98.4% in total over about 26.6 years, an average of 2.61% a year. Put the other way round, the $100 from 2000 buys what about $50 buys today.

A much longer span shows the same effect. One dollar in January 1913 matches $34.18 in August 2026.

Steady-rate projections and the rule of 72

The flat-rate modes are for planning. At a steady 3% a year, $100 of today's spending needs $134.39 after 10 years, $180.61 after 20 and $242.73 after 30. A quick mental check is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the inflation rate to estimate how many years it takes prices to double. At 3% that is about 24 years.

Why inflation matters for savings and income

A saving that grows by less than inflation loses buying power. Your real return is roughly the interest rate minus inflation; more exactly it is (1 + rate) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. For example, 5% interest with 3% inflation gives a real return of about 1.94%, not 5%. The same logic applies to salaries: a 3% raise in a year with 3% inflation leaves your buying power unchanged.

What the index does not capture

  • The CPI measures the average basket of urban households. Your own costs may rise faster or slower, for example if you spend more on housing, healthcare or tuition.
  • It does not follow one product. A television can get cheaper while rent rises.
  • Results for dates after the latest published month are not available, and a month with no official figure is estimated (see the FAQs).

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a month with a yearly average. Use "Average" for both dates or neither.
  • Forgetting that the result is in U.S. dollars; other countries have their own indexes.
  • Treating the average rate as the rate of any single year. It is a smoothed figure.
  • Using a past inflation rate as a guarantee for the future.

How to read your result

The large number is what your amount is worth in the other date. The total change shows how much prices moved in between, and the average per year makes different time spans comparable. Use the chart to see whether the loss was steady or came in bursts. For long-term plans, use a flat rate near 2% to 3% for a base case and test a higher one for safety.

The calculator uses published price data and a simple formula. It describes general buying power, not your personal cost of living or any financial advice.

Common questions

What is the Consumer Price Index?

The CPI tracks the average price of a basket of goods and services that urban consumers buy, such as food, housing, transport and medical care. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes it every month, and the change in the index over time is the inflation rate.

How is the value of money over time worked out?

Multiply the amount by the index of the later date and divide by the index of the earlier date. If the index rose by 26%, then $100 in the earlier month needs $126 in the later month to buy the same things.

What does the Average option do?

Choosing Average uses the average index for the whole year instead of a single month. It is useful when you only know the year, such as a salary or price from 1995. The calculator treats a yearly average as the middle of the year when it works out the average yearly rate.

Why does October 2025 use an estimated value?

No official price index was published for October 2025. The calculator fills that month with the midpoint of September and November, so a result that uses October 2025 is an estimate.

What inflation rate should I use for planning?

Inflation in developed countries has often been near 2% to 3% a year, so 3% is a common planning assumption. Because the future is uncertain, test a higher rate as well and see how much your plan changes.

Is inflation the same as the prices of things I buy?

No. Inflation is an average across many items. Your own rate depends on what you spend money on, so it can be higher or lower than the published figure.
