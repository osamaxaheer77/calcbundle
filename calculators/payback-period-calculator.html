Quick answer. Payback period is the time an investment takes to recover its cost. $100,000 returning $30,000 in year 1, growing 5% a year, pays back in 3.16 years, or 3.92 years after discounting at 10%.

What payback period tells you (and what it does not)

Payback period answers a practical question: how long until an investment returns the money put into it? It is a liquidity and risk measure more than a profitability measure. A shorter payback means your capital is tied up for less time and exposed to fewer things that could go wrong. For equal yearly cash flows it is simply initial investment ÷ yearly cash flow: $10,000 earning $2,500 a year pays back in 4 years. With uneven flows, the calculator adds them up year by year and finds the point where the total turns positive, interpolating within the year.

Tool 1: a growing or shrinking cash flow

Enter the initial investment, the first-year cash flow, how much it grows or shrinks each year, the number of years and a discount rate. With the defaults of $100,000, $30,000 in year 1 growing 5% a year for 5 years and a 10% discount rate, the cash flows are $30,000, $31,500, $33,075, $34,729 and $36,465. After year 3 you have recovered $-5,425, leaving $105,425, and year 4 brings in $34,729, so the payback period is 3.156 years. The discounted payback period is 3.921 years, and the cash flow return rate (IRR) is 18.86% a year.

Why the discounted version matters

Simple payback treats a dollar received in year 5 the same as a dollar today, which overstates how quickly an investment pays off. Discounted payback shrinks each future cash flow to its present value first, so it always takes at least as long as simple payback, usually longer. If the cumulative discounted cash flow never turns positive within the years you enter, the calculator reports Never.

Tool 2: irregular cash flows

Real projects rarely pay the same each year. The second tool lets you type up to ten years of cash flows. The defaults are an initial $100,000 and flows of $5,000, $25,000, $35,000, $40,000, $30,000 and $10,000, with a 10% discount rate. The payback period is 3.875 years, the discounted payback period is 5.452 years and the IRR is 10.96%. A year left blank ends the list.

Payback is not the whole picture

Payback says nothing about what happens after the money is recovered. Compare two projects that each cost $100,000. Project A returns $50,000 a year for 2 years and then stops. Project B returns $25,000 a year for 10 years. A pays back in 2.0 years and B in 4.0 years, so payback favours A. But A ends with a total gain of $0 and an IRR of 0%, while B ends with a gain of $150,000 and an IRR of 21.4%. Pair payback with IRR or NPV for the fuller picture.

The cash flow return rate

The calculator also reports the internal rate of return of the initial investment against the full series of cash flows you entered: the yearly rate at which the present value of the inflows equals the outlay. It is independent of the payback figures and uses only the years you entered. Use the IRR Calculator for the same measure with more detail.

When payback is useful

  • Quick screening of projects when cash is tight.
  • Comparing risky projects where a fast return matters most.
  • Checking a home improvement or equipment purchase, such as solar panels or a new machine.
  • Setting a minimum acceptable payback for a team.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring cash flows after the payback point.
  • Using simple payback for long projects where the time value of money matters.
  • Forgetting maintenance or running costs. Enter net cash flows.
  • Setting the discount rate to zero and assuming the answer is discounted.

How to read your result

The large number is the simple payback period. Below it are the discounted payback and the IRR, with a year-by-year table of cash flow, cumulative cash flow, discounted cash flow and cumulative discounted cash flow. A payback shorter than your limit is a good sign, but check the IRR and what happens after.

Common questions

What does "Cash Flow Return Rate" mean here?

It is the internal rate of return (IRR) of the initial investment against the full series of cash flows you entered, an annualized return independent of the payback figures.

What if the investment never pays back?

If the cumulative cash flow never turns positive within the years provided, the calculator reports "Never" for that payback figure instead of a misleading number.

What is the difference between payback and discounted payback?

Simple payback adds the cash flows as they are. Discounted payback first converts each cash flow to its present value at your discount rate, so it is always as long or longer.

What is a good payback period?

It depends on the project and your risk tolerance. Shorter is safer, but a short payback is not enough; check the return after the payback point too.

How does the calculator handle part of a year?

It finds the year when the cumulative cash flow turns positive and interpolates, assuming the cash flow arrives evenly through that year.

Should I use payback or IRR?

Use both. Payback shows how quickly you recover your money; IRR shows the return over the whole life of the investment.
