Quick answer. DTI is monthly debt divided by gross monthly income. On $60,000 a year with $1,200 rent, $200 of cards and a $250 car loan, the back-end ratio is 33% and the front-end ratio is 24%, which is under 36%.

What debt-to-income means

Your debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, compares what you owe each month with what you earn before tax. DTI = monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income. Lenders use it to judge whether you can take on a new loan. The calculator gives two versions: the front-end ratio, which counts housing only, and the back-end ratio, which adds every other recurring debt.

Worked example with the default numbers

The page opens with a salary of $60,000 a year, which is $5,000 a month. The debts are rent of $1,200, credit cards of $200 and an auto loan of $250 a month. Housing is $1,200, so the front-end ratio is 1,200 ÷ 5,000 = 24%. Other debts are $450, so total debts are $1,650 and the back-end ratio is 1,650 ÷ 5,000 = 33%. That is under 36%, so the page says “Your DTI ratio is good.” A simple baseline: $900 of debt on $4,500 of income is 20%.

Enter each amount per month or per year, and the tool converts it to a monthly figure. Costs that are not debts, such as groceries, utilities, phone and health insurance, are not included. Always use income before tax, because lenders do.

The three tiers

The result is placed in one of three bands. Below 36% is good. 36% to just under 50% is the mid-range: workable, but with less room to qualify for the best rates. 50% or more is high and may limit what you can borrow. The percentages are rounded to a whole number on screen, so 35.6% is shown as 36% but is still treated as under 36%.

Changing the picture

Each $100 a month of debt you remove lowers the ratio by 0 points on a $5,000 income. Paying off the $250 car loan moves the default from 33% to 28%. Adding a $300 card payment pushes it up to 39%. Raising the salary to $75,000 with the same debts gives 26%.

An owner with several debts

Take someone earning $84,000 a year ($7,000 a month). Their housing costs are a $1,800 mortgage payment, $250 a month of property tax, $100 of insurance and $50 of HOA fees, a total of $2,200. They also pay $300 on credit cards, $400 on student loans and $450 on a car, which is $1,150. Total debt is $3,350 a month, so the back-end ratio is 48% and the front-end ratio is 31%. That is in the mid-range, and the tool says they would likely not qualify for a conventional mortgage, which usually caps the back-end ratio at 36%.

How much house the default income supports

When the back-end ratio is 36% or lower, the tool also estimates how much you could spend on a new home. It takes 36% of income, here $1,800, and subtracts other debts of $450, leaving $1,350 a month for housing. Assuming a 30-year loan at 7.047%, 20% down and about 2% of the price a year for property tax and insurance, that supports a house of about $192,462. The assumptions are fixed, so treat it as a rough guide.

Reading the lender guides

A common rule of thumb is that housing should be no more than about 28% of gross income and total debt no more than 36%. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s tools suggest keeping the ratio for all debts at 36% or less, and some lenders accept higher figures. Different loan types set their own limits, and lenders also look at your credit score, savings and the size of the down payment.

Ways to lower your DTI

  • Pay off a loan completely, which removes its whole monthly payment, as with the car loan above.
  • Pay down credit card balances so the minimum payment falls.
  • Raise your income, or add income that a lender will count.
  • Avoid new debt in the months before you apply for a mortgage.
  • Refinance or extend a loan to lower the payment, though that can raise the total interest.

Common mistakes

  • Using take-home pay instead of gross pay.
  • Leaving out minimum card payments or a car loan.
  • Counting utilities or groceries, which are not debt payments.
  • Mixing monthly and yearly amounts. Check the drop-down beside each box.
  • Treating a low DTI as a promise of approval. Lenders look at much more.

How to read your result

The large number is the back-end ratio. The rows below give the front-end ratio, total monthly income and total monthly debt. The bar shows how your income is split between housing, other debts and what remains. The note underneath either estimates a home budget or explains why a conventional loan may be out of reach.

DTI is a ratio, not a measure of the cash left after expenses. This tool is for planning. A lender’s own rules decide what counts.

Common questions

What's a good DTI ratio to aim for?

Generally, under 36% back-end is considered healthy by conventional lending standards. Between 36% and 50% is workable but leaves less room to qualify for the best rates, and 50% or more starts to limit borrowing options.

Does DTI affect my credit score?

Not directly. DTI is not a factor in credit scoring models. It is a separate figure that lenders calculate themselves when deciding whether to approve a loan and on what terms.

What is the difference between front-end and back-end DTI?

The front-end ratio counts only housing costs: rent or mortgage, property tax, HOA fees and homeowner insurance. The back-end ratio adds all other recurring debts such as cards, student loans and car loans.

Do I use gross or net income?

Gross, which is income before tax. Lenders measure DTI against gross income.

What debts are not included?

Everyday costs such as groceries, utilities, phone and health insurance are not debts, so they are left out.

How can I lower my DTI?

Pay off loans, reduce card balances, increase your income and avoid new debt. Each $100 of monthly debt removed lowers the ratio by 100 divided by your monthly income, for example 2 points on $5,000.
