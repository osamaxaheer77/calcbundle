How Much Do You Need to Retire? The 4% Rule Explained

A simple way to estimate your retirement target, what the 4% rule assumes, and how to adjust it for your own situation.

By Osama · Published October 13, 2026 · 9 min read

"How much do I need to retire?" has no single right answer, but there is a simple starting point that most planners use: work out what you will spend each year, multiply by 25, and treat the result as your savings target. This guide explains where that shortcut, the 4% rule, comes from, what it assumes, and how to adjust it for Social Security, taxes and your own timeline.

The short version

  • The 4% rule says you can withdraw 4% of your savings in the first year of retirement, then raise that amount with inflation each year, and expect the money to last about 30 years.
  • It works backwards to a target: yearly spending × 25.
  • It is based on historical US results and is a starting point, not a guarantee. Lower returns, a longer retirement or a bad early market can break it.
  • Social Security, a pension or part-time work lower the amount your savings must cover, which can cut the target sharply.

Where the 4% rule comes from

The rule grew out of work by financial planner William Bengen in 1994, and a 1998 study by three Trinity University professors (Cooley, Hubbard and Walz) known as the Trinity Study. They tested historical US stock and bond returns to see which first-year withdrawal rates would have survived 30 years of retirement. A 4% starting rate, with later withdrawals rising each year for inflation, survived most historical periods for a portfolio of mostly stocks and bonds. The authors presented it as a starting point, and the study left out taxes and fees.

The 25× shortcut

If 4% of your savings is your first year's income, your savings must be 1 ÷ 0.04 = 25 times your yearly spending.

Savings target = yearly spending ÷ withdrawal rateAt 4%, that is yearly spending × 25. Example: $50,000 ÷ 0.04 = $1,250,000.

The input that matters most is spending, not income. Add up what you actually spend, then adjust for costs that will change in retirement (a paid-off mortgage, no commuting, higher healthcare).

The withdrawal rate is a choice

Four percent is a convention. A cautious planner might choose 3% to 3.5%, especially for a retirement that starts early and may last 40 years or more. Others accept a higher rate if their spending can flex in bad years. Each choice changes the target a lot.

Why returns and timing matter

The 4% rule was tested against real market history, with its good and bad years. Steady returns hide the real risk, which is a market fall early in retirement while you are also withdrawing money. The chart uses simple steady returns just to show how sensitive the outcome is: with the same $1.25 million and the same withdrawals, the result depends heavily on the return.

This is why many planners suggest flexible spending, holding some cash or safer assets for the first years, or reviewing the withdrawal each year.

Social Security and other income

You only need to fund the gap between what you spend and what other sources pay. Suppose you want $50,000 a year and Social Security pays $1,800 a month ($21,600 a year, a hypothetical figure). Your savings need to cover $28,400, so the target becomes $710,000 instead of $1,250,000.

When you claim matters too. The Social Security Administration reduces benefits if you claim before full retirement age (67 for anyone born in 1960 or later) and raises them if you wait until 70.

Once you know the target, the monthly saving depends on how long you have and the return you can expect. The chart assumes 7% a year, compounded monthly, as an illustration only.

What the rule leaves out

  • Taxes. Withdrawals from pre-tax accounts are taxed, so you need more than your spending. Roth withdrawals generally are not taxed.
  • Fees. Investment fees reduce what you can withdraw safely.
  • Healthcare and long-term care. These costs often rise with age and can be uneven.
  • Inflation. The rule adjusts withdrawals each year, so higher inflation than assumed raises the amount needed.
  • A longer or shorter retirement. Retiring at 50 is a 40-year plan, retiring at 70 may be a shorter one.

Common questions

Is the 4% rule still valid?

It is a reasonable historical starting point for a 30-year retirement, but it is not a guarantee. Some researchers suggest slightly lower starting rates today, while others suggest flexible spending can support higher rates. Treat it as a guide and review it every year.

How much do I need to retire at 60 or 65?

Work out your yearly spending, subtract income such as Social Security or a pension, and multiply what is left by 25. Retiring earlier means a longer retirement, which usually calls for a lower withdrawal rate and a larger target.

Does the 4% rule include taxes?

No. The original studies did not include taxes or fees. If you will withdraw from pre-tax accounts, plan to withdraw enough to cover the tax on those withdrawals as well.

Should I count my home as part of my retirement savings?

Usually not, since you need somewhere to live. A paid-off home lowers your spending, which lowers the target, but the value of the home is not income you can withdraw unless you sell or borrow against it.

What if I am behind on saving?

Options include saving more, working longer, delaying Social Security, lowering planned spending, or planning some part-time income. Even a few extra years of work can both add savings and shorten the time the money must last.
