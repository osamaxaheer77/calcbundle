Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA vs 401(k): 2026 Guide

How the three main US retirement accounts differ, with 2026 contribution limits and a worked example of the tax trade-off.

By Osama · Published October 13, 2026 · 10 min read

Choosing where to save for retirement can feel harder than saving itself. The three most common choices in the United States are a traditional IRA, a Roth IRA and a workplace 401(k). They all grow tax-deferred or tax-free, but they differ in when you pay tax, how much you can contribute and who can use them. This guide explains the differences with the 2026 limits and a worked comparison.

The short version

  • The main difference is when you pay tax: traditional accounts give a deduction now and tax you on withdrawal, while Roth accounts tax you now and let qualified withdrawals come out tax-free.
  • If your tax rate in retirement equals your rate today, the two give the same result. The better choice depends on whether you expect your rate to be higher or lower later.
  • A 401(k) allows far larger contributions than an IRA, and employer matching money is usually the best deal available.
  • You can use more than one account. Many people hold a mix.

The three accounts in plain terms

  • Traditional IRA: you may deduct contributions (subject to income limits if you or a spouse has a workplace plan). Money grows without yearly tax, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
  • Roth IRA: you contribute money you have already paid tax on. Growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals (after age 59½ and once the account has been open for five years) are tax-free too.
  • 401(k): a retirement plan offered by an employer. Contributions normally come out of your pay before income tax. Many employers add matching money. Some plans also offer a Roth 401(k), which works like a Roth IRA but with the higher 401(k) limits.

How much can you contribute in 2026?

The IRS sets yearly limits. For 2026 the 401(k) limit is $24,500 and the IRA limit is $7,500. People aged 50 or older can add catch-up contributions. The IRA limit applies to all your IRAs together, so you can split it between Roth and traditional but not exceed it.

Who can use a Roth IRA or take the deduction?

Anyone with earned income can contribute to a traditional IRA, but the tax deduction shrinks and then disappears at higher incomes if you are covered by a workplace plan. A Roth IRA has its own income limit for direct contributions. Both are based on your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI).

If your income is above the Roth limit, some people use a "backdoor Roth" conversion. The rules are technical, so ask a tax professional before trying it.

Traditional or Roth? A worked example

Suppose you are in the 22% tax bracket and can put aside the equivalent of $7,500 of pre-tax pay. With a traditional account you invest $7,500. With a Roth you first pay 22% tax and invest what is left, $5,850. Both cost you the same take-home pay. After 30 years at 7%, the traditional account holds $57,092, and the Roth holds $44,532.

One more point favours Roth: the limits are in dollars, so a Roth shelters more after-tax value than the same limit in a traditional account.

Do not skip the employer match

Employer matching is part of your pay. For example, say you earn $75,000 and your employer matches 50% of the first 3% you contribute. Contributing 3% costs you $2,250 a year and your employer adds $1,125, an instant 50% gain before any investment return.

Rules to know before you withdraw

  • Early withdrawals before age 59½ from traditional accounts are normally taxed and may add a 10% penalty, with some exceptions.
  • Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, which gives the account some flexibility.
  • Required minimum distributions force withdrawals from traditional accounts and most 401(k)s starting at age 73, or 75 for those born in 1960 or later. A Roth IRA has none for the original owner.
  • Rollovers: when you leave a job you can usually move a 401(k) into an IRA without tax if done correctly.

Common questions

Can I contribute to both a 401(k) and an IRA in the same year?

Yes. The limits are separate, so you can contribute to a 401(k) at work and to an IRA. If you or your spouse is covered by a workplace plan, the traditional IRA deduction may be reduced or removed at higher incomes.

Is a Roth IRA or traditional IRA better for young people?

Many people early in their careers choose Roth because their tax rate is often lower now than it will be later. It is not guaranteed, so some split contributions between the two.

What happens to a 401(k) when I change jobs?

You can usually leave it in the old plan, roll it into your new employer’s plan, or roll it into an IRA. A direct rollover avoids tax and penalties. Cashing it out normally triggers both.

What is the five-year rule for Roth IRAs?

To withdraw earnings tax-free, your first Roth IRA must have been open for at least five years and you must also be 59½ or meet another qualifying condition. The five years start on January 1 of the tax year of your first contribution.

Are the limits the same every year?

No. The IRS adjusts them for inflation, usually announcing the next year’s numbers in the autumn. The figures in this guide are for 2026.
