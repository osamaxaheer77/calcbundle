How Many Calories Do You Need? BMR, TDEE and Deficits

A step-by-step way to estimate the calories you burn, and how to turn that into a realistic plan.

By Osama · Published October 13, 2026 · 9 min read

Your body burns calories all day, even while you sleep. How many you need depends on your size, age, sex and how much you move. Once you can estimate that number, called your maintenance calories, you can plan to hold your weight, lose some or gain some. This guide shows how the estimate is made, with a worked example, and how to use it sensibly.

The short version

  • BMR is what you burn at rest. TDEE is BMR plus everything else you do, estimated by multiplying BMR by an activity factor.
  • A common weight-change rule is about 3,500 calories per pound, so a 500 calorie daily deficit is roughly a pound a week at first.
  • Every figure here is an estimate that can easily be several hundred calories off. Treat it as a starting point and adjust from real results.
  • Very low calorie targets can be unsafe. Large changes should be discussed with a doctor or dietitian.

Step 1: Your basal metabolic rate (BMR)

BMR is the energy your body needs to stay alive at rest: breathing, circulation, temperature control and cell repair. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990 and widely used by dietitians, estimates it from weight, height and age:

Men: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5Women: the same, but subtract 161 instead of adding 5.

Take a 25-year-old man who is 5 ft 10 in (177.8 cm) and weighs 165 lb (74.8 kg).

  1. Weight term: 10 × 74.8 = 748.
  2. Height term: 6.25 × 177.8 = 1111.
  3. Age term: 5 × 25 = 125, which is subtracted.
  4. Add 5 for a man: 748 + 1111 − 125 + 5 = 1,740 calories a day.

A woman with the same height, weight and age works out to about 1,574 calories, because the equation subtracts 161 instead of adding 5, a difference of 166.

Step 2: Add your activity (TDEE)

Your total daily energy expenditure, or TDEE, is BMR multiplied by an activity factor. Calculators vary slightly in the exact multipliers. These are the ones used on this site:

Step 3: Choose a goal

Eating at your TDEE roughly holds your weight steady. To lose weight you eat less than you burn, and to gain you eat more. A pound of body fat stores roughly 3,500 calories of energy, so a daily change of 500 calories adds up to about a pound a week. The example man at a moderate activity level needs about 2,549 calories to maintain, so a 500 calorie deficit means about 2,049.

Why real weight loss slows down

The 3,500 calorie rule assumes your burn stays the same while you lose weight, but it does not. A lighter body needs less energy, so the gap between what you eat and what you burn narrows over time. How low is too low?

Bigger deficits lose weight faster on paper, but they also increase the chance of muscle loss, tiredness and nutrient gaps. Commonly cited guidance is not to go below about 1,200 calories a day for women or 1,500 for men unless a doctor or dietitian is supervising, and many people need more than that. Gradual loss of around one to two pounds a week at most is the usual recommendation, and less is perfectly reasonable.

Not for everyone. Calorie targets are not appropriate during pregnancy, for children and teenagers who are still growing, or for anyone with a history of disordered eating, without guidance from a healthcare professional.

Getting more out of the number

  • Track for two to three weeks, then compare your actual weight trend with the estimate and adjust by 100 to 200 calories if it is off.
  • Weigh yourself under the same conditions, and look at weekly averages, because day-to-day water changes can hide real progress.
  • Protein and strength training help preserve muscle while you lose fat.
  • Small steps count: walking more raises the activity side of the equation without requiring harder diets.
  • Recalculate when your weight, age or routine changes by a meaningful amount.

Common questions

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE?

BMR is the energy your body uses at complete rest. TDEE adds the energy used by movement, exercise and digesting food, so it is always higher than BMR. TDEE is the number used for planning how much to eat.

Which BMR formula is the most accurate?

Mifflin-St Jeor is generally regarded as one of the most reliable equations for the general population. The Katch-McArdle equation can be better if you know your lean body mass accurately. All of them are estimates.

Is 3,500 calories really a pound of fat?

It is a rule of thumb based on the energy stored in a pound of body fat. It works reasonably well at the start of a diet, but weight loss usually slows as your body adapts, so the straight-line prediction overstates later results.

Do I need to eat back the calories I burn exercising?

Not if you used an activity multiplier, since your exercise is already built into the TDEE. Adding exercise calories on top counts it twice.

Why do two calculators give different numbers?

They may use different BMR equations, activity multipliers or rounding. A gap of a few hundred calories between tools is common, which is another reason to adjust from your actual results.
