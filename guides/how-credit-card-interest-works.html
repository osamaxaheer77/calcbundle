How Credit Card Interest Works and How to Pay It Off Faster

How interest is worked out each day, why the minimum payment is a trap, and the quickest ways out of card debt.

By Osama · Published October 13, 2026 · 8 min read

A credit card is the most convenient loan most people have, and one of the most expensive. The interest rate is high, it is charged every day, and the minimum payment is set so that the balance falls slowly. Understanding exactly how the charge is worked out makes it much easier to decide how much to pay and when.

The short version

  • Interest is usually worked out daily, from your average daily balance, and then added to the balance.
  • If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, you normally pay no interest on purchases, thanks to the grace period.
  • Paying only the minimum keeps you in debt for many years and can cost more in interest than you originally borrowed.
  • A fixed payment, even a modest one, beats the minimum by a large margin, and a lower rate helps even more.

APR, the daily rate and the average daily balance

The APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly price of borrowing. Issuers convert it to a daily periodic rate by dividing by 365. At 24% APR the daily rate is 0.0658%.

Each day, the card company records your balance. At the end of the billing cycle it adds those daily balances and divides by the number of days to get the average daily balance. The interest for the cycle is the daily rate multiplied by the average daily balance and the days in the cycle. Some issuers compound daily, so unpaid interest also starts earning interest immediately. Your cardholder agreement says which method applies.

Interest = average daily balance × (APR ÷ 365) × days in cycleExample: $5,000 × (0.24 ÷ 365) × 30 days = $98.63

A rough shortcut is to divide the APR by 12 for a monthly rate. At 24% that is 2% a month, so a $5,000 balance adds about $100 in interest each month.

The grace period

Most cards give a grace period: if you pay the whole statement balance by the due date, purchases from that cycle carry no interest. This only works if you pay in full. Once you carry a balance, the grace period is typically lost, and new purchases start collecting interest from the day you make them until you clear everything. Cash advances and balance transfers generally have no grace period at all.

Why minimum payments keep you in debt

Take a $5,000 balance at 24% APR. Assume the minimum is the interest plus 1% of the balance, with a floor of $15, which is a common formula. The first minimum payment is $150.00.

Because the minimum shrinks as the balance shrinks, the balance falls more and more slowly. Compare that with fixed payments of $150 and $250 a month:

How much the rate matters

The rate drives how long a given payment takes. At $200 a month, the same $5,000 balance takes 25 months at 0% and 40 months at 30%.

Ways to pay it off faster

  • Pay a fixed amount, not the minimum. Choose a payment you can afford and keep it the same even when the minimum falls.
  • Avalanche or snowball. With several cards, paying extra on the highest-rate card first (avalanche) saves the most interest. Paying the smallest balance first (snowball) costs somewhat more but can be easier to stick with. Our Debt Payoff Calculator compares both.
  • Balance transfer. A card with a low or 0% introductory rate can save a lot, but there is usually a fee of about 3% to 5% of the amount moved, the rate rises when the offer ends, and new purchases may not get a grace period.
  • Consolidation loan. A fixed-rate personal loan at a lower rate than your cards turns revolving debt into a fixed schedule, so long as you stop adding to the cards.
  • Call your issuer. It costs nothing to ask for a lower rate or a hardship plan.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Paying only the minimum for months because the bill "looks manageable".
  • Making new purchases on a card with a balance and assuming they are interest-free.
  • Using cash advances, which usually carry higher rates, fees and no grace period.
  • Missing a payment, which can add a late fee and may raise your rate.

Common questions

How is credit card interest calculated?

Most issuers multiply your average daily balance by a daily periodic rate (the APR divided by 365) and by the number of days in the billing cycle. Some compound the interest daily. Your card agreement describes the exact method.

Do I pay interest if I pay my balance in full?

Not on purchases, as long as you pay the full statement balance by the due date and did not carry a balance from the month before. Cash advances usually accrue interest immediately.

What is a good APR for a credit card?

Rates vary widely with credit score and the economy. A lower APR is always better, and many cards charge well above 20%. Compare offers on your own credit profile and look for any annual fee.

Is it better to pay off the highest-rate card first?

Mathematically yes, because it saves the most interest. If you need quick wins to stay motivated, paying the smallest balance first can work, and it costs a little more.

Does carrying a balance help my credit score?

No. That is a common myth. Paying on time helps, but carrying a balance only costs you interest. A lower credit utilisation, meaning a smaller balance compared with your limit, is generally better for your score.
