How Inflation Erodes Your Money, With Charts and Examples

How inflation is measured, what it does to savings over decades, and how to judge a return after inflation.

By Osama · Published October 13, 2026 · 8 min read

Inflation is the slow rise in prices that makes each dollar buy a little less every year. It is easy to ignore because it happens gradually, but over a working life it changes how much your savings are really worth. This guide explains how inflation is measured, what it did to a dollar over the last few decades, and how to judge returns after inflation.

The short version

  • Inflation is measured by the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks the price of a typical basket of goods and services.
  • A few percent a year sounds small, but at 3% prices double in about 24 years.
  • What matters for saving is the real return: your return minus inflation. A savings rate below inflation loses buying power even though the balance grows.
  • Your own inflation rate depends on what you spend money on, and can differ from the headline number.

How inflation is measured

In the United States the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the CPI each month. It follows the prices of more than 200 categories of goods and services, from food and rent to medical care and transport, weighted by how much households actually spend on each. The most-quoted version is the CPI-U, which covers urban consumers. The index is set to 100 in a base period (1982–84), so a value of 321.9 means the same basket costs about 3.2 times what it did then.

The inflation rate is the percentage change in the index. To convert an amount between two dates, multiply it by the ratio of the two index values:

Value later = value earlier × (CPI later ÷ CPI earlier)Example: $100 in 1996 × (321.9 ÷ 156.9) = $205.19 in 2025

Inflation does not move in a straight line

Over 2000 to 2025 US inflation averaged about 2.6% a year, but individual years varied a lot, from -0.4% in 2009 to 8.0% in 2022.

The long-run effect: buying power

The effect that matters for retirement is cumulative. At 3% inflation, $100 of buying power shrinks to about $55 after 20 years and about $41 after 30. The rule of 72 gives a quick estimate: prices double in roughly 72 ÷ inflation rate years, which is about 24 years at 3% and 36 years at 2%.

Real return: what your savings actually earn

A savings account that pays 4% sounds good, but if prices rise 3% you are really only ahead by about one percent. The real return is the nominal return adjusted for inflation:

Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1Example: (1.04 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = 0.97%

This is why cash held for decades tends to lose value in real terms, and why long-term savings are usually invested in assets that have historically beaten inflation over time, even though those assets rise and fall in the short run. See the compound interest guide for how that growth builds up.

Your personal inflation rate

The CPI describes an average household. If more of your budget goes to rent, healthcare or college than the basket assumes, your own inflation can run higher. If you spend less on those, it can run lower. Look at your own largest categories when you plan.

What you can do about it

  • Plan in real dollars. When setting a retirement or savings goal, ask what the amount will buy, not just its size. The retirement guide adjusts withdrawals for inflation.
  • Keep only needed cash in low-interest accounts. Emergency money should be safe and easy to reach, and the rest can work harder.
  • Look at the real rate before locking in. A fixed rate that is below inflation is a loss in buying power. Inflation-linked bonds exist for this reason.
  • Ask for raises that keep up. If pay rises 2% in a year with 3% inflation, that is a pay cut in real terms.
  • Remember debt. Inflation helps borrowers at a fixed rate, because the debt is repaid in cheaper dollars.

Common questions

What is a normal level of inflation?

Many central banks aim for about 2% a year. The US Federal Reserve's stated long-run goal is 2%. Actual inflation moves above and below that target from year to year.

How do I calculate inflation between two years?

Divide the later CPI by the earlier CPI and subtract one. For example, if the index rises from 156.9 to 321.9, prices rose by about 105%. Multiply any earlier amount by the same ratio to find its value in later dollars.

What is the difference between nominal and real return?

Nominal return is the headline percentage your money earns. Real return subtracts inflation, so it shows the change in what your money can buy.

Does inflation affect my debt?

It can help a borrower with a fixed-rate loan, because later payments are made with money that is worth less. It hurts lenders and anyone holding cash. Variable-rate debt usually rises with inflation because interest rates tend to rise too.

Is the CPI accurate for my own costs?

It measures an average basket. Your own rate depends on what you spend on, such as housing, healthcare, food and transport, so it can be higher or lower than the headline figure.
